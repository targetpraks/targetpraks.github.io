GeoWind GW-series · Cape Town feasibility

The icosahedron turbine,
measured against real Cape Town wind.

The reel is GeoWind — a Korean icosahedron-frame vertical-axis turbine, CES 2026 Innovation Award, patented, with a geodesic sail frame that catches wind from any direction from about 4 m/s. It is a real design. The question is what it actually yields in this city, what generator turns it, what happens on a 100 km/h Cape Doctor day, and whether it beats a solar panel on the same patch of roof. All three are answerable with numbers.

The short answer

On an exposed site it is roughly solar-equivalent per square metre. On an actual Cape Town roof it loses badly to solar. The wind resource is real — this city runs a 5.4 m/s annual mean — but a rooftop is the worst place to harvest it, because a vertical-axis turbine cannot turn away from a storm.

Solar peaks in the same window, which is why the two do not complement each other here as neatly as people assume.

What converts it — generator, not motor

You asked about the motor. Strictly, the machine on the shaft is a generator — the wind spins it and it makes electricity. For a turbine this shape it has to be a direct-drive, low-speed, high-torque permanent-magnet synchronous generator (PMSG). A cheap 1500 rpm alternator is useless here: at 1.2 m blade radius the rotor only turns at 30–135 rpm.

The machine to buy

  • Type: axial-flux PMSG, 3-phase, direct drive (no gearbox, no brushes)
  • Rating: 1.5 kW continuous, 2 kW peak — headroom for gusts
  • Speed: 30–150 rpm operating; ~135 Nm continuous torque
  • Poles: 24–36 poles (12–18 pole pairs) so it makes usable frequency at 90 rpm: ≈17–27 Hz
  • Output: ≈40–60 V AC line-to-line at rated speed

The electronics after it

  • 3-phase bridge rectifier → raw DC
  • MPPT boost converter, 20–60 V in, charging 48 V (51.2 V LFP) at up to 25 A
  • Dump load — a 1.5 kW resistor bank that burns surplus once the battery is full, so the rotor never runs unloaded
  • Contactor short-circuit brake — shorting all three phases is the emergency stop

Never run it open-circuit. An unloaded turbine has almost no braking torque and over-speeds in minutes. The dump load is not an accessory — it is the speed control.

Too-fast wind — the problem this design cannot dodge

A horizontal-axis turbine yaws sideways and furls. An omnidirectional icosahedron has nowhere to hide. It is fully exposed to whatever Cape Town throws at it, and the maker publishes no cut-out and no survival speed. That is the single biggest engineering risk in putting one on a roof.

Cd = 1.2, ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, moment arm 1.9 m. At 50 m/s this unit puts three tonnes of sideways force into its mount. A timber-truss roof is not taking that — it needs a designed steel base with the load path into a structural wall or a ballasted frame.

What protection has to be there

1 · Electromagnetic brake

Short the three generator phases through a contactor — a PMSG becomes a very effective brake. Automatic above ~18 m/s, and the first stage of any emergency stop.

2 · Dump load

A resistive bank sized to the generator, so kinetic energy has somewhere to go once the battery is full. This is also how you hold rotor speed down on a windy-but-not-stormy day.

3 · Aerodynamic spill

A genuinely fixed-axis VAWT needs hinged sail panels or passive pitch that spills lift above ~15 m/s. If the design has none, its survival wind is its structural limit, not its aerodynamic one.

4 · Mechanical brake

A disc and caliper for maintenance lockout, plus a centrifugal overspeed brake as the last passive line of defence if the electronics fail.

What Cape Town asks of it: 170 hours a year above 12 m/s, six hours above 15 m/s, and winter cold fronts that gust past 100 km/h. Ask the maker for the survival speed and the cut-out strategy in writing before anyone bolts one to a roof. If the answer is "it just spins faster", that is the answer.

Head to head with solar on the same roof patch

A GW1200 needs a clear circle 4.6 m across — about 16.3 m² of plan area, plus set-back. Put tilted PV modules on the same patch and you get roughly 1.6 kWp once you allow for row spacing.

The one thing wind genuinely does better: it makes power at night and in winter storms. Solar in Cape Town gives you almost nothing from 18:00 to 06:00, and that is exactly when a house is drawing. A turbine that lands 300 kWh in a windy November evening is filling a gap a panel physically cannot. That is a real argument — it just is not a big enough one to beat four times the yield per rand of hardware in a sheltered suburb.

Method and sources

Wind data

8760 hourly observations for calendar 2025 from Cape Town International (WMO 68816), pulled from the Meteostat v2 hourly bulk archive. Two impossible hourly means (47.9 and 41.1 m/s — next highest 15.8) are METAR reporting errors and were capped. ERA5 reanalysis via Open-Meteo was run as a cross-check: 3.73 m/s at 10 m for the CBD grid cell, 6.36 m/s at Cape Point — reanalysis smooths terrain, so the station measurement (5.43 m/s) is the honest rooftop-city number.

Turbine model

P = ½ ρ A Cp η v³, capped at nameplate, cut-in 3.5 m/s, cut-out 20 m/s. ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, Cp = 0.30 (realistic peak for a small VAWT; Betz ceiling is 0.593), drivetrain and electronics η = 0.85. Effective swept area = 0.40 solidity factor × the icosahedron's mean projected silhouette (2.165·a²): 1.25 m² at 1.2 m edge, 4.99 m² at 2.4 m, 10.6 m² at 3.5 m. Rated wind speed is then solved from the nameplate, giving 14.6 / 11.5 / 10.3 m/s. Torque figures use tip-speed ratio 1.8, typical for a drag-lift hybrid VAWT of this shape.

Solar benchmark

PVGIS 5.3, PVGIS-ERA5 database, Cape Town (−33.9249, 18.4241), 1 kWp, optimal tilt, 14% system losses: 1 540 kWh/kWp/yr. Independent check from Open-Meteo 2025 shortwave radiation: 1 975 kWh/m² global horizontal, 5.41 kWh/m²/day — consistent with published Cape Town figures. Monthly PV shape and totals taken from the PVGIS run, scaled to 1.6 kWp.

What the manufacturer does and does not publish

geowind.kr states edge lengths (1.2 / 2.4 / 3.5 / 7.0 m), cut-in ≈4 m/s, ≤45 dB noise, an icosahedron patent (PCT/KR2023/019844), CES 2026 and iF Design 2026 awards, and a $1M SAFE raise at $10M valuation. It publishes no power curve, no rated wind speed, no cut-out speed, no survival wind speed and no price. Note also that the model numbers track geometry, not watts — 1.2 m → 600, 2.4 m → 1200, 3.5 m → 1800, 7.0 m → 3600, i.e. edge in mm × 500. Treat "1.2 kW" as an inference from size.

Honest limits of this analysis

The Cp, solidity factor and tip-speed ratio are engineering estimates for this class of rotor — the maker's real numbers are unpublished, which is precisely the gap that needs closing before a purchase. Rooftop turbulence intensity in Cape Town is not modelled beyond the exposure factors. No cost comparison is offered because no retail price exists yet.
