Can we go up a level?

Revision 8. Three routes costed side by side: a level on a cast slab, a demolish and rebuild, and selling to buy a modern house. The third one was cheaper than I expected.

Three ways out. Selling the house is the cheapest in new money, and the worst value per square metre.

The level is legal — 10 m from existing ground to the top of the building, no storey cap, no land-use application — but on your method it costs R13.25 m, and R3.25 m of that buys no room.

Selling and buying modern asks for R11.0 m of new cash, not R13–16 m, because the house pays for part of itself. But you hand over an erf worth about R7 m to get 460 m² instead of 780 m², and the market charges R29,000–47,000 per m² for modern space that you can build yourself at R19,000.

1. The rule, as it stands today

This changed on 11 September 2026. The erf is zoned Residential 1 — the City renamed Single Residential 1 to R1 in October 2025. Table 2 of the by-law gives a stand of this size one row:

The old split between wallplate height and roof height was dropped in September 2026. It is now one number: ground to the top of the building, roof and parapet included. There is no cap on storeys in this zoning, so how many levels the house has today never enters the test — only metres. The erf is 2,050 m², comfortably inside the band that gets 10 m.

The method does not change this. Taking the roof off and casting a slab does not lower the building; the slab lands where the old ceiling sat, and the new storey rises 2.7 m from there. The top of the new building is about 57.8 m — 9.3 m above the ground at the downhill wall, 5.8 m above the ground at the street. Measured against the worst reading the margin is 0.7 m; with the half-metre survey tolerance, about 0.2 m. It fits, but not with room to spare.

What that means for the design. Put the stair and the heaviest of the new floor over the uphill half, where the ground is highest, and keep the downhill edge low. A third level over the whole 499 m² footprint, measured from the downhill wall, does not fit — and now that the slab is a real option, that constraint costs money, because a smaller upper floor leaves part of an expensive slab as bare terrace.

3. What is still open

Three things. Any of them can still end this.

  • The title deed (T15018/2024). A restrictive condition can bind this erf independently of the zoning. The conveyancer has been asked for a copy. Until it is read, every answer here is conditional.
  • The building lines. Table 2 wants 6 m from street and common boundaries. The house is lawful as built, but nobody has checked whether building above the existing footprint adds an encroachment.
  • The walls and the ground under them. This is now the big one. A 1984 single-storey brick shell on strip footings was never designed to carry a concrete slab plus another storey. The engineer will either pass the existing footings, require local strengthening, or require underpinning — and that single answer moves the price by more than a million rand (section 4).

5. Why the roof has to come off — and what is behind it

Worth stating plainly, because it is not obvious from the street. The four 1984 elevations draw the house with a flat parapet line at +55.080; nothing pitched is visible from outside. But the roof plan in the same set — “NUWE DAKPLAN”, September 1984, with trusses engineered by Maitland Timber — shows what sits behind that parapet: timber planes at 17½°, ridges, valleys and a flat-roof junction, all enclosed in the box.

So the top of the house is a shell, not a roof over rooms. Two things follow. First, there is no usable volume in there to salvage — at 17½° you get standing height only in a strip near the ridge, which is why attic conversions are normally worth doing only from about 35° of pitch. Second, if the slab goes in, those trusses have to go, and their weight with them: 499 m² of roof structure comes off, and a slab of similar weight goes on. That is the trade the engineer has to sign off, and it is why the strengthening allowance is not padding.

That middle column is worth R1.86m and is not a marketing line. Buying a completed new house from a VAT-registered developer means the price already carries VAT and there is no transfer duty at all. Buying the same value second-hand pays 13% of everything above R13.31m. At these prices, "buy new" is cheaper than "buy nearly new" by more than a million rand before you compare a single tile.

  • You lose the erf. The land, the view down to Batavia Road, the address. That is not sentiment: land is the part of a Cape house that holds its value.
  • You lose the income. The house is let to 30 June 2027. Selling it sells the lease with it, at a discount, or waits. No rent figure is in this model because I have not seen the lease.
  • You take on a levy. Heerenzicht: R5,540 a month, R66,480 a year, before rates of R3,900 a month. Nothing in the level or rebuild columns carries that.
  • You get certainty. No engineer, no underpinning, no winter with the roof off, no eighteen months of dust, no living through it. That is real and it is why people sell.
  • The company is the seller. R M Nova Property (Pty) Ltd holds the title. Getting the proceeds across to a personal purchase is a loan-account or dividend question with its own tax. Ask the accountant before you list, not after.

A hole to close before this table is trustworthy: the capital gains tax line assumes a base cost of R6.5m — the March 2024 purchase price plus transfer costs. Nobody has seen the deed yet. If the base cost is lower the tax rises; at a base of R5.5m it is about R512,000 rather than R296,000.

So what would I do?

If the goal is modern and you do not want to manage a build: sell and buy a completed new house, never a recent resale. R11.0m of new cash, no transfer duty, no engineer, in by Christmas. Accept that you are paying market for market and that the erf is gone.

If the goal is the most house for the rand: demolish and rebuild. R15.80m buys 500 m² of new structure at R31,599 per m², keeps the land, and beats every asking price per square metre on the market. It costs R4.8m more cash than selling, and it is the only route that creates value rather than buying it.

If the goal is one more level and no more: you can have it for R13.25m, with a 0.2–0.7 m height margin and a 1984 structure underneath. It is the most expensive square metre of the three, and the only one where the risk sits in someone else's forty-year-old brickwork. I would not.

9. What to do next

  1. Read the deed. The only document that can bind the erf regardless of zoning.
  2. Surveyor's levels, R8,000–20,000. Fixes the metre the City measures from and turns the 0.7 m margin into a single number.
  3. Structural engineer, R35,000–80,000, with a written brief covering: can the 1984 strip footings carry a 499 m² slab plus a storey; is underpinning needed and how much; can the slab be tied to unroughened 1984 brickwork; and what the slab costs to prop and cast over a house that keeps its tenant. That report is worth every rand of it now — it decides a R4m swing.
  4. Then a quantity surveyor prices the build routes on the same drawings. Anything else is a guess with two decimal places.
  5. If selling is live, get the deed and the base cost first. The capital gains tax line and the company-to-person question both hang off a document nobody has read yet. And price two or three completed new houses against the resale equivalents — the transfer duty difference alone is worth shopping for.

10. What is not in any of the totals

  • The house is unusable while the roof is off. Not "inconvenient" — open to the weather. The lease runs to 30 June 2027, so either the work starts after that or the tenant has to be bought out. Add temporary roof hire, and add the risk of a Cape winter arriving mid-strip.
  • Rent lost. Every month the property is a building site is a month of income forgone, after the lease ends as much as before it.
  • Finance. About R1.09m on the level and R1.30m on the rebuild, at 11% on half the spend over 18 months.
  • Escalation. About R0.60m and R0.71m at 6% a year on the same basis.
  • Asbestos. A 1984 house warrants a survey, R15,000–30,000. Removal runs R100,000–400,000 and is not inside the demolition rate.
  • Insurance, site security, services, and the fittings you choose after handover.
  • Making good the floor below. A slab over a house means props standing in the rooms underneath, cracked plaster, and new beams where walls are cut for the stair.
  • On the sell route only: levies and estate rates from the day of transfer, the capital gains figure while the base cost is unknown, any dividend or loan-account tax on moving company money to a personal purchase, and the cost of a tenant buying out or a sale delayed to June 2027.
