Erf 465, Bellville · 3 Batavia Street, Welgemoed · 18 November 2026

Can we go up a level?

Revision 6. Costed against the by-law as it stands today, and reviewed twice by an independent model.

On the planning rules: yes. With about 0,7 m to spare.

The height limit on this erf is 10 m from existing ground level to the top of the building, and it is the only control here that can bite. The house uses 3,1–6,6 m of it. One more level takes it to 5,8–9,3 m. No land-use application is needed if that holds. There is no storey limit.

That is a planning answer, not a permission. Three things are still unopened: the title deed (never read), the building lines (the existing footprint is assumed lawful), and the structure (nobody has looked at whether 1984 footings carry a third level).

And then there is the money. R8,97 m to add the level. R15,80 m to demolish and rebuild. Against a house worth R6,85–8,89 m.

And the roof changes the price. The house is topped by a false roof — a flat parapet hiding 17½° timber planes. Replacing those planes with attic trusses gives the same 200 m² for R4.93 m, not R8.97 m, and lifts the roof line 1.8 m instead of 2.7 m.

1. The rule, as it stands today

This changed on 11 September 2026, which is why the answer moved. The erf is zoned Residential 1 — the City renamed Single Residential 1 to R1 in October 2025. Table 2 of the by-law gives a stand of this size one row of figures:

The old split between wallplate height and roof height was dropped in September 2026. It is now one number: ground to the top of the building, roof and parapet included. There is also no cap on storeys in this zoning, so how many levels the house has today never enters the test — only metres. The erf is 2,050 m², comfortably inside the band that gets 10 m (every stand above 650 m² does).

The ground levels either side of the house are read off the survey contours on the plans, good to about half a metre. The two readings — downhill wall, street wall — are the two ends of the argument. Nominal margin: 0.7 m in the worst case, 4.2 m in the best. Apply the half-metre survey tolerance and the worst case narrows to about 0.2 m, so treat 0,7 m as the optimistic end of a thin margin rather than a comfort. The 2,7 m is the net rise to the highest point of the new roof, build-up and parapet included — not a room height. A second level does not fit from the downhill side.

What this means for the design. Set the new level back over the uphill half, where the ground is highest and the margin is widest, and drop the roof away from the downhill edge. That is not a stylistic preference; it is what keeps the building inside 10 m without needing a departure.

The one reading that fails. If the City measured from the lowest corner of the erf (46.5 m, 30 m down the garden at the Batavia Road frontage) rather than from the ground against the building, the house would already be about 8.6 m tall and no level would fit. Nobody measures a building from a point in the garden, and item 126 on earth banks and retaining walls is the by-law's guard against gaming it — but it is why the surveyor's levels in section 7 come before any design work.

3. What is still open

Three things. Any of them can still end this.

  • The title deed (T15018/2024). A restrictive condition can bind this erf independently of the zoning — a limit on dwellings, a height or a design clause. The conveyancer has been asked for a copy. Until it is read, every answer here is conditional.
  • The building lines. Table 2 wants 6 m from both street and common boundaries. The house was built in 1984 and is lawful as it stands, but nobody has checked whether adding above the existing footprint adds an encroachment. It should not, if the new walls sit inside the old ones — that has to be drawn, not assumed.
  • The structure. The drawings show no footings designed for a third level, and no engineer has been on site. Whether the 1984 frame and foundations carry it decides both the feasibility and the R550,000 strengthening allowance in section 4.
  • The ceiling line. Nothing in the set dimensions the top-floor ceiling, and it is the one number the attic route in section 4b turns on: the lower the ceilings, the further the parapet has to go up. It is a tape measure in two rooms, and it should be the first thing measured when you next walk the house.

4b. The false roof — and the cheaper way up

You are right about the roof, and it matters more than the wall heights do. The 1984 elevations draw the house with a flat parapet line at +55.080 — from the street it reads as a single crisp slab. Nothing pitched is visible anywhere in the four elevations. But the roof plan (a later sheet in the same set, “NUWE DAKPLAN”, September 1984, trusses engineered by Maitland Timber) shows what is behind that parapet: timber roof planes at 17½° with ridges, valleys and a flat-roof junction, all hidden inside the box.

So the top of the house is a shell, not a roof over rooms. That has three consequences, and one of them is money.

  • It does not change the height test. The parapet at +55.080 is the top of the building, whatever is hidden behind it. The 10 m arithmetic in section 2 is unchanged.
  • You cannot simply move into the void. Attic conversions are normally only worth doing from about 35° of pitch upwards; at 17½° there is standing height only in a strip near the ridge. Converting these trusses as they are would give you a crawl space, not bedrooms.
  • But replacing them is far cheaper than building a storey on top. The walls already carry a roof load, the parapet already exists, and nothing has to be craned into place. Strip the 17½° planes, fit attic trusses that give a full-height centre, lay the floor at the existing ceiling line, raise the parapet and finish. Same 200 m², no new external walls, no re-loading of the walls below, no crane.

Because the floor goes down at the existing ceiling rather than a full storey above it, the roof line rises about 1.8 m instead of 2.7 m. That is the part that buys back the margin: the worst-case clearance in section 2 goes from 0.7 m to roughly 1.6 m.

Why I did not lead with this. It is not a storey on top, it is a storey inside the roof, and the ceilings will be broken by the slope on at least one side. It also has one genuinely awkward dependency: the trusses are Hydro-Nail engineered, and an engineer may insist on a new truss design rather than a conversion — which is exactly what the price above assumes. If the engineer condemns the existing roof structure instead of replacing it in kind, A2 collapses back to A. If he does not, A2 is the better answer on both cost and height, and it is the option I would take to a quantity surveyor first. The 1.8 m rise assumes the top-floor ceiling sits about 2.4 m above the main floor; measure it and the figure moves, by roughly 0.1 m of parapet for every 0.1 m of ceiling.

Welgemoed's established houses sell between about R5.2m and R8m. Its best new estate asks R10.995m to R21.995m — asking prices, not sales. The rebuild needs the finished house to be worth R22.65m or more, which is above the highest asking price currently listed in the suburb. That is the argument against rebuilding, and it does not depend on my cost estimate being exact: even at the low end of the cost band, R12.61m added to a R6.85m house asks for R19.46m.

So: build up, or knock down?

Build up, and build into the roof. R4.93m against R8.97m and R15.80m is not a close call, and the level is the easier legal route — no demolition, no NHBRC enrolment, no reconnection, and the tenant's lease can run to its end while you draw. Don't rebuild for value. The honest reasons to demolish are a structure that has failed, a layout that cannot be fixed, or a plan to redevelop the 2,050 m² erf — and that last one is a better question than this one.

7. What to do next

  1. Read the deed. The one document that can bind the erf regardless of zoning. Chase the conveyancer.
  2. Get a surveyor to certify the levels, roughly R8,000–20,000. On a slope this is what fixes the metre the City will measure from — the by-law lets the City require exactly such a certificate, and a building plan cannot be defended without the number.
  3. Pay an engineer R20,000–60,000 to rule on three things at once: the footings, whether the 17½° trusses can be replaced with attic trusses, and whether the slope needs retaining or drainage work. That single report decides which of the three options is real.
  4. Then price the drawings with a quantity surveyor. That is the only step that turns R8.97m into a budget.

8. What is not in either total

  • Rent lost. The lease runs to 30 June 2027, so sequencing the work after it saves the break cost but not the income — while the property is a building site it earns nothing. At market rent, every month of construction is a month forgone.
  • Finance. About R0.74m on the level and R1.30m on the rebuild, at 11% on half the spend over 18 months.
  • Escalation. About R0.40m and R0.71m at 6% a year, on the same half-spend basis.
  • Asbestos. A 1984 house warrants a survey, R15,000–30,000. If it is found, removal runs R100,000–400,000 on the rebuild, and it is not inside the demolition rate.
  • Insurance, site security and municipal services during the build, temporary accommodation if you move in, and the fittings you choose after handover.
  • The lower ground floor will not be untouched. Stair openings, service risers and any foundation work reach into it, and access for the crane crosses the garden.
