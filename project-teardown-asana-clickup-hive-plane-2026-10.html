And the importers say plainly how they grow: take the customer from Asana, ClickUp, Jira or Linear with a per-tool token, not a CSV.

The price is not the bill. Four things sit outside these numbers, and in this category they routinely double the total: (1) monthly billing instead of annual — up to 33% more; (2) AI credits beyond the allowance, which every one of the four meters; (3) Enterprise tiers, gated behind "contact sales" precisely because they are priced on seats, security and volume, not a rate card; (4) onboarding and migration — Plane is the only one where migration tooling is free and self-hosting removes the per-seat problem entirely.

Where self-hosting changes the maths

Plane is the only one of the four that can run on your own infrastructure. At 100 seats the Business tier is about $15,600 a year; a Pro licence on your own server is the same per-seat rate but with full data ownership and no seat-count ceiling imposed by a vendor — you pay for the servers instead. Below roughly 25 seats the hosting cost outweighs the saving; above roughly 150 it starts to win.

05

For Luke

The short version, in plain English, without any of the code.

Four tools that all claim the same job

They all hold tasks, show them as boards and lists, and now all four have AI. The differences are in who they are built for and what they charge for.

  1. Asana is the enterprise one. It is built to show the board and the executives the same picture — projects roll up into portfolios, portfolios into goals. AI is included in every paid tier, which is unusual. You pay the most for it: about $25 a seat at the tier most teams actually need.
  2. ClickUp is the everything one. Eleven kinds of view, plus chat, docs and email, in a single app. It is the cheapest way to get a lot of surface area, and it charges separately for AI usage on top. The trade-off is weight: it is a big application and it feels like one.
  3. Hive is the agency one. If you bill by the hour, the things you need are timesheets, proofing, approvals and holiday calendars — and Hive ships all of them natively. It is the cheapest entry price of the four, but it is not trying to be your company's operating system.
  4. Plane is the one you can own. It is open source, it runs on your own servers, and it has the most serious AI design of the four — reusable instructions, connectors to other tools, a proper model registry. It is also the youngest, so it has the least polish and the smallest support bench.
If it is a company-wide rolloutAsana. The portfolio and goals layer is what buys executive buy-in, and AI is included rather than metered.
If it is a small team on a budgetClickUp at $7, or Hive at $5 if the team bills time.
If data has to stay on our serversPlane. It is the only one that can, and at $13 a seat it is half of Asana.
If the team is an agencyHive. Nobody else ships proofing and timesheet rounding natively.

What the code changed about this view. We expected the four to differ on features. They differ more on architecture: ClickUp builds one bundle per view and meters its AI; Hive ships 523 chunks and puts timesheets and proofing at the centre; Plane runs eight small services and treats migration from rivals as a product; Asana keeps its product code off the public internet entirely. That last one is a compliment, and it is also why we know least about Asana.

One thing worth copying, whichever we buy. Plane's skills library — the user writes the instruction, the system stores it and reuses it — is a better AI model than a chat box bolted onto a task list.

06

How this was done, and what it cannot tell you

Method

  • Each product's own page loaded in one controlled headless Chrome, driven over CDP.
  • REA inspect-web-page captured the page and the scripts it served.
  • export-web-scripts wrote each file out with a SHA-256 digest and a source evidence ID.
  • analyze-javascript-application rebuilt module graphs, route candidates, endpoint candidates and vendor fingerprints — statically, with no execution.
  • Direct mining of the same files filled in framework markers, GraphQL operation names and product vocabulary.
  • Prices read off each vendor's pricing page on 21 October 2026; totals computed as seats × rate × 12.

Limits

  • Static evidence only — captured JavaScript is never run, so runtime behaviour is unknown.
  • Asana shipped no product chunks publicly; its row is a statement about its login page, not its product.
  • Hive's analysis stopped at the provider timeout; its counts are the final reported state, not a ceiling.
  • String frequency is a presence indicator, not a feature census.
  • Server-side behaviour, encrypted configuration and anything built at runtime remain unknown.
  • Prices change; treat the costing as a dated snapshot, not a quote.
