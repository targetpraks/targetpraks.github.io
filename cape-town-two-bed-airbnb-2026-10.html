02What each one actually earns

Gross is 168 let nights at the suburb's measured nightly rate, adjusted for what this specific unit is. Costs are 34 % of gross (Airbnb, co-host, cleaning, utilities, supplies, linen, repairs), plus rates at 0.55 % of value a year, plus levies, plus R12 000 of insurance and licensing.

Rates and levies are read off each listing's own overview block, not assumed — which is why Disa Park falls: its levies are R5 731 a month, and that single line costs it a full point of yield. The pattern underneath is the same in every South African coastal market right now: yield falls as price per square metre rises. Observatory at R27 000–R29 000 returns 11 %; Woodstock and Salt River at R15 000–R25 000 return 8–9 %; Sea Point and the City Centre at R48 000–R53 000 return 5–8 %. The beach is a capital asset; the suburb is an income asset.

The other half of the picture: can it carry a bond?

Cash-on-cash at an 80 % bond, prime plus one, before tax. Below zero, the rent does not cover the instalment and the shortfall comes out of Ricardo's pocket every month.

This is the line that decides everything. Above roughly R3 million, a Cape Town two-bedroom does not service an 80 % bond out of short-let income at today's 10.75 % prime. Three Anchor Bay loses R4 668 a month before tax; Oranjezicht loses R35 652 a month. The cheap stock services its debt; the beach stock needs a bigger deposit, or cash.

The runner-up, and why it loses

515 Obs Court, the auction lot in the same building at R1 850 000 for 63 m², returns 11.4% — a hair more on paper. It is not the better buy. It is smaller, it has one fewer bathroom, it costs R29 365 a square metre against R27 329, and it carries transfer duty where the new unit does not. It also has a date: bidding closes 12 November, and bidding is not a price, it is a floor. Every 5 % the room pushes the hammer price costs about half a point of yield — at R2 034 500 it returns 10.4%; at R2 220 000 it returns 9.5%, which is where the new unit across the courtyard already sits, with no auction, no duty and ten more square metres.

In your own name: 15.9% a year. Through a company: 13.6%. The company halves the yearly tax bill — 27 % instead of 45 % — and then gives all of it back, three times over: a half-point higher bond rate, a 21.6 % capital gains rate instead of 18 %, 20 % dividends tax to get the money out of the company, and R18 000 a year of Companies Act paperwork that a personal tax return does not need.

Cumulative cash in Ricardo's hand, after every tax and every cost. The company line starts ahead — it holds on to more of each year's profit — and crosses below after the sale, where the capital gains and dividends tax arrive together.

Where the money genuinely differs

  • The transfer duty does not differ. SARS applies the same sliding scale to people, companies, close corporations and trusts — the old flat rate for legal entities no longer applies. Anyone quoting 8 % for a company is quoting a rate table that is not in force.
  • Income tax does. 27 % flat against a 45 % marginal rate. On this unit that is about R8 070 a year in the company's favour.
  • Capital gains tax does. Companies include 80 % of the gain, people 40 %. At the top rate that is 21.6 % against 18 %. On this exit: R48 083 more, in the wrong direction.
  • Dividends tax does. A company's profit is not Ricardo's money until it is distributed, and distribution costs 20 %. Retaining it avoids the 20 % and traps the cash.
  • Compliance does. Annual financials, CIPC returns, provisional tax, and a bank that wants personal surety anyway — so the liability shield leaks the moment it matters.
  • Small business corporation rates would change the answer — 7 % instead of 27 % at this level of profit — but section 12E's 20 % investment-income test is usually the wall when rent is the only income. Have the accountant confirm before anyone structures around it.
The VAT trap. A company letting for under 28 days is supplying commercial accommodation, so it can register for VAT voluntarily and claim back R260 217 of input tax on this purchase. It then charges output VAT on every night: R60 084 a year, and R466 010 on the sale. Over ten years the credit costs R806 629 more than it saves. Do not register. Do not let an adviser register you. Turnover here is R460 641 — comfortably under the R1 million compulsory threshold.

The one thing a company genuinely fixes

Not tax — ownership. Three investors, unequal contributions, one exit clause and a shareholders' agreement is a company problem, and a company solves it. A single owner with a bond and a short-let business is a personal problem, and a company only adds R180 000 of paperwork and two percentage points of return to it.

05The season, and the risk that actually bites

Live page-one medians for a two-bedroom whole home, peak week 19–26 December 2026 against low week 10–17 July 2027. Muizenberg swings 3.1×; Observatory 1.6×. The suburb with the highest December rate is not the one with the best year.

  • The by-law. Cape Town's draft Short-Term Letting By-law requires registration and moves any property let more than 183 nights a year onto commercial rates — roughly three times residential — from 1 July 2027. Budget for it, not against it.
  • The building's rules, not the city's. Most sectional-title schemes can ban short-letting outright. Obs Court and Three Anchor Bay's Princeton both already allow it; that is a condition of the offer, in writing, before any deposit.
  • Occupancy is the assumption that carries the model. Every number above runs at 46 % of nights, and the suburb's measured nightly rate. The grid below moves both at once, on the winning unit — the range is 6.5% to 17.3%, and that spread, not the interest rate, is what decides whether this is a good buy.
  • Auction terms. No bond clause, no cooling-off, deposit on the fall of the hammer. The two "no buyer commission" lots are the exception in this market — most platforms charge the buyer 10 % plus VAT.

06Method and sources

Measured, not quoted. Where a number came from a listing, the listing is linked above.

  • Nightly rates: live Airbnb search for each suburb, whole home, two bedrooms, four guests, two seven-night windows — peak 19–26 December 2026, low 10–17 July 2027 — page-one medians of the displayed seven-night totals. 10 suburbs sampled, 15–24 priced cards each.
  • Asking prices: Property24's own result counts under price filters, not page-one listings — page one is paid placement and overstates by up to 40 % in Sea Point.
  • Levies and rates: from each listing's own Property Overview block where stated; where a listing withheld them, 0.55 % of value a year for rates and an estimate for levies.
  • Transfer duty: SARS table in force 1 April 2026, unchanged from 2025/26, applied identically to individuals and legal entities.
  • Income tax: 45 % marginal for a natural person, 27 % for a company, per SARS rates for years of assessment ending 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027.
  • Capital gains: 40 % inclusion for natural persons, 80 % for companies, per SARS.
  • VAT: commercial accommodation is a taxable supply; residential letting is exempt; the compulsory registration threshold is R1 million of taxable turnover.
  • Shares in a residential property company are themselves "property" for transfer duty — so selling the company instead of the flat does not escape the duty.
  • Not modelled: the City's revaluation, special levies, tenant damage, and the possibility that short-letting is regulated out of a building entirely.
