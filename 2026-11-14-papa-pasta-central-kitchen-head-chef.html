The head chef, the sauces, and the wet-pasta line

A spec for the one seat that owns the standard, a profile that can be loaded into Constellation today, and the three-and-a-half-person team that can actually run this kitchen at launch.

Prepared 14 November 2026 · Bruce Wayne · For Ricardo's ruling · Nothing in here is written to a live system yet.

01 The answer, before the detail

One seat owns the standard, two seats run the line, and a part-time pair of hands keeps the racks and the wash-up moving. That is 3.5 full-time people at launch, not a brigade.

3.5 people at launch: head chef, sauce chef, pasta & pack technician, half a kitchen assistant
87 planned servings per 10 kg batch — the number the whole kitchen is planned on
3.1 batches a trading day for one store at the R600k planning revenue
9 sauces, one build each, all made here — never in the store

Three facts shape the whole design.

The pasta is not the hard part; the repeat is. The four formulas are written, costed and locked. What does not exist yet is a kitchen that produces the same 10 kg batch the same way on a Tuesday in month three. That is a person, a card and a record — and it is the head chef's job.

The sauces are the margin and the risk. All nine are made centrally. Five are held hot, three finish in the wok, one is never heated at all. Cream bases that split, an egg emulsion that breaks, a raw pesto with a four-day life: these are the failures a customer tastes and remembers. The sauce seat is not a commis role.

Wet pasta turns a kitchen into a shelf. Fresh pasta carries a 5–7 day life at 1–4 °C, and every pack needs a weight, a date and an allergen line. Retail adds revenue and adds waste at the same time; the head chef owns the daily decision of how much to make.

Three things only Ricardo can rule

  1. Make point. The written intent is that Bokgat Base is extruded in store (the Pasta Theatre) and the other three formulas plus every sauce are made centrally. That intent sits in the draft master, not in the canon or a decision log. One line makes it real, and it decides what this seat is measured on.
  2. Wet-pasta format and launch volume. Fresh chilled 500 g packs, anchored on the R49–79 retail prices already in the model, or a dried retail pack instead. The two need different equipment, different shelf life and a different person's day.
  3. Pay band. Central-kitchen labour is unpriced in the five-year model — a zero there means unknown, not free. This is the first real number to put in, and it is your call, not mine.

The three pillars, and what each one actually demands

Pillar 1

Pasta is the hero

The four formulas are the product. Three of them — Protein+ Pro, Farmhouse and Chickpea Power Pro — are central-kitchen work by design: the lupin allergen, The Local Farmer's powders, and pre-treated chickpea flour are all reasons not to put them in a 40 m² store. Bokgat Base is the volume driver and the theatre.

  • Extrude to the card. Hydration 32–34% on Bokgat, barrel never above 55 °C, bronze die, cut at the die. The machine is a Gustotek GT108, 0.55 kW, 16–20 kg/h.
  • Dry to a reading, not a feeling. Surface-dry 4–12 hours to 28–32% moisture — the fresh spec, not the 12.5% dry spec. Firm outside, supple inside.
  • Portion to 111 g dried (about 244 g cooked at a 2.2× yield). One size, no free-pour.
  • Hit 87 servings per 10 kg. 91 is the ceiling, 70 is void, and the band collapses the moment somebody weighs a real run.
  • Run the witnessed batch. Weigh the output off the racks and take a moisture reading, in front of a second person. One morning's work that replaces a 70–91 range with a fact — and it is the first thing this seat does.
  • Protect the allergen line. Lupin after Protein+ Pro means a documented line clean before the next formula touches that die.
  • Hold the cook test. Al dente in 3–5 minutes fresh, 90–180 seconds dried, and a batch that fails it does not leave the building.

Pillar 2

Pasta sauce

Nine sauces, one build each, all pork-free and cooked alcohol-free, all made here so that a Carbonara in Soweto tastes like one in Sandton. Five are held hot, three finish in the wok, one is served cold.

  • Write the nine batch cards. The recipes exist; the production cards do not. Each card carries ingredients, order, temperatures, times, yield, portion, chill step, allergen line and the sensory check. This is the seat's first written deliverable after the witnessed run.
  • Braise properly. The ragù is fully braised here — the one element a store cannot rush — then chilled and packed under a batch code.
  • Chill to a number. Core from +70 °C to +3 °C inside 90 minutes, probe in the record, every batch. No pouch leaves without a code and a date.
  • Check every batch against a held reference — colour, texture, taste, three points, signed. Consistency is a measurement here, not an opinion.

Pillar 3

Artisanal wet pasta — the retail line

Fresh extruded pasta sold as a retail pack. This is the pillar that turns a production kitchen into a brand on a shelf, and the one with the least written down. The fresh spec gives 5–7 days at 1–4 °C, up to 10 vacuum-sealed. A 40 kg run yields roughly 48.7 kg of fresh pasta — about 97 packs of 500 g.

  • Own the make-to-shelf decision daily. Shelf life is short; waste is the margin. The head chef decides the run size each morning against what sold, and signs the waste.
  • Every pack carries four things: net weight, batch, date code, allergen line. An uncoded pack is a recall waiting for a date.
  • Validate the shelf life. Five to seven days is what the recipe says, not what a test proved. The seat runs the trial — day 0, 5 and 7, sensory plus a laboratory count — and the spend is Ricardo's to approve.
  • Keep the retail anchors honest. The model carries R49–55 for Bokgat, R59–69 for Farmhouse and Chickpea, R69–79 for Protein+ Pro, per 500 g. Those are the numbers the line is measured against; a pack that cannot be made inside them is not a product.
  • Feed the label. Ingredients, allergens, storage, date. The legal sign-off on the label is a gate, not a kitchen task — the seat supplies the evidence for it.

03 Who to hire, and how to test them

Must have

  • Production cooking at volume, not à la carte only. Someone who has fed 200–500 covers a day from a fixed menu, or run a central production kitchen, bakery or food factory line.
  • Batch discipline. Has worked to a written recipe card and a batch record — and can explain what they did when the card and the reality disagreed.
  • Cold-chain literacy. Blast chilling, probe logs, date coding, HACCP language used correctly and without being prompted.
  • Allergen seriousness. Can describe a line clean between an allergen and a non-allergen run without needing the word "lupin" explained.
  • Numbers. Can work out batches from covers, and yield from kilos, on paper, in front of you.
  • Halaal-comfortable kitchen practice. No pork, no alcohol in cooking, and an understanding that a claim is not made until the certificate exists.

Disqualifiers

  • Talks about "my version" of a dish before tasting the standard one.
  • No experience of a written record — everything carried in the head.
  • Cannot say what they would do with 8 litres of cream sauce left at close.
  • Treats the pasta machine as a machine they "operate" rather than a process they own.
  • Wants a brigade and a sous before the kitchen has proven one store.

The three seats, in one line each

  • Head Chef — owns the standard, the record and the numbers. The only seat allowed to change a batch.
  • Sauce Chef — runs the nine sauces, the braise, the chill and the pack. Chosen for steadiness, because a ragù is three hours of doing the same thing correctly.
  • Pasta & Pack Technician — extruder, racks, moisture, portion, date code. Chosen for conscientiousness above all, because every pack carries a weight and a date and both have to be right.
  • Kitchen Assistant — part-time at launch: wash-up, rack turnaround, receiving. The seat that quietly decides whether the technician can keep the racks moving.

04 The profile, wired into Constellation

Constellation holds a department's capability requirements and a position's Role Blueprint — range bands on measured dimensions plus MUST, FLAG and BONUS rules that produce a fit verdict. Today the Papa Pasta side of the org has four seats under ZOR BU 2 — Brand Ops 2: a GM, an administrator, a customer-service rep and a support specialist. There is no kitchen seat in the application at all.

One gap to close in the app first

The capability library has eighteen capabilities — operations, quality, process, cold chain, compliance, planning and the rest. Every one of them describes a food factory. None of them describes a cook. A kitchen seat measured only on those dimensions will read as a production supervisor, and the wrong person will score well.

Proposed addition — one object in the library: culinary → REALISTIC, ARTISTIC, CONSC. Hands-on work, palate, and the discipline that makes a sauce repeat. Defined once, it works for all four kitchen seats and every future store.

4 · Load it, in this order

  1. Add the capability. culinary in the capability library, dimensions REALISTIC, ARTISTIC, CONSC.
  2. Create the department under Brand Ops 2, then set its requirements on the capabilities screen to the eight levels above. The app stores them in 5% steps and refuses a defining capability under 85% — the numbers pass both.
  3. Create the three positions in the new department, family Operations.
  4. Set each Role Blueprint — bands and rules as above. Bands save only at ten points wide, which is why every band in the file is exactly ten.
  5. Then assess. A shortlisted candidate's scores produce a verdict and up to three plain-language reasons, and the MUST rules are what stop a charming wrong hire.

That validator confirms: every blueprint passes, every band is exactly ten points, every capability key exists in the library, and the department clears the 85% headline and 50% floor rules.

What is not done, deliberately

Nothing was written to the Constellation database. The blueprint loader refuses a file without an acceptance receipt and reconciles one-for-one against seats that exist, so the seats have to be created and ruled first. The dev database currently holds no organisation at all — the seats in this document do not exist anywhere yet.

05 Build it in stages, each ending on a fact

StageWhat happensIt is finished when
A · Seat the standard Head chef hired and in the kitchen. The witnessed 10 kg run is done. The nine sauce batch cards are written from the BOM. The signed run record shows output weight, moisture and servings per batch — and one batch of each of the nine sauces has passed a sensory check against the card.
B · Prove the repeat Sauce Chef in. Production runs to cards, records every batch, waste is measured rather than estimated. Twenty consecutive batches land at ≥ 87 servings and within ±3% of BOM cost, with zero allergen cross-contact events.
C · Open the shelf Pasta & Pack Technician in. The wet-pasta line starts with a shelf-life trial and a label that survives the legal gate. A laboratory result supports the day-5 and day-7 claim, 20 packs out of 20 carry weight, batch, date and allergens, and waste is under 5% over four weeks.
D · Supply the network Second store supplied from Bedfordview. Kitchen Assistant goes full-time; the second sauce cook is added only when the volume demands it. Four consecutive weeks, zero rejected deliveries, and one cold-chain excursion caught, quarantined and documented correctly.

No dates. Each stage ends on a state of the kitchen, not a calendar — and stage D is the first point at which a second cook is a fact rather than a hope.

06 What the seat inherits that does not exist yet

Three documents a production kitchen normally opens with are empty pages in the build: sauce and ancillary production, the HACCP programme, and cold chain and distribution. The staffing page is empty too. That is not a criticism of the plan — it is the honest starting state, and it is why this seat is the first hire rather than the fourth.

  • Sauce production method — empty page. Becomes the nine batch cards.
  • HACCP programme — empty page. The head chef writes the kitchen's version with the GM, and it is signed off before the first delivery to a store.
  • Cold chain and distribution — empty page. Becomes the chill-to-dispatch routine, with the probe log as its evidence.
  • Commissary staffing and shifts — empty page. This document is the answer to it.

The one thing I would not compromise on

Hire the person who insists on weighing the batch and writing down the reading — even if they are slower in the interview and less charming than the alternative. The pasta is written, the sauces are designed, and the machine is chosen. The only thing standing between this concept and a consistent plate in store five is a person who will not let a batch go out unrecorded.
