01Where any claim comes from

Everything below hangs on one clause. Read the wording closely, because it does two different things: it creates the 7-day cure period, and it creates two alternative remedies that you must choose between — not stack.

Clause 13.1 — Breach of contract If either party breaches this agreement by failing to fulfill on due date any of the terms and conditions of this agreement and fails to remedy such breach within 7 (seven) days after written notice by the other party, addressed to the domicilium citandi et executandi of the other party, then and in that instance the non-defaulting party will be entitled to:
ClauseWhat it gives you
13.1.1 Cancellation + forfeiture of monies paid Purchaser forfeits all monies paid, "without prejudice to any other rights and remedies … and specifically the right to claim damages." Seller also gets the balance of the deposit after commission + VAT and the wasted costs in 8.4.
13.1.2 Specific performance Claim immediate payment of the purchase price and fulfilment of every term. Transfer is forced through against a buyer who is stalling. No cancellation, no remarketing, no loss to prove.
13.2 A 24-hour clock instead of 7 days Where the breach falls at a time critical to the registration procedure, you may require remedy within 24 hours. Rarely used — and only safe if the deadline genuinely sits inside the registration timeline.
8.4 Conveyancer's wasted fees and disbursements "The party in breach shall be liable for and pay on demand to the Transferring Attorney all fees and disbursements reasonably incurred … as at the date of cancellation." A direct, invoice-backed claim.
9.2 Estate agent's commission "If this Agreement is lawfully cancelled due to a breach by the Purchaser / Seller, the Purchaser / Seller shall be liable for payment of the commission." The defaulting buyer picks up the agent's bill.
9.3 Commission paid out of the deposit The parties irrevocably instruct the transferring attorney to pay commission out of the deposit and monies held in trust where the agreement is cancelled through the purchaser's default. This is how you actually get paid, not just how you get a judgment.

One caveat, stated plainly: the clause text above is taken from the signed Heydenrych Properties offer-to-purchase form on file for 51 Trichardt Street — the same standard form. The Frances Street offer may be on the Seeff form, which numbers its clauses differently. The substance of a South African residential OTP is near-identical, but Brian Frank must confirm each clause number against the actual Frances document before anything is quoted in a letter.

02What the seven days do — and do not do

This is the part that loses cases. "The notice lapses" feels like an ending. Legally it is a door opening, and only if you walk through it in writing.

Stage 1

The notice is served

Valid only if it lands at the purchaser's domicilium citandi et executandi — the address named in the offer, not the buyer's Gmail. It must name the specific breach and the clause breached.

Stage 2

Seven days run

The purchaser is in mora. It must actually remedy the breach — pay the deposit, deliver the guarantee, sign the transfer documents. An apology is not a remedy.

Stage 3

You elect — in writing

Nothing cancels itself. You must notify the purchaser that you cancel, or that you claim specific performance. Silence extends the contract indefinitely and costs you the claim.

The trap in the other direction. If the notice is defective — wrong address, no clause cited, seven days miscounted, a demand so vague it cannot be complied with — then it achieved nothing. And if you then treat the contract as cancelled on the strength of a bad notice, you have repudiated. Your buyer's wasted bond costs and legal fees become their claim against you. In a R5.9m deal that is an expensive own goal.

Five things the notice must have had

  • The correct legal name of the purchaser exactly as it appears in the offer.
  • Service at the domicilium address named in the offer — by hand or tracked delivery, with proof.
  • The specific obligation breached, with the clause number and the date it fell due.
  • A clear demand to perform, and a cure period of seven days stated on its face.
  • A reservation of rights, and no premature statement that the contract "is hereby cancelled".

03The heads of damage — what is actually claimable

South African law puts the innocent seller in the position it would have occupied had the buyer performed. For a cancelled sale of immovable property that means the lost bargain plus every rand spent because of it. Nine heads, in the order a court would work through them.

The good news about head 2 and head 3. Commission and the conveyancer's wasted fees are not discretionary nice-to-haves — they are written into clauses 9.2 and 8.4 as debts owed by "the party in breach". On a R5.9m sale at 3.5%, commission alone is R206,500 plus VAT = R237,475. Those two heads are usually recoverable even where the loss on resale is contested.

If they cannot pay, the claim has told you that in three months instead of eighteen, and you cancel, keep whatever deposit is held, and resell. Damages are what you do with the leftovers when both of those fail.

06The penalty trap — you cannot take both

This is the single most common way a seller overreaches and ends up with less. Section 2(1) of the Conventional Penalties Act 15 of 1962 is short and absolute.

Conventional Penalties Act 15 of 1962 — s2(1) A creditor shall not be entitled to recover in respect of an act or omission which is the subject of a penalty stipulation, both the penalty and damages, or, except where the relevant contract expressly so provides, to recover damages in lieu of the penalty.

What that means for this sale

Keeping the deposit and also suing for the loss on resale is double recovery. One or the other.

But clause 13.1.1 expressly preserves your right to claim damages — which is exactly the door s2(1) leaves open. So you may elect damages instead of the forfeiture. That is a materially better position than most sellers realise, and it should be quoted in the election letter.

And the moderation risk

If you keep the deposit, s3 lets a court reduce it where it is "out of proportion to the prejudice suffered". A deposit of R590,000 against a real loss of R120,000 will not survive intact.

The safe sequence is arithmetic first: total the real loss, then compare it with the deposit, then elect. Never elect before the numbers are done.

07Interest — the rate is fixed, not floating

Morato interest runs at the prescribed rate under the Prescribed Rate of Interest Act 55 of 1975: the repo rate plus 3.5%, reset on the first day of the second month after any repo change.

Drafting point: state the interest claim in the cancellation letter itself. A claim for interest first raised eighteen months later invites an argument about the date it started running, and courts are not generous about it.

08Four things that will shrink the claim

A damages claim is not a wish list. Each of these is a defence the purchaser's attorney will run, and each is answerable — if the file is built properly now.

  • Mitigation. You must resell at a reasonable price within a reasonable time. Holding out for R5.9m for eighteen months and then claiming the difference will fail. Get the property back on the market promptly and keep the listing trail.
  • Causation. Every head must flow from the breach, not from the market, load-shedding or the rates bill you would have paid anyway. Separate the two.
  • Betterment and double-counting. Carrying costs already reflected in the bond interest claim cannot be claimed twice. Show the workings, not just a total.
  • Duty to speak. If the purchaser's attorney asks whether you will accept a reduced deposit as full settlement, silence is not neutral — it is read as refusal, and it hardens the litigation.

09The next seven days

  1. Confirm the notice was validly servedPull the proof of service and the address it went to. If it was emailed to the buyer instead of served at the domicilium, the clock has not started at all — and there is still time to serve properly.
  2. Fix the election date in the diaryDay seven must not pass silently. On that day Brian Frank issues the election letter: cancel and claim, or claim specific performance.
  3. Do not resell before the cancellation is effectiveSelling to a second buyer while the first contract still stands is a breach by you, and it hands the defaulting buyer a counterclaim. Cancel first, market second.
  4. Build the loss file nowMunicipal accounts, insurance schedule, bond statements, the agent's invoice, the conveyancer's fee note, and a written market opinion supporting the resale figure. This is what the claim is made of.
  5. Get the conveyancer to freeze the trust positionAny deposit or guarantee already held must not be released. Clause 9.3 gives the transferring attorney an instruction to pay commission from it — that instruction should only be actioned once the election is made.
