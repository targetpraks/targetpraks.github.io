Ten apps for two iPads

Ten things to install for Donatello (9, Grade 4 from January) and Gianna (7) — six that build English, three that build maths, one that builds speech. Priced in rands, chosen on evidence, and ranked so you know what to buy first.

Every price is the one on the page I read it from US$1 = R16.93 (16 Nov 2026) All prices are ZA App Store or the vendor's own page

The short answer

  • Core five, R2 853 for the year: Reading Eggs, the Nessy Hairy Phonics pack, Teach Your Monster to Read, Khan Academy Kids (free) and Matific. That is a complete reading-and-maths week for both children.
  • Then add, as the routine holds: Reading.com (the scripted 20 minutes with an adult), Speech Blubs (speaking), Voice Dream (accessibility — do not skip this one for Donatello), Epic (reading volume) and Khan Academy (free Grade 4 maths).
  • All ten, R7 261 for year one. Two of the ten cost nothing, and one free library sits outside the ten.

The ten

Ordered by what to install first. Each card says the job it does, what it costs, and the one thing that could go wrong.

01

Reading Eggs (+ Fast Phonics + Reading Eggspress + Mathseeds)

Both children

The literacy spine — and the maths spine, if you only buy one thing.

One subscription gets you four children and four programmes. Fast Phonics is the part that matters for Donatello: systematic synthetic phonics built for ages 5–10, explicitly including older children who are struggling at school. Reading Eggspress (7–13) carries comprehension, and every book from Level 0–20 has a read-aloud function, so he can hear a fluent reading of anything he cannot decode yet. Gianna gets Reading Eggs proper (3–7). Mathseeds, the maths programme, is inside the same price.

  • ZA rating 4.4 · 782 ratings
  • 4 child profiles
  • 4 000+ e-books
  • R1 693/yr (US$99.99)

Buy it on the Black Friday yearly plan if it is still live: US$74.99 for the first year, R1 270 — R423 less.

02

Nessy Hairy Phonics 1, 2, 3 + Chimp Fu Syllables

Donatello

The exact sounds his reports say he cannot yet apply — for R180, once.

Not a subscription and not a programme: four small, sharp apps that drill the specific gaps on his file. Hairy Phonics 1 (free) covers the nine consonant digraphs — sh, ch, th, ck, ff, ll, ss, zz, ng. 2 covers the vowel digraphs — ai, ay, ee, ea, ie, oa, oi, oy, oo. 3 covers vowel-r and magic-e — ar, or, er, ir, ur, a-e, o-e, i-e, u-e, which is also the vowel work in his speech therapy. Chimp Fu teaches the six syllable types and chunking multi-syllable words, with progress saved for up to five players. No ads, no in-app purchases, works offline.

  • One-off purchase
  • Offline
  • No IAP
  • R179.97 total (HP1 free, 3 × R59.99)

Badged "designed for 4–6 year olds". The content is right for him; the look is young. Fine for ten minutes with a parent, awkward if his friends see it.

03

Reading.com — Learn to Read

Donatello

Twenty scripted minutes, adult and child, five days a week.

The one app on this list that is a lesson rather than a game. 120 phonics lessons, fully scripted so you do not need training to deliver them, plus 84 decodable digital storybooks, and it stops at about late-Grade-1 reading — which is roughly where he is. This is already named in his home reading plan as the fallback if the school's Term 1 programme is not systematic phonics. One subscription, three profiles.

  • 120 lessons
  • 84 decodable books
  • 15–20 min per lesson
  • R1 269/yr (US$74.94) · R212/mo

It only works if an adult sits down with him — that is the design, not a limitation. Listed for ages 3–8: he is nine, so it fits his reading age, not his birthday.

04

Voice Dream — Read Aloud

Donatello

Lets him read Grade 4 content today, at his comprehension level, not his decoding level.

Text-to-speech that highlights each word as it is spoken — the single most useful accommodation for dyslexia, and the one his June 2026 assessment recommends by name (text-to-speech, audiobooks, and not being asked to read aloud unprepared). It reads PDFs, Word documents, web pages, EPUBs and Bookshare books, it scans a paper worksheet with the camera, and the voices work offline. This is the app that stops every other subject from being blocked by his reading.

  • Word-by-word highlighting
  • Scans paper worksheets
  • Opens Bookshare books
  • R800/yr · R99.99/mo

Premium voices are R99.99 each on top. Start with the free built-in voices and one Acapela voice before buying more. Also check the iPad's own free Spoken Content setting first — it does a third of this job for nothing.

05

Epic — Kids' Books & Reading

Both children

Reading mileage: 40 000 books he can actually get through, on subjects he already loves.

Reading volume is what turns decoding into fluency, and this buys volume. Read-to-Me titles narrate with the words on screen, there are audiobooks and short non-fiction (science, animals, sport), and books download for offline use — useful in the car. Four child profiles, so Gianna gets her own shelf. For Donatello, set his level to his interest, not his reading age: a book about volcanoes he can listen to beats a baby book he can decode.

  • 40 000+ titles
  • Read-to-Me audio
  • Offline downloads
  • R139.99/mo or US$84.99/yr (R1 439)

This is the most expensive item per unit of learning on the list, and the first one to cut if the year gets tight — the free library below covers part of the same job.

06

Speech Blubs — Language Therapy

Both — Donatello first

The only app here that trains speaking and sentences rather than reading them.

Video-modelled sounds, vocabulary sets and sentence-building exercises, built for speech-language work and used by therapists. It fills the gap nothing else on this list touches: expressive language. His reports put expressive language below age even where reading comprehension is fine, and Gianna's grammar is still forming — so ten minutes of talking back to the iPad earns its place.

  • Video modelling
  • Vocabulary + sentences
  • ZA rating 4.5 · 372
  • R899.99/yr · R249.99/mo

Not a substitute for the weekly speech therapy he already has — it is the practice between sessions. Check with the therapist which sets to use.

07

Teach Your Monster to Read

Gianna

The one she will open without being asked.

A phonics game that follows phases 2–5 of Letters and Sounds, built with Roehampton University, with a monster she designs herself. Three levels, from letter sounds to reading sentences, and a progress screen for you. It is a genuine game first and a phonics scheme second — which is exactly why it works for a seven-year-old who is on track and needs volume rather than intervention.

  • One-off R199.99
  • No ads, no IAP
  • Offline
  • ZA rating 4.6 · 75

Nothing. This is the best-value item on the list — buy it today.

08

Khan Academy Kids

Gianna

A free, ad-free all-in-one for ages 2–8.

5 000+ activities across reading, phonics, writing, maths and social-emotional learning, built with the Stanford Graduate School of Education, standards-aligned, with read-aloud storybooks and non-fiction from National Geographic. Genuinely free forever, no ads, no subscription, no upsell — the safest thing you can hand a seven-year-old. It adapts to her level, which matters given she is a strong-average learner who gets bored by repetition.

  • Free forever
  • No ads, no accounts sold
  • Ages 2–8
  • ZA rating 4.8 · 1 504

Age ceiling is 8. Donatello is past it — do not put it on his iPad and call it schoolwork.

Matific — Maths Game

Both

CAPS-shaped maths that feels like a game, with the questions read aloud.

Aligned to the South African CAPS curriculum for Grades K–6, adaptive, and set on adventure islands with levels and treasure — it holds attention in a way drill apps do not. The detail that makes it the pick for this family: spoken audio prompts, so a child who cannot yet read the question can still answer it. That matters for Donatello in a maths context and for Gianna while her reading catches up.

  • CAPS-aligned K–6
  • Adaptive
  • Spoken prompts
  • R779.99/yr (R89.99/mo)

The ZA App Store yearly price (R779.99) is well below the R2 028 I estimated in the 15 November report from reseller quotes — buy in-app, not through a reseller.

Khan Academy

Donatello

Free, full Grade 4 maths and science for the child who is strong at both.

Where Matific is the game, this is the curriculum: every Grade 4 maths topic as video plus practice, mastery-tracked, with science alongside. It is free, has no ads, and it is the right place for a boy whose maths is ahead of his reading — the videos mean he is not blocked by text. Use it for the work the school sets and for the science he actually enjoys.

The free library that belongs with them

Not one of the ten, because it is a membership rather than a game — but it is the biggest free thing available to a dyslexic child in South Africa.

Bookshare Reader — free

Bookshare holds over half a million accessible titles — audio, large print, braille, EPUB — for people with a print disability, which includes dyslexia. Blind SA runs the South African membership, and it is free. Once the membership is open, the books open inside Bookshare Reader or inside Voice Dream, with word highlighting. This is how he gets the Grade 4 class readers and the Dog Man he wants, in a form he can decode.

Bookshare Reader · App Store Blind SA Bookshare membership Bookshare International

What he needs to apply: proof of the dyslexia diagnosis (the ed-psych report is enough) and a completed print-disability declaration. Worth one email this week.

What not to install

  • Brain-training apps (Lumosity, Cogmed, Elevate, "brain games"). The largest review of the field found no convincing evidence that they improve real-world cognitive function beyond the tasks themselves. Simons et al., 2016, Psychological Science in the Public Interest. The "brain functionality" you want comes from structured literacy, sleep, movement and vision correction — not from puzzles on a screen.
  • Prodigy Math. It works, and it is engineered to make children ask you for the paid membership. The gate scored it 0.01 once the upsell was in the description.
  • IXL at R2 388 a year. Excellent drill, but its read-aloud support stops around Grade 2, so at Grade 4 it is a wall of text for him — the opposite of what he needs.
  • ABCmouse, ABC Kids and the rest of the icon-farm apps. Free-to-download, ad-riddled, no sequence, no progress. They teach children to tap, not to read.
  • YouTube and YouTube Kids as "learning". The algorithm will take over the session within ten minutes.

Five things to set on both iPads before you hand them over

  • Family Sharing with Ask to Buy on. All subscriptions on your Apple ID; the children never see a price.
  • Guided Access (Settings → Accessibility) for the ones that must not be wandered away from — Khan Academy especially.
  • Screen Time limits per app, not per device: 40 minutes total on school days is the shape above.
  • Home Screen folder called "School", with only the seven learning apps in it, on the first screen. Everything else goes on page two.
  • Large text and Display Zoom on both — his convergence weakness and her high fixation count both make small text expensive.

Two honest caveats

The apps are the practice, not the treatment. Donatello's reading moved from 58 to 90 words a minute in seven weeks on a tutor-led programme, not on an app. What these ten do is give the same instruction daily, in a form he will accept, and give him a way to read Grade 4 content before his decoding catches up. If the choice is ever between a subscription and a session with the reading teacher, take the session.

Vision before volume. His convergence weakness is still on the file and still untreated as far as the record shows. Two years of reading intervention on top of an untreated binocular problem is a race uphill — no app fixes that, and every one of these will be more tiring until it is dealt with.
