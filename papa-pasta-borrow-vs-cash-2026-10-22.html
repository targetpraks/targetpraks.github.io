1 · Executive summary

The short answer

Your plan works as a cash-flow story and fails as an economic one. The interest on R7.5m at 7.75% (R48,438 a month) does cover the interest on R2.5m at 12.75% (R26,563 a month) — and that is the trap: it covers it only because the deposit is three times the loan. On the money that is actually borrowed, you are paying 12.75% to earn 7.75%.

  • The spread is negative at every realistic price. A bank will lend a new business somewhere between prime and prime + 7. On R2.5m outstanding that is a guaranteed loss of R75,000 to R250,000 a year before tax. There is no rate at which a 7.75% deposit beats a business loan — break-even would need a loan at 23.25%, which is 12.5 percentage points above prime.
  • Paying cash for the shop is worth R75,335 a year after tax at the base case (loan at prime + 2, deposit at 7.75%). Over the full 60 months the gap narrows to R78,651 because the loan amortises and the larger deposit compounds — it is a close call on arithmetic, not an obvious win for debt.
  • The leverage is not ruinous — it is expensive in the wrong currency. It costs R78,651 of net worth over five years, about R15,730 a year, and it buys R2.5m of liquidity. An undrawn facility buys the same access for R12,500–R37,500 a year in fees. The costs are comparable; the obligations are not. One is a fee you can cancel, the other is R56,563 a month for 60 months.
  • The strategy only turns positive if the loan is priced at prime flat and the deposit is locked at 8.28% (Investec's 12-month prime-linked fixed deposit). That combination is worth R265,766 over five years — but prime flat for a business with no trading history is not a realistic quote, and the deposit is locked.
  • South Africa has no true offset account on a business loan. The working equivalents are an access bond (which saves the bond rate tax-free) and a revolving credit facility (interest only on the drawn balance). Both beat a notice account, and neither requires you to borrow R2.5m you do not need.
Recommendation

Pay for the shop from the R7.5m. Leave R5.0m invested as a ladder (R1.5m same-day, R2.0m 32-day notice, R1.5m 32-day with 40% on demand — 7.55% blended, R377,600 a year gross), and arrange a R2.0–2.5m revolving credit or access facility that stays undrawn as the liquidity backstop. Borrow only the working-capital buffer, and only once the shop's own cash flow has proved itself for two quarters. This is not a case against leverage — §7 prices the version of it that builds.

2 · The benchmark

Prime is 10.75%, and it is no longer a safe assumption

The SARB raised the repo rate 25bps to 7.25% on 23 September 2026, taking prime to 10.75% with effect from 25 September 2026. Prime is fixed at repo + 3.5 points. Every business loan you will be quoted is expressed as a margin over that number, and almost every business loan reprices the day prime moves.

A structural risk most borrowers have not priced

In February 2026 the SARB published a discussion paper proposing that prime stop being the reference rate for lending, replacing it with the policy rate. If that happens, a "prime + 2" loan is repriced against a different benchmark and the margin you negotiated may not survive the transition. Ask any bank you approach for the replacement-benchmark clause in writing before you sign a five-year prime-linked facility.

3 · The borrowing side

R2.5m for a business with no trading history is the hard part

South African banks do not publish business loan rates — they are risk-priced per deal. What they do publish is the structure, and the structure is what decides whether you qualify at all. The two published constraints that matter here:

  • Standard Bank's Business Term Loan requires the business to be trading for two years or more with an active business account, before it will even price the deal. Monthly service fee R69–R290; once-off initiation R74.75–R3,775; term 36 to 60 months; interest linked to prime.
  • Capitec's Business Term Loan likewise needs a minimum of two years' operating history, and its business credit facilities run to about R500,000 on turnover.

Papa Pasta, at the point of purchase, has neither history nor a track record of turnover. That pushes the file towards one of three doors: security (property or a personal surety), a state-backed lender, or an equity-style partner. The rate you get is a function of which door you walk through, not of which bank you like.

Two constraints that will decide the file before the rate does

The NCA still applies to you. A juristic person with turnover or assets under R1m is a "consumer" under the National Credit Act, which means the bank must run an affordability assessment on the business — a business that has not traded yet. This is the single most common reason a start-up acquisition file dies, and it is not a pricing problem. A home-loan access facility sidesteps it entirely because the affordability test is yours, not the shop's.

Interest deductibility follows the borrower, not the purpose. If you borrow personally to buy shares in the company that owns Papa Pasta, section 24O of the Income Tax Act can deny the interest deduction outright. Borrow in the entity that will own and run the business, and get the structure signed off before you sign the loan.

5 · The offset question

South Africa has no true offset account. It has three things that do the same job.

In Australia, an offset account is a transaction account welded to a loan: every rand in it reduces the loan balance on which interest is charged, daily, and the money stays yours to spend. South African retail and business banks do not sell that product. What they sell instead is close enough to matter, and one of the three is better than anything on the deposit table.

1 · Access bond / flexi facility

Absa FlexiReserve, Standard Bank AccessBond, FNB Flexi Bond. Extra money paid into the home loan reduces the balance it earns interest against, and you can draw it back. Standard Bank's version explicitly links the bond to your business account, not just your personal account.

This is the closest SA product to a true offset, and it is only available against residential property.

2 · Revolving credit facility

A committed line on the business account. Interest is charged only on the drawn balance, so every rand of surplus cash sitting in the account reduces the interest base. Repay and redraw as often as you like, subject to an annual review.

The business equivalent of an offset — but short-dated and repriced every year.

3 · Cash sweep / notional pooling

Where several accounts or entities are set off against each other so that credit balances cancel debit balances for interest purposes. Real, but negotiated at corporate scale, not sold to a single small company.

Not available at R2.5m. Do not let a banker sell it to you as if it were.

Why the access bond beats every deposit on the table

Interest you save is not interest you earn. R2.5m parked against a bond priced at 11.25% saves R281,250 a year, tax-free, because no income arises. The same R2.5m in a 7.75% notice account earns R193,750 and is taxed — R106,563 in your hands at 45%.

The structure that actually matches your intent

You want three things: the shop paid for, the cash still reachable, and no interest bleeding out. That is not a loan-plus-deposit. It is: pay cash, keep the operating buffer in a same-day tranche or an access bond, and hold an undrawn revolving facility for the rest if the bank will give you one on relationship terms. A committed facility costs roughly 0.5%–1.5% a year on the undrawn portion — R12,500 to R37,500 against R296,949 of interest in year one on the drawn loan. Comparable money over five years; nothing like comparable risk.

6 · The arithmetic

The offset calculation, run honestly

Base case: R2.5m borrowed over 60 months at prime + 2 (12.75%), R7.5m invested at 7.75% on 32-day notice, both taxed at the company rate of 27%.

What you receive

R48,438 a month

Interest on R7.5m at 7.75%. After 27% tax: R35,359.

What you pay

R26,563 a month

Interest on R2.5m at 12.75% in month one. After the 27% deduction: R19,391.

The simple offset calculation you asked for

R48,438 received − R26,563 paid = R21,875 a month gross, or R15,969 after tax. The investment covers the loan interest 1.82 times over — and it would still cover it if the loan were priced at 23.25%, because the deposit is three times the debt.

That is not a return. It is the arithmetic of holding three rands of cash for every one rand of debt. The number that matters is the next one.

The same decision measured against paying cash

If you pay for the shop outright, you invest R5.0m at 7.75%: R32,292 a month gross, R23,573 after tax. The leveraged plan nets R15,969 after tax. Paying cash is worth R75,335 a year more — and it carries no R56,563 monthly obligation and no rate risk.

Over the full term, the picture softens — and then flips on pricing

Because the loan amortises, the average debt is about R1.35m over five years while the full deposit keeps compounding. That narrows the gap considerably: full leverage at prime + 2 ends five years roughly R78,651 behind paying cash, not the R625,000 a naive spread calculation would suggest. Get the loan to prime flat and lock 8.28% for twelve months, and leverage is R265,766 ahead.

One more number worth knowing

If you have a residential bond with R2.5m of headroom, the comparison changes completely. Borrowing the R2.5m from the bond at 11.25% and leaving the R7.5m invested still loses money — 11.25% against 7.75% taxed is worse than the business loan, not better. But parking surplus cash against the bond is the best after-tax "return" available to you in South Africa, at an effective 20.5% pre-tax equivalent. The bond is a place to keep cash, not a place to borrow from for this deal.

7 · The OPM case

Using other people's money to build — the version that works

Your instinct is right, and the recommendation above is narrower than it reads. Leverage is how you build. But the test is not loan rate against deposit rate — it is loan rate against the return on the asset the loan buys. Borrowing to hold cash fails that test at every rate. Borrowing to open a second shop passes it at almost any rate, because the shop earns on R2.5m of capital while the loan is paid down.

Same R7.5m, same shops, three structures. The pool always keeps R5.0m at 7.55%; the only variable is how many shops the bank's money buys.

Five-year cumulative profit after tax at 27%, each shop earning the same return on its R2.5m of capital, 60-month amortising debt charged to the shop it buys, R5.0m pool earning 7.55% in every column. Capex is treated as a retained asset, not an expense.

Two break-evens, and they are 20 points apart

Profit break-even is 7.2%. Because the loan amortises, the average balance is about half the principal, so the interest drag is only 7.2% of the shop's capital a year. Any shop earning more than that adds profit for every rand the bank puts in.
Cash-flow break-even is 27.2%. The shop also has to fund the principal, so it needs to throw off R678,759 a year before the instalments pay for themselves. At a realistic independent-shop return of 15–20% it does not, and the group carries R178,759–R303,759 a year while the shop builds value. The pool's R377,500 of income covers that — which is exactly why the pool must stay intact and unpledged.

The sequence that makes OPM work

  1. Open shop one for cash. Not as a concession to caution — as the pilot that produces the only thing a bank actually prices: a trading record. No surety, no R56,563 a month, no rate risk while you learn the site.
  2. Two quarters of real trading before you ask for a rand. That is worth one to two points on the margin and it usually removes the personal surety. It is the cheapest interest-rate improvement available to you, and it costs nothing but time.
  3. Then borrow for shop two, against shop two. Prime + 1 or better, 60 months, no early-settlement penalty, secured on the second site's cash flow rather than on your house.
  4. Repeat on the same terms. Three shops at 20% return is R5,658,963 of five-year profit against R3,202,875 for one — the bank's R5.0m of debt buys R2,456,088 of extra profit. That is what the money is for.
  5. Never borrow the cushion. R2.5m borrowed to sit in a notice account is the one structure where the bank's money earns less than it costs, and no number of shops fixes it.

That is the difference between leverage and a loan: debt against a proven unit, priced below that unit's return, repeated. It starts one year later than the plan you had, and it is the only version of it that compounds.

8 · Risks and the decision

What breaks this plan

1 · Rate risk runs one way for you

A prime-linked loan reprices instantly when prime moves; a 32-day notice deposit reprices too, but the margin stays negative. A 200bps rise takes the loan to 14.75% and widens the gap from 5 to 7 points — R50,000 a year more, on top of the loss you already carry. And the SARB is actively trying to replace prime as the benchmark altogether.

2 · The obligation is fixed, the shop's income is not

R56,563 a month for 60 months, starting in month one, from a business that has never traded. Miss it and the surety is called against your personal balance sheet — the same R7.5m you were protecting. The strategy converts liquid cash into a fixed claim on an unproven business.

3 · The approval may not exist

R2.5m unsecured for a start-up is a thin file at any bank: two years' trading is the published floor on the standard term loans, and the NCA affordability test applies to the new entity. If the file is declined, the plan fails at the counter rather than in the spreadsheet.

4 · Tax eats the difference silently

Interest earned is taxed; interest saved is not. At 45% the deposit nets 4.26% against a 9.31% after-tax loan cost. Investing the R7.5m personally while the company borrows is the worst combination — check the ownership of both sides before you sign anything.

5 · The purchase price is not the full price

Confirm whether R2.5m includes VAT. A sale that does not qualify as a going concern attracts 15% VAT — up to R375,000 on this ticket — and any property component attracts transfer duty. Size the finance after those numbers, not before. Interest, VAT treatment and s24O are your accountant's call, not mine.

6 · The deposit is not insured, and it is not locked

CODI covers R100,000 per depositor per bank. The 7.75% is a floating rate, not a guarantee: if the MPC cuts, your income falls while the loan margin stays. The only way to fix both sides is a prime-linked deposit against a prime-linked loan — which fixes the spread, not the risk.

The recommendation

Pay for Papa Pasta from the R7.5m. Do not borrow R2.5m in order to keep R7.5m earning 7.75% — the spread is negative, the interest is taxable, and the only thing the structure buys you is the illusion of liquidity that an undrawn facility gives you for a fee you can cancel.

  1. Fund the purchase in cash, after your accountant confirms the VAT and transfer-duty treatment. Keep the remaining R5.0m invested.
  2. Ladder what is left: R1,500,000 same-day money market at 7.29%, R2,000,000 32-day notice at 7.75%, R1,500,000 32-day with 40% on demand at 7.55%. Blended 7.55% → R377,600 a year, R31,467 a month gross (R275,648 after 27% tax). Split across at least three institutions.
  3. Ask for a revolving credit or access facility and leave it undrawn. If it is priced at R12,500–R37,500 a year in commitment fees it is worth having as a backstop; if the bank wants more, the same-day money market tranche in the ladder is already your liquidity and the facility is optional. Either way: cancellable beats committed.
  4. If you still want leverage, only take it at prime + 1 or better, fixed or capped for three years, with no early-settlement penalty — and only against a matched prime-linked deposit so the spread is locked rather than hoped for. At prime + 2 the leverage costs you money; at prime + 4.5 it costs R273,060 over five years.
  5. If a bank will not lend without a personal surety, the answer is the state-backed route: SEDFA or Business Partners, both of which exist precisely for a viable business with no trading history. Apply there before accepting a 15%+ quote from a bank or a 35% quote from a fintech.

The one-line version: the R7.5m should not be paying the loan's interest — it should be replacing the loan.
