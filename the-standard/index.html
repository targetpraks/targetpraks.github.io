Page 1 of 5 — what this is

Eleven weeks, one standard, nothing soft and nothing dishonest

You asked for weekly motivational posts with a GIF, a cartoon theme, voice notes, real stories or proven methods, and a plan to the end of the year, pre-built in Zoho Connect. It is built. Everything sits in AI-Drive/Agent-Output/batman/campaign-the-standard/ and is staged for Connect.

What is finished

  • 11 Monday posts, written and dated 19 Oct → 28 Dec.
  • 11 cartoon frames — one character, one style, rendered from the canonical identity reference.
  • 11 animated cards (the GIF) plus 11 print-quality stills.
  • 6 voice notes recorded; the last 5 when the audio quota resets tonight.
  • Each week: one quote, one real story, one proven method with its honest limits, one drill, one win prompt.

What I will not do without you

  • Publish a single one of them. Every post is permanent and goes to all staff.
  • Sign your name to words you did not write and did not record.
  • Publish toughen up / weak emotions in any form. The evidence says it costs you bad news, and bad news is the one thing a distribution business cannot hear late. Substitutes are on page 2.

The rules every post obeys

  • Demand plus a method. No "push harder" line without a named technique and a drill you can finish by Friday.
  • Real or nothing. Every story is a dated, checkable event; every method is a real study with its failed replications and limits stated next to it.
  • Plain words. Short sentences, no borrowed idioms, roughly 150 words, no jargon, no lecture.
  • No shaming, ever. No ranking, no comparing people, no "real ones don't feel it", nothing that tells someone how to feel about themselves.
  • Wins rotate. Warehouse, admin, sales, marketing, junior office — not only sales wins, or the warehouse learns the campaign is not for them.
  • A way to answer back. Every post carries a human contact line and gets answered within 24 hours.
Page 2 of 5 — the three decisions

Three decisions, then it ships

The council was blunt about one thing: a permanent, staff-wide, one-way motivational post is a noticeboard, not a culture. These three decisions fix that. Everything else is done.

1 · Whose name is on it

Connect posts from your account, so the byline is yours unless we give the campaign its own identity. Three options:

OptionWhat it costsWhat it buys
A. A dedicated Connect identity — "The Standard" / Culture Desk (recommended) One user and a little admin. No money. Attribution matches authorship. Posts can be answered, commented on and kept as their own thread without your personal account carrying eleven permanent notices.
B. Your account, signed honestly — every post closes "written and voiced by Bruce Wayne, chief of staff" Nothing. Works today. Ships Monday. Staff already know the AI chief of staff exists, so nothing is disguised; it just lives in your feed.
C. Your byline, your pen and your voice You write and record all eleven yourself. The strongest version of all — but only honest if you actually author them. My drafts become your drafts.

2 · The words we cut, and what replaces them

You saidWe publishWhy
"culture of a strong competitive team"Kept verbatim. Campaign tagline.It is your line and it is the spine of the arc.
"focus on wins"Kept verbatim, and defined as "anything we would want repeated" — flagging a problem early counts as a win.Otherwise only the loud wins get claimed and the quiet fixes vanish.
"get stronger and do better"Kept only in the same post as a named drill.Alone it is an exhortation with no method.
"if you have weak emotions, toughen up"Never published. Nearest safe line: "Hard weeks come to everyone. Strength is what you do next." It converts struggling into a personal defect, and a floor that believes that stops reporting errors, near-misses and late deliveries. The cost is silence, and silence is expensive.
"we don't teach weak things""Everything we teach here gets tested in real work."Same intent, without telling anyone that asking for help is weak.

3 · The two controls that make it survive contact with a bad week

  • A front-line pre-reader. One warehouse or junior admin colleague reads each post before it goes live and answers one question: "If the person having their worst month read this, would they feel attacked?" Hesitation means rewrite. Ten minutes a week, and the only control that catches harm before it is permanent. Tell me who, and I will route the drafts to them.
  • A pause rule. No culture post in a week where the company has an unresolved staff problem. A motivational post in the same week as an ignored complaint turns the whole campaign into the company joke — and December is both the busiest and the most fragile month.

What I need from you, in one line each

  • Byline: A, B or C.
  • Contact line: the person and number that goes at the foot of every post.
  • Pre-reader: the name of one front-line colleague.
  • Then: "go", and the eleven posts go into Connect, scheduled Mondays 07:00.

Week 1 — Own what you control

“There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power.”Epictetus, Enchiridion §1 · trans. Elizabeth Carter, 1758
The storyOn Robben Island the state owned Nelson Mandela's hours, his letters and the glare of the lime quarry. It never owned his timetable: fixed exercise in his cell, law studies by correspondence, discipline the warders could not reach. He walked out on 11 February 1990 and took the oath four years later.
The methodThe control split. Two thousand years old, and it is the spine of cognitive behavioural therapy today. Two columns: mine, not mine. Work only column one.
The drillTomorrow: take your five biggest problems, split them mine / not mine, and work the mine column before your first break.
Win promptWhat moved on your mine list this week?
Voice note scriptMonday, team. Eleven Mondays, and this year is done. So we start with the only split that matters. Mine, not mine. The rand's mood? Not mine. A buyer's temper? Not mine. Busier than ever? That is column two's favourite excuse. Your first touch on the machine? Mine. Your words in a meeting? Mine. The extra five minutes on a job with your name on it? Mine. The Stoics wrote that down two thousand years ago and it has not failed since. And understand this: no one in this company will ever be shamed for having feelings. Feelings are weather. Work is ground. A strong person walks through weather on solid ground. Tonight: two columns. Hands on column one before anyone asks you a question. That is the whole method. Go start column one.

Week 2 — Name the heat early

“Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them.”Epictetus, Enchiridion §5 · Carter / Long translation
The story13 April 1970, Apollo 13, an oxygen tank explodes 330,000 km from home. There is no panic on the radio. The fault is named in plain words, then the checklists start, and three men come home on 17 April.
The methodName it and it shrinks. In brain scans, putting a feeling into a few plain words lowers the threat response. Hiding the feeling does the opposite: it raises the body's stress load and costs memory. Name it, don't swallow it.
The drillOnce a shift, name your strongest feeling in one short sentence — then take the next physical action.
Win promptWhich feeling did you name before it turned on you?
Voice note scriptWeek two. Short one. Everybody in this business has weather upstairs. Anger. Fear. The tiredness that starts talking on Wednesdays. I will not insult you by pretending otherwise, and I will not insult you by pretending the weather decides your shift. The trick is old and measured. Say the name. Two words to yourself. This is anger. This is fear. That cuts it down to size. Pretending it is not there costs more, and that is measured too. So the strong move was never silence. The strong move is the name, then the hands. On Apollo 13 the fault was said out loud before a single line of it was solved. Same rule on the floor. Name it in one sentence, then take the next action. Feelings are information. They are not management. Now go run your shift.

Week 3 — Plan for the punch

“Everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth.”Mike Tyson, 2002
The story15 January 2009, US Airways 1549 loses both engines over New York. Captain Sullenberger has about three and a half minutes of glide and 155 people behind him. He does not invent the answer in the air — years of drills had already written it.
The methodIf-then plans. One trigger, one action, written before you need it: "If a client says no on price, I move to option two." It is one of the best-replicated simple tools in psychology. It fails only when the if is vague.
The drillWrite one if-then line for this week's hardest moment. Say it once out loud. Use it.
Win promptWhere did your if-then hold this week?
Voice note scriptWeek three. I have watched good plans die in about nine seconds. Not because the plan was wrong. Because it was written for a calm day. A plan that only works when everything goes right is a hope with a title. Here is the fix, and it is boring on purpose. If, then. One line. If the buyer says no on price, I move to option two. Not to a mood. If the line floods at three, I call X first, not my feelings first. Write it before you need it. Say it once out loud. Then forget it, because it now lives in your reflexes. Sullenberger did not invent his answer over the Hudson in 2009. He had decided years earlier. Same standard for us. Write the hinge. Take the hit. Keep working.

Week 4 — Targets you can tick

“No human is limited.”Eliud Kipchoge
The story18 July 1976, Montreal. Nadia Comaneci, fourteen years old, scores the first perfect ten in Olympic gymnastics history. The scoreboard had never been built for a ten and printed 1.00. Behind it: thousands of counted repetitions in an ordinary gym.
The methodChase what you own. Specific, hard, accepted goals beat "do your best" across hundreds of studies — with one caveat: a target handed down and never signed on to loses its force. So keep three, and make them process-shaped.
The drillThree process targets for the week — tick each daily, review Friday. Hit at least two.
Win promptWhich daily tick survived even your worst day?
Voice note scriptWeek four. Let us kill a comfortable word: outcome. It worked out is how some people describe a season they never touched. Not here. Here we count what we can touch. The first hour done properly. Ten client touches before ten. The stock action done before lunch. You cannot tick an outcome. You can tick an hour. Comaneci's ten came from thousands of counted reps, not from a good day. Three things per person, ticked daily, adjusted Friday. Small. Boring. In your hands. The scoreboards of this business only print what somebody ticked first. Decide your three. Tick them when it is dull. Especially when it is dull. Now go make the day's ticks.

Week 5 — Do the hard rep

“It is not because things are difficult that we do not dare; it is because we do not dare that things are difficult.”Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 104
The story12 October 2019, Vienna. Eliud Kipchoge runs a marathon in 1:59:40 — the first human under two hours. Not an official record; he and his team were open about the pacing and the shoes. What is official is the decade of slow designed kilometres behind it.
The methodDeliberate practice. Skill is built, not handed over: one rep slightly past comfortable, with a check you can see. The honest part — raw hours alone explain far less of skill than the myth claims. Hours without design are attendance.
The drillPick one skill that currently costs you. Fifteen honest minutes of hard reps, every shift this week.
Win promptWhich rep did you dread at 07:00 and finish anyway?
Voice note scriptWeek five. I am tired of the word talent. Skill gets built, it does not get handed over. Seneca, two thousand years back: we do not dare because things are difficult, when in truth things are difficult because we do not dare. Read it twice. Difficulty is mostly a queue of untested reps. In 2019 Kipchoge ran a marathon under two hours. Not an official record, and he said so himself. What nobody argues with is the decade of designed practice behind it. A plain camp, shared chores, slow kilometres nobody films. The research calls it deliberate practice, and it also says raw hours alone explain far less than the myth claims. Design beats attendance. Everything we teach in this series gets tested in real work. Pick the skill that costs you: the call you route to somebody else, the job you queue behind. Fifteen minutes a shift, five shifts. Not a motivation. A schedule. Go do the boring rep.

Week 6 — Run it in your head

“I never hit a shot, not even in practice, without having a very sharp, in-focus picture of it in my head.”Jack Nicklaus, Golf My Way, 1974
The story13 August 2008, Beijing. Michael Phelps's goggles fill with water in the 200 m butterfly final. He swims the last lap effectively blind and still wins in a world record. His coach had made him run every race as a quiet mental film, stroke by stroke, for years.
The methodRehearse the work, not the trophy. Thirty seconds, eyes closed, run the task start to finish — obstacles included. A perfect film with no problems in it is no film. Nothing replaces the real rep; imagery just means the real one is a rerun.
The drillBefore each daily rush: thirty seconds, eyes closed, run it once. Tonight, correct the film in one line.
Win promptWhat did you see in your head before it happened?
Voice note scriptWeek six. Big week ahead, and it will not negotiate. So we do not hope our way through it. We rehearse it. Thirty seconds a day, eyes closed. Run the rush in your own eyes. The door opens. The first wave lands. Where the bottleneck forms. What you do at the bottleneck. Obstacles included, because a perfect film with no problems is no film at all. Phelps's coach built him a small cinema in his head before Beijing in 2008. When the goggles filled, there was nothing new to invent. The race had already been seen a thousand times. Same for us. Friday at nine should feel like a rerun. Do the mental run today. Correct it tonight. Run the real one Friday. Then tell me what you saw before it happened. Now go run it once.

Week 7 — Break it on paper

“What stands in the way becomes the way.”Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.20 · Long translation, 1862
The storyIn a 1989 experiment, people told to imagine a project had already failed produced about a third more real reasons for failure than people simply asked to list risks. Gary Klein turned that into the pre-mortem in Harvard Business Review in 2007.
The methodBefore the big job, everyone writes one page: it is months later and this failed — why. Read it aloud. No blame, no defending. Then fix the top causes before Monday. Break the plan publicly, fix it quietly.
The drillBefore Friday's big job: three ways it fails, on paper. Fix the top one on Monday.
Win promptWhat did you break on paper before it broke for real?
Voice note scriptWeek seven. I used to love one phrase: we will sort it out when it breaks. Let me tell you what that phrase costs a yard. Friday hours. Double the stress. Half the credit. So we change the language. Before the big job every one of us writes one page of failure history. It failed. Here is how. No softening. On paper. Researchers found people who did that spotted roughly a third more real problems than people just listing risks. Klein built the pre-mortem on it. Now the part that takes spine: reading your own failure story out loud, and somebody else's, without one word of blame. Blame makes people lie in the review and bleed in the yard. No blame. Break the plan publicly. Fix the top causes before Monday. Go break something on paper.

Week 8 — Review like pros

The storyThe Springboks beat England 32-12 in 2019, and in 2023 they won three knockout matches by a single point each: 29-28, 16-15, 12-11. Nobody collects one-point titles on vibes. That margin is built in the review room.
The methodThe after-action review, straight out of military doctrine. Ten minutes, four questions: what was planned, what happened, why, what changes Monday. One rule — no blame, or people stop telling the truth and the review dies.
The drillThis Friday: ten minutes, your team, four questions, one written change for Monday. Let the newest person explain their win first.
Win promptWhose win taught you something this week?
Voice note scriptWeek eight. A question for you. What do you actually do with a win? Because here is what I have watched for years. The win goes into a message. Somebody claps. And the knowledge inside it evaporates by Monday. That is waste. A champion's win is raw material. The Army worked this out in its training camps: review it while it is still warm. Four questions. What was the plan? What happened? Why? What changes by Monday? And one rule, which I mean. No blame. No names. Process only. The moment one person gets shamed in that room, the truth leaves the room and the review dies with it. Let the newest person explain their win first. Men learn while they teach, said Seneca, two thousand years ago. We do not clap and move on here. We harvest. Ten minutes, Friday. Carry the change into Monday. Now go book those ten minutes.

Week 9 — Reset in sixty seconds

voice note recording when the audio quota resets tonight — script is final and sits in the voice column of this block.

“Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone.”Alan Watts
The storyJune 2018, Thailand. Twelve boys and their coach are sealed in the Tham Luang caves for more than a week. They are found on 2 July and brought out over three days. All thirteen survived. Their coach, a former monk, kept them still, quiet and breathing steadily in the dark.
The methodThe sixty-second reset: two short inhales through the nose, one long complete exhale through the mouth, three times. A 2023 Stanford-led trial found five minutes a day of this beat box breathing and mindfulness for steady mood and lower physical arousal. Small study, honestly early. It costs you a minute.
The drillUnder pressure: two quick inhales, one long exhale. Three times a day this week.
Win promptWhere did sixty seconds of breathing save your afternoon?
Voice note scriptWeek nine. December, and everybody is running hot. Here is the truth about the panic that hits you at the peak. It is not a character flaw. It is a breathing pattern. You can beat it in under a minute, and it has now been measured. Two short inhales. One long exhale. Three times. That deep stretch in the lung does what shouting cannot. That is not meditation for soft people. That is system control: the same family as what a fighter does between rounds, and what those Thai boys did in the dark. Nobody here needs to perform being rattled, and nobody needs to pretend they feel nothing. You feel it. You take the minute. You come back with your voice an octave lower. Sixty seconds, three times a day, all week. Now breathe once and go count the stock.

Week 10 — Out-wait the urge

voice note recording when the audio quota resets tonight — script is final and sits in the voice column of this block.

“Fall seven times, stand up eight.”Japanese proverb — 七転び八起き
The storyBruce Fordyce won the Comrades Marathon nine times between 1981 and 1990. Over ninety kilometres the killer is the urge to go too fast too early. His trademark was the opposite: hold yourself down while fresh rivals charge, then reel them in over the second half. He won by refusing that urge, again and again, for a decade.
The methodThe two-minute hold. Feel the urge — to snap, to rush, to shortcut — and do not answer from inside it. Eyes away, ten steps, mouth closed, hold two minutes. Then choose once, on purpose. The waiting skill is trainable at any age; the old nursery test never decided anybody's life.
The drillUrge to snap, rush or shortcut? Hold two minutes: walk, breathe, then choose.
Win promptWhat urge did you out-wait this week?
Voice note scriptWeek ten. Christmas is close. Shops are stripped and the noise at home can be louder than at work. That is the real test this week, and I will not pretend it is easy for everybody. So one tool. The two-minute hold. The urge arrives: to snap at the till, to shortcut the count, to fire one remark across the table. You do not argue with the urge. You time it. Eyes away. Ten steps. Two full minutes. Then choose. The old Stanford children who beat the treat did not out-muscle it. They looked away.
