02

Their model cannot produce a regular

In the reel, a guest comes back with a fixed probability at every visit. That is a geometric distribution, and it has one shape: each step is a fixed fraction of the last.

Out of 100 first-timers, at the same two dials: 35% come back, 45% of those come a third time.

The second visit is a decision. The fourth is a habit. A coin cannot tell the difference, so it forces the third-visit rate to equal the second, and then compounds the error down the ladder.

Their model says 1.5 of every 100 first-timers ever eat with you a fifth time. Every restaurant owner knows a dozen people who come every Thursday. That long tail is the regulars, and in this engine it is 12.1 of 100, averaging 4.15 visits each against their 1.54.

The difference is not cosmetic. At R501 a first-timer instead of R267, every downstream decision — what a promotion may cost, whether a loyalty card pays for itself, what the business is worth — moves with it.

03

Three things one coin cannot say

Each of these is a decision a restaurant owner actually makes. None of them can be answered by a single chance-to-return.

The third visit is not the second

R3.03m → R4.36m

Give the engine two numbers instead of one. At a 20% third-visit rate the room settles at 336 covers a week; at 60%, 483. That is R1.32m a year of contribution, from a dial their model does not have.

A full room is not a healthy room

77 a week

at 200 new guests a week, 77 people are told there is no table — while crowding has already cut every guest's chance of returning to 67% of what it was. Past about 160 new guests a week the only honest move is more seats, not more ads.

What a point of retention is worth

Not “retention matters”. A number, at your size, that you can put against a price.

Marginal value of one percentage point of second-visit rate, starting from each level.

The early points are worth ten times the late ones: about R135k a year when you are at 15%, falling to R11k at 47.5%. Not because retention stops mattering, but because by then the room is full and the next gain has to come from seats.

05

Papa Pasta: two numbers from the till

Everything above is a typical restaurant. Two numbers off your own system make it yours.

  1. Of the guests who ate with you for the first time in a month, how many came back within 90 days? Most POS and booking systems can answer this if a phone number or card is captured, in an afternoon.
  2. Of those, how many came a third time? This is the number that separates a book from a queue.

Add average spend and margin — both already on the P&L — and the engine above is calibrated to Papa Pasta instead of to an assumption.

Then it decides four things

  • How the next rand of marketing splits between filling the door and bringing people back.
  • Whether to add covers, add seats, or change the service so the room can run hotter without the quality penalty.
  • Whether a loyalty programme's discount pays for itself — its cost against the points it moves.
  • What a regular is worth when the business is sold, franchised or borrowed against.

Model and assumptions

Weekly discrete-event simulation, written and run on this machine. Each guest carries a state: first visit, returned once, returned twice, habitual, lapsed. A first-timer gets six weeks to come back, a second-timer four. On the third visit a guest draws a visit rate: 35% become habitual (0.45/week on average), the rest occasional (0.06/week). Crowding above 85% of capacity cuts every return probability — that is the only penalty, and it is an assumption, not a measurement. Returning guests are seated before new ones, so a full room turns arrivals away. All figures are the average of 7 runs on different seeds over weeks 105–156, with the same seed producing identical output.

These are a typical restaurant's numbers, not Papa Pasta's. The structure is the claim; the levels are the assumption.
