01 / 16The answer

It works — if the royalty moves to five per cent and two of them go to the zone

The zone layer is the right structure for a 96-zone network. One point of turnover is not enough to pay for it and two points carved out of a three per cent royalty guts head office. Move the royalty to five and carve two: the store pays two extra points of turnover, Papa Pasta keeps every rand it keeps today, and the zone owner gets a business he can sell.

Put a regional owner between Papa Pasta and the store operator and three things happen at once: the operator's entry ticket falls by a quarter, head office stops employing regional managers, and somebody with capital and local presence starts selling stores for you. That is the prize, and it is real.

The rate is where it breaks. A zone owner who buys 25% of five stores, collects one point of turnover and runs the zone properly spends R3 375 000 to earn R252 360 a year — 7.5% on his money, before he has paid himself a salary. One and a half points gets him to 11.0%. Two points carved from a five per cent royalty gets him to 14.5% and costs head office nothing, because the fourth and fifth points never belonged to it.

Four findings you can act on

1
The zone needs four stores before it is a business

At one and two stores the zone owner loses money every year, because the zone's own management cost — R936,000 a year at three stores — is bigger than what three stores hand him. Three stores leaves him R62 280: break-even and nothing else. Four pays R275 040; five is the first size worth selling. Anything below five is a franchisee with extra paperwork.

2
The seventh store is a cliff, not a step

A second zone manager costs R540,000 a year and arrives at store seven. Going from six stores to seven takes the zone owner's cash from R418 032 to R55 704 — backwards. Zones should be drawn at six or at ten. Seven, eight and nine are the worst sizes on the page.

3
The layer has to be paid for by a royalty rise, not by a raid on the existing one

One point carved out of a three per cent royalty is a third of head office's recurring income. Two points carved out of five per cent leaves head office exactly where it is today, gives the zone owner a rate that pays, and costs the store two points of turnover for a partner who funded a quarter of its fit-out. Part 9 runs every route through the same store; 4% is the floor that funds one point and nothing more.

4
The zone map and the store plan are incompatible

Ninety-six zones and a 189-to-273-store build means 2.8 stores per zone. That is a zone that cannot pay its own manager. The 273-store plan wants 46 zones of six, not 96 of three. One of those two numbers has to move before the first zone is offered for sale.

Note

Every figure in this paper is produced by one model, applied identically to every zone size, structure and scenario. The inputs are Papa Pasta's own store numbers — R600,000 a month at maturity and a 22% EBITDA margin inside the 20–25% band the master document records. Where the documents disagree with each other, part 16 names the conflict rather than smoothing it over.

02 / 16The proposal

Three layers instead of two

The traditional franchise has a franchisor and a franchisee. This adds a third party who owns a territory, sells the stores inside it, holds equity in them, and is paid a share of their turnover.

Today there are two parties and one relationship: Papa Pasta licenses a store to an operator for R300,000, the operator builds it for R1.5m and carries R700,000 of working capital, and pays 3% royalty, a 3% marketing levy and a technology fee. Head office supports every store itself.

The proposal inserts a zone owner — a regional master franchisee — between them:

Layer 1 · Papa Pasta
The brand
Owns the trademark, the system, the recipes, the central kitchen and the franchise agreement. Signs every store. Keeps 3% royalty, the levy, the technology fee and the kitchen margin.
Layer 2 · Zone owner
The region
Buys a territory for R200,000–R250,000. Finds and signs off operators. Holds at least 25% of every store in the zone. Runs the standards, the training, the audits and the financial reviews. Paid a share of turnover plus his dividends.
Layer 3 · Operator
The store
Owns 75% of one store, runs it day to day, puts in R1.1m of cash against R1.0m of asset finance. Pays royalty, levy and technology fee. Gets a partner who funded a quarter of his shop.

Watch

The brief describes the zone owner receiving “1% of the turnover of the royalties”. Read one way that is one percentage point of the stores' turnover, paid out of the royalty stream — R72,000 a store a year at base sales. Read the other way it is 1% of the royalty itself, which is R2,160 a store a year and not a business. This paper models the first reading throughout, because it is the only one that can pay a zone manager, and flags the wording as a clause that must be written precisely.

What the zone owner is buying

Territory
A defined area, exclusive against other zone owners and against head office opening corporate stores there — subject to the open territory question in part 13.
Right to recruit
He finds the operators, screens them against the franchisee profile, and puts them forward. Papa Pasta signs the franchise agreement with each store; the zone owner signs a zone agreement with Papa Pasta.
Minority equity
At least 25% of every store in the zone, bought at cost as each store opens. Staged capital, not a lump sum.
A share of turnover
One to two percentage points of every store's gross sales, paid monthly, for as long as the zone agreement runs — whether the store is profitable or not.
The obligations
Signage, training, compliance, financial review, brand audits and the operator relationship. Part 5 prices this properly, because it is where most of his money goes.

03 / 16The problem

Twenty-five million rand is not a franchise, it is a property portfolio

A zone of ten stores is R25m of somebody's money. The traditional franchise model asks one person to find it, guarantee it and run it — and then wonders why the network grows three stores a year.

Papa Pasta's store costs R2.5m all in: R300,000 licence, R1.5m fit-out, R700,000 of working capital. One store is a stretch for a first-time operator. Five stores is R12.5m. Ten is R25m. The zone structures on file ask the zone licensee to own everything himself — the V6 rate card charges him a per-store licence rate times his committed stores, and the 4 September restructure moves him to 100% of a flagship plus 51% of four more. Either way he is a property owner with a restaurant habit, and the pool of people who can write that cheque in South Africa is small, cautious and slow.

The zone owner carries less and the operator carries less, because the bank carries R1m of the store. That is the whole trick: the capital comes out of the store's cash flow, not out of a family's savings.

Three buyers, not one

The reason this matters is not the size of the cheque — it is who can write it. Today the buyer must be an owner-operator: the franchisee ideal profile requires R3m of liquid capital and R8m of net worth for a multi-unit zone, plus a bank pre-approval at letter-of-intent stage, and the owner-operator rule requires the principal to be active in the business for at least twelve months before a franchisor-approved general manager takes over.

Split the ticket three ways and three different buyers appear:

  • The operator with R1.1m of cash and a bank willing to fund R1m of equipment — a working chef or restaurant manager, not a property investor.
  • The zone owner with R250,000 for the territory and R625,000 per store as each one opens — staged, so the capital follows the openings rather than preceding them.
  • The landlord or family office who will fund a second or third store once the first is trading, because by then there is a profit history to underwrite.
Finding

The franchisee ideal profile as recorded requires R3m liquid and R8m net worth for a multi-unit zone. A zone owner buying 25% of five stores needs R3.4m of capital in total — but he needs R250,000 to start. That gap between what he must eventually own and what he must produce on day one is the entire commercial case for the layer.

The store can only share what survives tax, depreciation and the next refit

The number that matters for the zone layer is the bottom line: R881,000 a store, or 12.2% of turnover. A 25% stake is worth R220 230 a store a year. Five stores is R1 101 150 of dividends — which is roughly what it costs to employ the zone manager who looks after them.

Watch

The master document records a payback of four to seven months on a store. That does not reconcile with these inputs: R2.5m at a 22% margin on R7.2m a year is a 19-month payback, and R1.8m at the same margin is 14 months. A four-month payback on the R2.5m ticket would need R7.5m a year of EBITDA — more than the store's entire turnover. The zone layer inherits whatever the store really does, so this is the first number to settle with finance before a zone is sold on the strength of it.

Every point of royalty moves the share

The franchise documents carry two royalty bases — 3% in the franchise agreement and disclosure document, 5% in a later finance instruction — and the difference is not cosmetic. Each point of royalty takes R72,000 a year off the store and R18,000 off the zone owner's 25% dividend. At 5%, which is the rate this paper recommends, the store's distributable profit falls to R775 800, and every zone number in this paper is built on that lower figure — not on the 3% the contract still says.

05 / 16The job

Being a mini-franchisor is a full-time job with a payroll attached

The zone owner is not a passive investor taking a point of turnover. He is the region's franchisor: he recruits, trains, audits, disciplines and reports. Priced honestly, that work costs R216,000 a store a year.

This is the part of the idea that decides whether the economics work, and it is the part that is easiest to underestimate. The zone owner does everything head office would otherwise do for five to ten stores — and he pays for it out of his own share.

Watch

Head office's own field structure — one consultant per five stores, travel, a compliance officer, training delivery — costs R1 980 000 for ten stores, or R198 000 a store. The zone's own overhead is R210 000 a store. The zone layer is not cheaper than employing the people. It is cheaper for Papa Pasta, because the zone pays for it out of store profit and the zone owner's capital, not out of head office's payroll.

That distinction is the honest version of the pitch, and it survives scrutiny. A zone owner who is told he will get rich on 1% of turnover has been misled. A zone owner who is told he is buying the region's management contract, 25% of the stores, and a job that pays R540,000 a year on top — that is a business a person can understand and a bank can underwrite.

The shape of the curve

The curve explains the whole model, and it did not move when the rate changed — only its height did. Three stores cannot pay for a manager: the zone's own overhead is R936 000 a year before he earns a rand. Six stores earn 17.5%. Seven is worse than six, because a second manager costs R540,000 and arrives one store too early. Ten earns 15.7%. At one point of turnover the same curve tops out at 8.5% — the shape is the business, the rate is whether it is worth buying.

What the matrix says

  • One point at 25% does not work at either size. 7.5% at five stores and 8.5% at ten is less than the store next door earns on its own cash, and the zone owner is taking more risk.
  • Raising the override to 1.5 points is worth more than doubling the zone. Five stores at 1.5 points outsells ten stores at 1 point on every measure that matters to a buyer — less capital, less complexity, more cash per rand invested (11.0% against 8.5%).
  • Equity is the expensive dial. Moving from 25% to 35% adds R1 250 000 of capital at five stores and buys 5.1% of return. Moving the override from one point to one and a half costs the zone owner no extra capital — only a thinner dividend at the store — and buys 3.5%. If only one dial can move, move the override.
  • Twenty per cent is out of reach at a quarter of the stores. Two points at 25% reaches 14.5%; 20% needs 51% of the equity, or ten stores at half the equity — which is a mini-franchisor with a balance sheet, not a zone manager. The way past the hurdle is the salary he draws for running the zone himself, and that is why part 6 prices it.
Finding

The override is the only dial that costs Papa Pasta nothing — provided it is carved out of a royalty that was raised to carry it, which part 9 prices. And the store can afford it, because the zone layer removes R625,000 of capital from the operator's ticket. Two points of turnover is R144,000 a year; the capital it saves him is R625,000. Framed that way, the operator is buying R625,000 of funded equity for R144,000 a year, and the zone owner is being paid for work head office would otherwise have to employ somebody to do.

Watch

The first year is not the mature year. At 70% of mature sales a store earns roughly half of the numbers above, which is exactly what the R700,000 of working capital is for. Any zone offering that quotes the mature case as year one is selling a store that will run out of cash in month five.

The bank question

Asset finance is the hinge, and it is the part of this model least under Papa Pasta's control. Three things make a bank comfortable, and all three are structural rather than cosmetic:

  • One operator with a personal surety. Banks lend to a person, not to a partnership. The operator must be the majority shareholder and the signatory on the lease, with the zone owner's 25% held under a shareholders' agreement the bank has seen.
  • A franchise agreement Papa Pasta signs. If the franchise contract sits with the zone owner, the bank is lending into a sub-franchise it cannot enforce. This is the strongest argument for keeping Papa Pasta as the franchisor of record — part 13.
  • A trading precedent. Store one is the proof gate for every loan after it. Until it has twelve months of accounts, the zone model is a proposal, not an asset class.

Read the middle three columns together and the structure of the decision appears:

  • Four per cent funds one point and nothing more. A one-point override carved out of 4% leaves Papa Pasta exactly where it is today — R216 000 a store — and costs the franchisee one point of turnover. That is the cheapest version of the layer. It is also the version that pays a five-store zone 7.5%, which is not a business.
  • Four per cent with a point and a half needs head office to give up half a point — R180 000 a store instead of R216 000, or R360 000 across ten stores, forever. It buys 11.7% for the zone owner. That is a defensible trade, and it is the trade the 4 September restructure already implies.
  • Five per cent with two points costs the store half a point more than the 4.5% carve and pays the zone 14.5% instead of 11.0% — while Papa Pasta keeps its full 3%. The store's margin falls from 22% to 20.0%; the operator still banks R310 346 a year against a R1 125 000 ticket.
  • Charging the override on top of a 4% royalty is the one route that hurts everybody. The store pays 5.5%, its margin falls to 19.5%, and the zone owner earns 7.1% — worse than the 4.5% carve, because a quarter of every extra point of royalty comes out of his own dividend.
Finding

The recommendation is the fifth row: a 5% royalty with two points remitted to the zone owner. It leaves head office on R216 000 a store — the same as today — and it is the only rate in the table that keeps a five-store zone above 7% even when sales come in 10% under plan (7.3% in the downside case, against 1.0% at one point). Everything above 5% starts pricing the store out of its own bank facility; everything at 4% leaves the zone owner running a full-time job for a bond return.

Ten reasons the zone layer is worth the paperwork

None of these are opinions about franchising in general. Each one is a number this model produced, or a clause already sitting in Papa Pasta's own documents.

1
The operator's ticket falls by a quarter, and his cash by more

R2.5m becomes R1 875 000, and with 40% asset finance the cash he needs is R1 125 000 instead of R1.5m. That single change moves the franchise from "wealthy investor" to "experienced restaurant person".

2
A territory costs R250,000, not R12.5m

The entry point to owning a zone falls fifty-fold. That is what turns a property decision into a career decision — and career decisions are made faster.

3
Head office stops carrying the regional payroll

Covering ten stores with employed field staff costs R1 980 000 a year. The same coverage costs the store R144 000 at two points — and nothing at all on Papa Pasta's payroll.

4
The network is funded by other people's capital

Ninety-six zones at R250,000 is R24 000 000 of zone fees. Four hundred and eighty stores at a 25% zone stake is R300 000 000 of equity that does not come from head office's balance sheet. That is the difference between growing a network and financing one.

5
Ninety-six people selling instead of one sales desk

A zone owner who has paid R250,000 and must open five stores to earn anything is the most motivated salesperson the brand will ever have. He is paid on success, not on salary, and he lives in the territory he is selling.

6
His money is at risk before head office's is

A 25% stake in every store means the zone owner loses R220 230 a store a year before head office loses a rand of royalty. A salaried area manager risks his job; a zone owner risks his capital.

7
The zone becomes a tradeable asset

An override plus equity plus a territory is something another person can buy. A salaried area manager's job is not. That resale market is what makes the R250,000 defensible to a buyer's accountant — and a zone owner who can exit is a zone owner who will invest.

8
The royalty annuity dwarfs the licence fee

At maturity, 480 stores at 3% is R103 680 000 a year. The V6 map's R119 000 000 of licence value is a one-off that has never been realised. The zone fee is not where the money is.

9
It makes the brand competitive with cheap formats

R1.1m of cash against a fish-and-chips franchise's R800,000 is a conversation. R1.5m against R800,000 is not. The barrier to entry is the product, and this lowers it without cutting the royalty.

10
It answers the family-business question directly

A parent with R3.4m can buy a territory, place a child in the zone manager's seat at R540,000 a year, and hold 25% of five stores that each throw off R880 920 before distributions. That is a business a family can run together — and it is a much better answer than handing a child a single restaurant to fail at alone.

13 / 16The law

The Consumer Protection Act decides the structure before the economics do

South Africa does not define a franchise in the Companies Act sense — it defines it in the Consumer Protection Act, and that definition reaches the zone owner. Whether he is a franchisor or a development agent is the most consequential clause in the whole design.

What the Act requires of any franchise sale

Written and signed
Section 7(1): the agreement must be in writing, signed by the franchisee, contain the prescribed information and be in plain language.
Ten business days
Section 7(2): the franchisee may cancel without cost or penalty within ten business days of signing. The agreement must carry a conspicuous notice of that right, in plain language.
Fourteen days before
Regulation 3: a signed disclosure document at least fourteen days before signature, with the number of outlets, turnover and profit growth, written projections and the assumptions behind them, an accounting officer's or auditor's certificate of solvency, a list of current franchisees with contact details, and an organogram of the support system.
Every page qualified
Each page of the disclosure document must be qualified in respect of its assumptions — which is precisely where a zone override must be honest about the three-store break-even.
Territory in detail
Regulation 3(f): the territorial rights granted must be set out in detail. An exclusive zone that head office can breach with a dark kitchen is a disclosure problem, not a drafting nuisance.
If it is wrong
A defective disclosure does not make the agreement void from the start, but it makes it voidable at the franchisee's instance, with a damages claim and a complaint to the National Consumer Commission available on top.
Legal

Regulation 3 disclosure applies to sales to sub-franchisees too, and the duty rests on the master franchisee or sub-franchisor. That single line decides the structure: if the zone owner signs the store's franchise agreement, he must produce his own disclosure document, his own solvency certificate and his own projections — for every store, for the life of the zone. A brand at pre-revenue stage cannot carry that. If Papa Pasta signs, the duty stays where the balance sheet is.

The proposal sits closer to an area-development model with a service mandate than to a classic master franchise — and that is a feature, not a compromise. It keeps the brand, the contract and the disclosure duty at head office while putting the regional work and capital in the field.

Two clauses that will be argued about

  1. The zone agreement is probably a franchise agreement in substance. It grants the right to carry on business under Papa Pasta's system and brand, in a defined territory, for a continuing relationship, for a fee. Counsel must decide whether the Act reaches it; the safe assumption is that it does, which means a disclosure document, a fourteen-day window and a ten-day cooling-off for the zone owner as well. Budget for that in the zone sale process, and do not let a zone be signed at a kitchen table.
  2. His authority must be delegated, not assumed. A 25% shareholder has no power to audit, suspend or remove an operator. If the zone agreement does not grant those powers expressly, the zone owner is a passenger with a dividend — and the franchisor will end up doing the work he is being paid for.

Franchisee profile and the owner-operator rule

The recorded profile requires R3m of liquid capital and R8m of net worth for a multi-unit zone, with a bank pre-approval letter at letter-of-intent stage, and a principal who is active in the business for at least twelve months before a franchisor-approved general manager takes over. A passive investor does not qualify for a zone licence. That rule is compatible with this proposal only if the zone owner is genuinely the zone's manager — which is also the version the numbers support (part 6). If the intent is to sell zones to absent capital, the rule has to be changed deliberately and the disclosure document rewritten to match.

Tax and admin, in plain terms

  • The override is revenue in the zone owner's hands, not a dividend. It is taxable, it counts towards the R1m VAT registration threshold, and it should sit in a company rather than a personal name.
  • Dividends between companies are exempt from dividends tax; the 20% falls when the zone company distributes to the individual. The model applies it at that point, which is why every return in this paper is post-dividends tax.
  • One zone company, one shareholders' agreement per store is the admin minimum. Ninety-six zones with ten stores each is up to 960 agreements — the template and the exit formula matter more than the drafting fee.
  • FASA membership is not compulsory but brings a code of ethics and its own disclosure requirements. A new network selling a novel structure benefits from being seen inside the tent.

Charts are drawn inline from the model's own output — no figure on this page was typed by hand. The model file and its worked output sit beside this page.
