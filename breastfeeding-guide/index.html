A guide for a new mother

Breastfeeding — from the first days through the first year

Sofia is brand new, and this is your first time doing this. Almost everything written about feeding a baby assumes you already know how, and nobody does the first time. So here is the one thing to take from all of this: you cannot ruin it by feeding her too often, and almost nothing about it needs to be bought, added or fixed. The rest is detail, ordered by her age. Looking after you is about you, and it is for now.

The one thing that matters most

Milk is made by being removed. That is the whole mechanism. The more often and the more completely Sofia drains the breast, the more milk your body makes next time — which is why these weeks feel almost constant, and why that is not a problem to solve but the system working.

What changes outcomes most is not a product, it is people. Someone watching the latch, someone bringing food, someone saying that's normal. That is the strongest evidence there is: support, not supplements.

And you will worry about whether she is getting enough — probably this week, probably in the evening, probably in the fourth hour of a feed. Almost every mother does. It is usually wrong, and you measure it with nappies and weight, not with how you feel at nine at night.

The first two weeks

These are genuinely the hardest — not because anything is wrong, but because everything is new at once, your body is doing something it has never done, and you are doing it on broken sleep in a house that already runs on two other children. It is hard. It also ends.

Feed her at least eight to twelve times in twenty-four hours, and more in the evening. Wake her if she is too sleepy to ask. Feed on the early signs — turning her head, hands to mouth, stirring, mouthing — not on crying, which is a late signal.

Getting the latch right, because it is the whole game

A few seconds of tenderness as she draws the nipple in is normal. Pain through the whole feed, nipples that look creased or lipstick-shaped afterwards, or a clicking sound is not something to push through. It is a latch problem, and latch problems are fixable — mechanical, not a matter of endurance.

  • Tummy to tummy. Her chest against yours, body in line, not twisted with her head turned.
  • Nose to nipple, head tipped slightly back. Bring her onto you rather than pushing your breast into her.
  • More of the darker skin visible above her top lip than below. Chin pressed into the breast.
  • Support her back and shoulders, not the back of her head, so she can tip it back.
  • Come off properly — a clean finger in the corner of her mouth to break the seal. Never pull her off.

Once the latch is right, position is the easy part

There is no single correct hold. These are the ones that cover almost every situation, and the right one is whichever lets her get a deep latch without you hurting. If one hurts, changing position is a legitimate fix — not a defeat. Try each of them in the first fortnight, so you have them in your hands before you need them at three in the morning.

Engorgement in the first week — and what the evidence honestly says

Around day three or four, when your milk comes in, your breasts may become full, warm, hard and heavy, sometimes up into the armpit. It usually eases over a few days as supply settles to what she actually asks for.

What helps is mechanical, and it is the same principle as everything else here: get milk out often and comfortably, and do not clamp down on the breast. Feed often, start on the fuller side, and use both. If a breast is so full that she cannot latch, hand-express a little first to soften it enough for her to take it. Warmth just before a feed can help the flow; something cool between feeds, for as long as it feels good.

On the remedies people recommend — cold cabbage leaves, cold gel packs, compresses, enzyme tablets — the honest position is genuine uncertainty. The systematic review of engorgement treatments found 21 trials in more than 2,000 women, could not combine them, and reached no firm conclusion; the certainty of the evidence was low or very low, and no single treatment can be recommended. Cold cabbage leaves and cold gel packs may help with pain and hardness, at that low level of confidence. So they are cheap and harmless, and using one because it comforts you is reasonable — just do not expect it to be the thing that fixes this.

What not to do: aggressive emptying, deep massage, or vibration. High pressure inside the breast makes inflammation worse, not better.

What's normal here:

breasts that feel enormous, hard and hot for a few days; a baby who struggles to latch onto a very full breast; leaking; and relief that arrives within a feed. Engorgement is uncomfortable rather than harmful, and it passes.

A blocked duct, or mastitis — what to do at home

A blocked duct is a tender, firm lump in one part of a breast, often with a red patch over it. Mastitis is the same process further along: the breast is red, hot and painful, and you may feel fluey and aching all over, as though you were coming down with something. These are one continuum, not separate illnesses, and most of them settle at home.

The rules here have changed in recent years, and it is worth having them clearly, because the old advice was to attack the lump. The modern principle is to stop hurting it: remove milk often and flexibly, and keep your hands off the lump.

  • Feed often, and feed normally. Start on the affected side at each feed, and keep going. Feeding through a blocked duct or mastitis is usually the right thing — the milk is safe for her, and getting it out is the treatment.
  • Change position so her chin points towards the lump. Different angles drain different parts of the breast.
  • Do not massage, press on, or vibrate the lump. This is the single biggest correction to the folk advice you will hear. Focused pressure on inflamed tissue makes the swelling and the tissue damage worse, not better. Gentle hands are fine; working it out is not.
  • Do not pump to empty it. Extra pumping drives up production and pressure. Express a little for comfort only.
  • Take the pressure off it. Loosen the bra or go without, move tight straps and the seatbelt off that side, and try not to sleep on it.
  • Cold for comfort, briefly. Warmth only just before a feed, if it helps your milk come down.

Call today, not at the next appointment, if you have a fever with a red, hot, painful, hard patch on a breast, a red streak, or aching all over with breast pain. That pattern needs assessment and often antibiotics, and it is a doctor's call rather than a home one.

What's normal here:

a tender lump that shifts or softens within a feed or two; a breast that feels lumpy and over-full for a day or two; one side being more prone to it than the other. Most blocked ducts clear with feeding alone within a day.

Tongue-tie — worth asking about, not the first thing to reach for

Tongue-tie is where the strip of tissue under the tongue is short or tight. Someone may raise it, or you may read about it if feeding hurts. Here is the honest position, and it is more cautious than the internet suggests: the case for cutting it is narrow, and the evidence is contested.

Reviewing the trials together, releasing the tie probably reduces a mother's nipple pain in the short term, but it showed no consistent improvement in how the baby feeds — and every one of those trials had serious weaknesses in how they were done. A large trial that set out to answer the question properly was stopped early, and reported three adverse events after the surgery. So this is not something to rush towards.

What that means for you: if feeding is painful or she is feeding poorly despite a good latch and help from someone qualified, it is fair to ask about tongue-tie as part of that assessment. It is a clinician's examination rather than something to diagnose from a photograph, and it comes after the latch, not before it.

Watch her output, not the clock

  • At least six wet nappies in twenty-four hours after day five. Before that, expect fewer.
  • Stools change: black and sticky, then greenish-brown, then loose and mustard-yellow by around day five. Stools that stay dark, or stop, need a call.
  • Weight: losing up to about a tenth of her birth weight is expected, lowest around day three or four, and she should be back to birth weight by about two weeks.

Do not use a pump to check your supply. A pump does not empty a breast as well as a baby does. If a session gives you forty millilitres and she is gaining, the pump is just less efficient than she is.

A top-up is a clinician's decision, with a plan — not something to add on your own to see if it helps. In a well baby who is feeding and gaining, topping up is associated with stopping sooner, and it deepens the very worry it was meant to soothe. There are real reasons to supplement — significant weight loss, dehydration, a medical reason — and every one is a clinician's call.

What's normal here:

feeding for forty minutes, then again twenty minutes later, then all evening; milk arriving day three or four with breasts that suddenly feel like someone else's; leaking; a baby who wants to be held constantly. The first fortnight is a blur for almost every mother who has ever done this, including the ones who make it look easy now.

Have one whole feed watched this week, even if nothing is wrong. That half hour is the most useful thing anyone can give you right now.

Hiccups — the loudest thing that means nothing

She will get hiccups constantly in these first weeks, sometimes several times a day, sometimes in the middle of a feed, sometimes while fast asleep. They can go on for ten or fifteen minutes and sound far more dramatic than they are. This is one of the most normal things a newborn does, and it bothers you far more than it bothers her.

The reason is mechanical. The diaphragm — the big muscle under the lungs — twitches, and the vocal cords snap shut over the top of it, which is what makes the noise. Nothing is wrong with her, nothing is stuck, and there is no air bubble that needs to come out. Preborn babies hiccup too; most mothers feel it as a rhythmic flutter late in pregnancy, which means Sofia was doing this before you ever met her.

Nothing you do reliably stops them, and that is the honest position — they stop on their own, and the things people try mostly don't change that.

What is worth knowing, in order:

  • Do nothing. Wait it out. This is the recommendation most paediatricians actually give, and it is the right one most of the time.
  • If she is feeding and hiccupping, pause and let her settle before offering the breast again, rather than pushing through. A short break usually resets it. If the hiccups have not settled within a few minutes, offering the breast again often stops them.
  • Try a dummy if you use one. Sucking relaxes the diaphragm, and this is the one trick with a sensible mechanism behind it.
  • Feed her before she is frantic. Hiccups are more likely during a feed when she is very hungry and gulping fast. Offering the breast earlier and calmer is the most useful preventive thing.

What does not help — and one thing to avoid:

  • Holding her upright, patting, bouncing, or "winding" her will not stop hiccups — and since hiccups are not trapped air, there is nothing to bring up.
  • Do not reach for gripe water. There is no good evidence it does anything for hiccups, and the products themselves say not to use them under one month — Sofia is younger than that. It is also unregulated, its ingredients vary between brands, and it has been recalled repeatedly: for contamination that put a nine-month-old into septic shock, and for undissolved matter that is a choking risk. Please put it in the same category as the top-up question: well-meant, widely sold, and not something she needs.

When hiccups are worth mentioning to someone. Almost never — but hiccups that come with the reflux picture are the exception. If she hiccups and is unsettled or arching during and after feeds, and is bringing milk back often, and is not gaining weight, that combination is worth raising with your midwife or clinic. Hiccups on their own, in a baby who is feeding well and gaining, are not a symptom of anything.

What's normal here:

all of it. Hiccups several times a day, for ten or fifteen minutes, mid-feed, waking her, while she sleeps — every one of those is normal.

Weeks two to six

This is where it settles — not into a schedule, but into something predictable enough to plan around.

Expect a growth spurt at two to three weeks and another at six. Each brings a few days of near-constant feeding, usually in the evening. It is not a sign your milk has dried up; it is how supply rises to meet the next stage, and it passes. It does not need a supplement, a herb, or a stricter routine.

"Is she getting enough?" — the worry that visits everyone

About half the women who stop breastfeeding early say this was why. But it is usually the perception, not an actual shortage, and what resolves it is skill and confidence rather than a substance:

  • Count nappies and weight. Those are the measurements. Crying is not.
  • Feed more often before assuming less milk. Frequency is the lever you control.
  • Have one feed watched by someone who knows what an effective feed looks like.
  • Notice if a top-up has crept in, and ask why, and by whose decision.

The nights

Night waking is normal at this age, it is not caused by breastfeeding, and starting solids early will not fix it. What helps is not a technique but a split: someone else does the nappy changes, the winding, the settling and the older children's bedtime, and your only job in the evening is to feed her. That is not a favour; it is what keeps you upright.

The safest sleep arrangement is her in your room, on her own surface, on her back, on a firm mattress, with nothing soft around her.

What's normal here:

evenings that feel like one long feed; a baby who feeds more on some days than others; the tingle when your milk lets down; one side producing more than the other; and a strong urge to cry at around six in the evening for no reason. Also normal: an appetite bigger than you expect.

Six weeks to three months

Somewhere between six and twelve weeks, feeding stops being something you think about constantly. She gains along a curve, her nappies are predictable, feeds are comfortable, and you notice you have stopped counting.

This is the sensible window to start expressing — if you have a reason to. Not before, unless there is a clinical one. Be honest about the cost: expressing takes real time, and mothers who express routinely are more likely to stop feeding at the breast sooner. Aim it at a purpose. A store so someone else can do a feed is a good purpose. Expressing to prove you have enough milk is not.

Storing expressed milk

  • Room temperature (16–29 °C): up to 4 hours. Six to eight hours if everything was scrupulously clean.
  • Fridge (about 4 °C): up to 4 days. Five to eight days under very clean conditions. Back of the fridge, not the door.
  • Freezer (below −4 °C): up to 6 months. Twelve months at a push. Back of the freezer, away from the walls of a self-defrosting one.

Then the practical bits, which matter more than the table:

  • Label the date, and store in small amounts — 15 to 60 mL — so a half-used bottle is not a wasted one.
  • Leave a little headspace for expansion, and never add warm, freshly expressed milk to milk that is already cool or frozen. Cool it first.
  • Thaw overnight in the fridge, or under warm water — not hot. Warm to no more than about 40 °C, because higher breaks down the parts of milk that do the protecting.
  • Use fresh milk before frozen milk — it is better. And once she has fed from a bottle, throw the rest away after an hour or two.
  • Carrying it anywhere? An insulated bag keeps it measurably cooler than a bare bottle.

Bottles, dummies and the folklore

The idea that a bottle or dummy will "confuse" a breastfed baby and ruin feeding is narrower than the folklore suggests, and it is bottles that matter, not dummies. The rule is timing rather than prohibition: introduce a bottle only if you need one, after feeding is established, with a slow technique that lets her set the pace, and stop if it causes trouble. If you want her to take one later, practise a couple of weeks before you need it, not on the day.

If and when you go back to work

Whether you go back, and when, changes everything below — so decide it rather than waiting for it to decide itself. If you go back: feed before you leave, feed when you get home, keep the night feeds — those protect supply. Express or feed midday on a schedule, with a place already sorted. You are entitled to two thirty-minute feeding or expressing breaks each working day for the first six months, and to attend postnatal appointments; that is in South African employment law, even where workplaces pretend otherwise. And expect the return itself to be the hard part, not the pumping.

What's normal here:

a baby who suddenly pops off to study the room; a feed that takes ten minutes instead of forty; a day of constant feeding followed by a day of barely any interest; a first proper period. Also normal: feeling ambivalent about going back to work. Both feelings are allowed at once.

Three to six months

Nothing much changes here except that it all feels easier — and then a curve ball arrives at around four months.

Her sleep changes at around four months. Not because of anything you did: her sleep cycles reorganise, and a baby who was sleeping in longer stretches starts waking more. It is developmental, it passes, and it is not fixed by starting solids. Early food does not help sleep and it pushes out milk at exactly the point where milk is still doing the heavy lifting.

She will get distracted — popping off to look at her brother, the ceiling fan, a sound in the next room. Feed somewhere quieter and dimmer, feed before she is overtired, and accept short feeds more often.

If your supply feels lower, look at what changed first. A dropped night feed, a busy week, a baby sleeping longer, an illness — those reduce demand, and supply follows. Restore the stimulation: feed more often, express if a feed was missed, skin to skin, and give it a few days.

Comfort feeding is not a bad habit

Feeding her when she is not hungry — because she is hurt, tired, overwhelmed, or just wants you — is one of the things breastfeeding is for. It is not creating a rod for your back. If it ever becomes something draining you rather than comforting her, the fix is practical: more help, more rest, someone else settling her for one feed.

What's normal here:

frantic feeding before a growth spurt; the long evening feeds returning; going days between dirty nappies as long as they are soft; a sudden fierce attachment to one side or one position. Also normal: feeling touched out by the end of the day. That means you need a break, not that you are doing something wrong.

Around six months — starting solids

Around six months food starts — as an addition to milk, not a replacement. Not before four months, and not later than six.

Wait for readiness, not just age: sitting with support and good head control, the reflex that pushes food straight back out having faded, real interest in what you are eating, and the ability to turn her head away when she has had enough.

Start with iron. Her iron stores were laid down before birth and are mostly used up by around six months. The first foods that matter are soft cooked meat, small amounts of well-cooked liver, egg, iron-fortified cereal, lentils, beans and tofu. Serve plant iron with something containing vitamin C — the household's citrus and gooseberry already do this — and keep a big cup of tea or coffee out of that meal, because it blocks absorption.

Allergens, one at a time

Introduce the common allergens — peanut, egg, milk, wheat, sesame, fish — one at a time from around six months, then keep the ones she tolerates in her diet regularly. Regularity matters more than a single taste. If she develops severe eczema, or reacts to egg, stop and see a specialist — that is a different pathway with an earlier window.

Gagging is not choking — learn the difference now

  • Gagging is a protective reflex, and it is noisy: coughing, spluttering, red face, tongue pushing food forward. It looks alarming and means the food went too far back and is coming out. Stay calm, let her work it forward, and never put your finger in her mouth while she is gagging.
  • Choking is silent: no cough, no cry, no air, and she may go pale or blue. That is an emergency.

Never leave her alone while eating. No whole nuts, whole grapes, hard raw vegetables or popcorn yet; cut round foods lengthways. Take an infant first-aid course. No honey at all before she is one, not cooked, not in anything. No added salt, no added sugar, no sweetened drinks.

What's normal here:

food coming straight back out; most of it ending up on her, on the floor and in her hair; enthusiastic eating one day and refusal the next; nappies that change in ways you will want to describe to somebody. Almost none of it is about nutrition in the first month — the milk is still doing that.

Six months to a year

The balance shifts. Milk stays important, but food becomes a real part of her day. A workable shape: breastfeeds on waking, at bedtime and around naps, plus three meals and a couple of snacks, with water in an open cup at meals. Offer milk before food in the early weeks of solids so food adds rather than crowds out, and keep the number of feeds steady while you introduce something new.

Milk is worth a great deal when she is ill. On a day with a fever, a cold or a tummy bug, a baby who refuses all food and takes only milk is doing exactly the right thing — it hydrates, protects and comforts her. Offer fluids often in small amounts and keep offering food without pressure.

Teething, biting and the sudden refusal

Biting usually appears with teething around eight to ten months and is not aggression. Do not yelp — the reaction is interesting enough to repeat. Break the latch calmly, take her off, say a firm short "no biting" once, and pause that feed.

A baby who suddenly refuses the breast is having a nursing strike, not weaning. Real self-weaning is gradual. Strikes usually follow illness, a cold, sore ears, teething, a change in soap or washing powder, a scare at the breast, or a change in routine. Offer when she is sleepy or half-asleep, in a quiet dim room, skin to skin, and keep expressing so supply holds.

Eating together, without the battles

Give her what the family eats, in age-appropriate form, and keep offering vegetables including the bitter green ones without making a performance of it. Use the division that removes most conflict: you decide what, when and where; she decides whether and how much. Offer, do not force. Stop at the turned head and the closed mouth. Do not use food as a reward. And do not promise yourself this will program her appetite for life — feeding method and food introduction do not determine how she eats as a teenager. What they build is the habit of eating with people who are calm about food, which is worth having on its own.

What's normal here:

a huge lunch followed by two days of nothing; preferring the food on your plate; throwing everything on the floor to see what happens; wanting to feed herself badly rather than be fed well. Also normal: you getting bored of the mess before she does.

Past a year — and stopping whenever you choose

Breastfeeding past a year is a supported, mainstream thing to do. Well beyond the first year it still supplies a real share of her protein, fat and most vitamins, and still protects her against infection. What it does not have is solid evidence that it makes her cleverer or taller or better at anything later on; that research is genuinely thin and contradictory. That is fine. It is reason enough that she is fed, protected and comforted, without dressing it up as more.

The awkwardness is real, and it is other people's problem

Somebody will say "you're not still feeding her?" in a tone that has nothing to do with nutrition. That awkwardness is not a medical fact, it is a social one, and it says something about how feeding an older baby is seen here, not about what you are doing. You do not owe anyone an explanation — and you are also allowed to decline the fight entirely and feed her somewhere quieter, without that meaning you have conceded anything.

When you decide to stop

You get to decide, and you do not have to decide in advance. When you do:

  • Drop one feed at a time, a few days to a couple of weeks apart, starting with the easiest rather than the one she loves most.
  • Replace it, do not just remove it — a snack, a drink, a cuddle, a distraction, someone else.
  • If a breast feels uncomfortably full, express a little for comfort. Not to empty.
  • Taper rather than stopping suddenly, especially if you are producing a lot.
  • Save the last feed for last, and expect it to feel emotional. That is not a sign you chose wrong.
  • Do not do it during an illness or a big upheaval — exactly when feeding is doing the most work.

What's normal here:

wanting to stop and not wanting to stop in the same week; missing it after you have stopped; a toddler who asks loudly for milk at the worst possible moment; and eventually a day when it has simply finished, and it is smaller than you expected.

What the older traditions say

There is an old idea behind the food and rest you have been asked to take this month, and it is worth knowing, because it turns a list of rules into sense.

The older traditions — Indian, Chinese, Japanese, and most of the world's grandmothers — all arrive at the same picture: birth empties a woman out, and the weeks afterwards are for filling her back up. Pregnancy and delivery draw on her stores, and milk is made from what is left, so the milk is only as good as the reserves behind it. In their language, the postpartum body is depleted, and depleted bodies are not repaired by effort. They are repaired by being fed, warmed and rested.

That is the whole logic, and it is the same logic your household has already adopted: no fasting, no high-intensity training, sleep protected, night duty shared. It is tradition, translated.

So the food. Warm, cooked and generous. Soups and broths, slow-cooked grains, ghee, soft cooked vegetables, stews, and warm spiced milk-style drinks — turmeric, cardamom, cinnamon, a little ginger. Cooked rather than raw while you recover. Fennel and ajwain in the amounts you would use to season a dish are a normal, gentle part of this in the traditions that favour them.

Then the oil. A daily warm-oil massage — sesame or coconut, worked into the shoulders, upper back, lower back and legs — is one of the oldest instructions there is, and the one most likely to change how a day feels. Ask Ricardo to do your shoulders and back; that is genuinely part of the practice, not a favour. One thing the older traditions do not say is to massage the breasts themselves while feeding is still finding its rhythm. That is not the same as the gentle breast care you may be shown for engorgement — check that with your midwife.

The herbs. Fenugreek, shatavari, fennel, oats and barley are the traditional milk-supporting foods, and they have been used this way for centuries. Here is the honest part: that is tradition, not proof, and the studies on them are few and mixed. Food amounts of spices and a bowl of oats are not a supplement — nothing in a bottle, capsule or concentrated tea is, and none of those should be started without checking with your midwife or doctor first.

Rest is not a luxury or a reward for finishing the chores. In every one of these traditions it is the treatment. In Chinese practice the point of the protected first month is that the mother is not the one carrying things, managing visitors, or on her feet.

And one more, which matters more than it sounds: you and Sofia are one unit for now. Not two people with two schedules, but one system resting, feeding and recovering together. That is why keeping her close, on your chest, and sharing the nights properly is not sentimentality. It is the tradition's oldest instruction, and it is the one your household has already got right.

You are not being told to do more. You are being asked to let yourself be cared for, in the way women have been cared for after birth for a very long time. Let the month be soft.

Looking after you

Feeding a baby ten times a day costs your body somewhere around an extra four to five hundred calories daily. It needs eating, not dieting.

Water. Three litres or more a day — and, more importantly, water within arm's reach of wherever you sit to feed. The problem is never intention, it is distance.

Food. Protein and fat first, then everything else: eggs, yoghurt, cheese, fish, chicken, beans, nuts, oats, seeded bread, fruit. The useful version of "lactogenic food" is simply good food, made by somebody else, in a form you can eat with one hand while feeding. Overnight oats with nut butter. Boiled eggs. Soup in a mug. A wrap left in the fridge. The intervention that works is not an ingredient, it is a system for getting fed. The one thing worth being deliberate about is eggs: two or three a day puts you where you want to be on choline, which matters for you as much as for her.

Nothing herbal gets added. No fenugreek capsules, no nursing teas, no lactation cookies, no "milk supply" blends. There is no good evidence any of it works, some of it has real safety concerns in breastfeeding, and the ones tested properly came out no better than placebo. The rule in this house — anything without breastfeeding safety data does not go in — is the right rule.

Caffeine. One or two cups, before about two in the afternoon. She clears caffeine far more slowly than you do and is more sensitive in these early weeks. Energy drinks are a different thing entirely and are not worth it while feeding.

Alcohol, plainly. Not in these first weeks. That belongs at the front of this paragraph rather than the end of it: right now, while feeds are every two or three hours, this is the one stage where the answer is simply no. The old advice to have a beer to help your milk is wrong in any case — alcohol works against your milk coming down and slightly reduces the amount.

Later, once feeding is established and feeds are further apart, the picture is more reassuring than the folklore: occasional drinking has not been convincingly shown to harm a nursing baby, and the amount that reaches her is small. The rule then is timing, not abstinence — feed her, then have the drink, then leave about two hours per drink before the next feed, and use stored milk for a feed inside that window. Pumping and dumping does not help: alcohol leaves your milk as it leaves your blood, on its own schedule.

Sleep. Take the split nights. Protect one stretch where someone else is on duty. Keep her in your room, on her own surface, on her back.

Your mood. If the days feel flat, heavy or frightening, say so plainly to someone — your midwife, your doctor, whoever you actually talk to. It is common, it is treatable, and it is not the same as being ungrateful or bad at this. Treating how you feel is the priority, always, ahead of any feeding decision.

The two checkpoints. At about six weeks, take three things to your check: how you are actually feeling, your pelvic floor and stomach muscles, and any feeding problem still unsolved. Around three months, get the blood panel done — iron, thyroid, vitamin B12, vitamin D — because tiredness has causes worth measuring, and the plan is to test before supplementing.

What's normal here:

hunger and thirst like you have never had; hair falling out in a few weeks; aching wrists and shoulders from carrying her; losing your temper and feeling terrible about it; and loving her so much it occasionally feels like panic.

  • Is breathing oddly, or is hard to rouse at all.

For you — call today if you have:

  • A fever with a red, hot, painful, hard patch on a breast, a red streak, or aching all over with breast pain. That is the pattern needing treatment, not waiting.
  • Breast pain that is severe and burning and comes after feeds, with colour changes. Mention this specifically — there is a common cause of nipple pain that gets missed and treated with the wrong thing for months.
  • Any thought of harming yourself or the baby. That is a medical emergency and an immediate call. It is not a character flaw.

What's normal here:

not knowing whether something is serious enough to call. Everyone feels that. The rule for this house is simple and it is on your side: if you are wondering, call. Nobody at a breastfeeding clinic has ever been annoyed by a mother phoning about a two-week-old.

The bit nobody tells you

  • You will cry on day four or five. It is hormonal, it is nearly universal, and it does not mean anything is wrong with you or with how much you love her.
  • The evening is the worst hour of the day, every day, for weeks. She will feed and fuss and you will be convinced it is a supply problem. It is not — everything has simply accumulated by then.
  • You will be touched out before you are tired. That is real, and it is why split nights matter even on the days she sleeps well.
  • Someone will say something stupid about feeding. It might even be someone who means well. It is not information. Let it go past you.
  • You will compare her to somebody else's baby. Everybody does, and it tells you nothing: she is on her own curve, and the only useful measure is whether she is moving forward.
  • You will feel like you are doing nothing but feeding. You are not doing nothing. You are doing the thing that is doing the most work of anything in the house right now.
  • The hard part is genuinely hard, and it genuinely ends. Not the same way or on the same day for everyone — but it ends.
