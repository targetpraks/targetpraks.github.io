01Yes, send it — but not as 'please reconsider'

0.96 on the framing

Send the email. Put to the decision model as a choice between asking as questions (0.96), asking them to reverse their professional opinion (0.006), waiting for the reports first (0.02) and not sending (0.01), the answer is not close. What it is choosing is a shape: a request that asks them to put hours, mechanism and a comparison on paper gets a better answer than a request to change their mind.

Two reasons, and neither is politeness. First, their own email left the door open: they asked for his speech and language report and Prof Fourie's report to decide whether therapy continues next year. That is an invitation to supply evidence, not a closed door. Second, a no costs you nothing and a no is still worth having: whatever they say, you end up holding a written statement of what the repeat year contains. Right now nobody has that — not you, not Julie, not their own head of school.

The one sentence that makes the email worth writing

You are not asking them to ignore his profile. You are asking them to answer one question they have not answered: what in the repeat year is different from the year he just had, in hours per week, and could the same hours be delivered alongside Grade 4? If they can answer it, the repeat becomes a real intervention and you can agree to it with confidence. If they cannot, they have told you something more important than any placement letter.

Repeating a whole year for one subject is a big costHolds — it is your strongest pointOne year of his life, matric at 19, and R95,880 in fees. The retention evidence does not justify the year on its own: across 207 effect sizes the better-designed studies find effects statistically indistinguishable from zero (Allen et al. 2009). What creates the gain is the intervention bolted onto the year, at roughly g = 0.37 for structured literacy work in these grades (Denton et al. 2021). That is the argument to make: not the year is too expensive, but the year only pays if the hours are in it.
Grade 4 is harder, but he could get it thereOpen — and it is the real questionFrom Grade 4 the curriculum shifts from learning to read to reading to learn (Chall's stages), and the fourth-grade slump in maths scores tracks reading comprehension rather than number sense (IDRA; reading–math dynamics). So "his maths is strong" will not carry him through Grade 4 alone — he will meet word problems and written explanations. But that argues for support in Grade 4, not automatically for a repeat. Which is exactly what you should ask them to compare.

03The question underneath the question

dose, not placement

Strip the language out and the disagreement is not about whether Grade 4 is harder — everyone agrees it is. It is about where the literacy hours come from. Uitzicht's model is a maximum of 12 learners with learning-support teachers and an occupational therapist on site. That is room for the reading work to happen inside the school day, whoever's grade he is in.

So the comparison to put to Veronica is not "repeat or not". It is three placements, and only one of them is bad for him:

Grade 4 at Reddam, no committed supportA mainstream Cambridge class, 100% pass cohort, a reader and a scribe if the package lands, a parent-contracted facilitator if you employ one.The one to avoid. This is the placement the evidence is actually against, and it is the default if nothing changes.
Grade 4 at Uitzicht, with in-class support12 to a class, LS teachers and OT on site, daily literacy withdrawal, accommodations for content subjects.Does not exist yet. It is the option the decision model ranks second only because the school has not offered it — and it is the one your email is trying to create.
Grade 3 at Uitzicht, repeat yearThe same 12-to-a-class room, an extra year, the literacy work the school says he needs before Grade 4.Exists, conditional. The better structure at the price of a year. Ranked first today (0.63 of the three-way run) because a place that exists beats a place that does not.
If they say yes to Grade 4, hold them to numbers

A "yes" that means "we will see how he goes" is worth nothing. What makes a supported Grade 4 real: 180–200 minutes a week of structured literacy in a group of two to four, delivered by a named learning-support teacher; a reader and a scribe for content subjects where reading or writing is the barrier, not the thinking; reduced written volume with the same content; and a Term 1 2027 review against his 5 October baseline, with the criteria for staying in Grade 4 agreed in writing before he starts.
