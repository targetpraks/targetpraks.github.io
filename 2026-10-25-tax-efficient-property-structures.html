Where to put the bricks

Three structures for owning and running short-let property in Greece, Portugal and Spain — built through Singapore, Cyprus, Estonia or Georgia, held for your family, with you resident in none of them. Researched from primary sources, priced on one consistent model.

The answer, in three lines

  1. Best structure for the portfolio you described: a local company in each property country, one Cyprus holding company above them, and a Maltese foundation owning the Cyprus shares for the family. Cyprus is in the EU, has treaty access to Greece, Portugal and Spain, pays 0% on dividends out, is not on Portugal's blacklist, and its notional interest deduction can take the effective corporate rate to roughly 3%.
  2. Cheapest structure that still does the job: buy as an individual, and put the family wrapper in place only when there are three or more properties. On one Greek flat the company is a €5,000-a-year subscription the rent cannot carry; on Portuguese short-lets the individual pays 8.75% of gross while a company chain pays 28%.
  3. Estonia and Georgia are the wrong tool for property and the right tool for the operating business; Singapore is the right tool for family governance and the wrong one for tax. Details and the numbers behind each line are below.

Part 0 — read this firstTwo facts outrank the choice of jurisdiction

Every jurisdiction question you asked sits downstream of two facts. Get them wrong and the comparison is decoration.

1. The rent is taxed where the bricks are — not where the company is

Article 6 of every double tax treaty you would rely on gives the country where the property sits the first and usually the only claim on rental income. Greece taxes the profit of a foreign company's Greek property at 22%. Portugal taxes it at 20%. Spain taxes it at 25%. A Cypriot, Estonian, Georgian or Singapore company owning a flat in Rhodes does not change that line by a single euro — it only changes what happens to the money after Greece has taken its cut.

So the holding jurisdiction is not a tax-rate decision. It is a decision about four things: the exit (can you sell without triggering a second property transfer), the deferral (can profits sit and compound before you are taxed personally), the succession (does the structure survive you), and the annual cost. The right question is never "which country taxes least" — it is "which country costs least over a ten-year hold and a family handover".

2. Where you are tax resident decides whether any of this works

You said you will not live in the country of the company. The working assumption in this document — correct me if it is wrong — is that you are a South African tax resident. If so, that single fact outranks every jurisdiction you named.

South Africa's controlled foreign company rules (section 9D) look through any foreign company in which South African residents hold more than 50%. Its net income is attributed to you and taxed in your hands at marginal rates — up to 45% — whether or not a dividend is ever paid. There is one real defence: the income must be attributable to a foreign business establishment, meaning a genuine place of business abroad, properly staffed and equipped. And even then, section 9D(9A) claws back rental income on movable property.

The practical consequence is sharp, and it is the reason I would not start with company formation:

  • A passive Cyprus holding company with nominee directors and no staff is transparent to SARS. The deferral it looks like it gives you does not exist, and you pay 45% on profits you never received.
  • A genuine Greek operating company — local manager, local contracts, local office, real staff — has an arguable foreign business establishment. That is where the shelter actually lives.
  • If you intend to cease South African residence, that decision must come before the property build-up, not after: ceasing residence triggers an exit charge on your worldwide assets, and assets bought afterwards are outside it.
One question, and it changes the whole document: are you remaining a South African tax resident for the next five years, or is the plan to cease residence? If you are leaving, the order of operations in Part 8 changes and the company layer becomes genuinely valuable. If you are staying, everything below is about asset protection, succession and clean ownership — not tax avoidance — because SARS will see the profit either way.

The four verdicts, plainly

  • Cyprus — the holding company. The only one of your four that is simultaneously in the EU, in treaty with all three property countries, charges 0% on the way out, does not tax a foreign property gain, and is off Portugal's blacklist. Its notional interest deduction — a deemed return on share capital introduced after 2015, capped at 80% of the profit the equity finances — can drop the effective rate toward 3%, which is why it survives the 15% headline.
  • Estonia — the operating company, not the landlord. Zero tax until you distribute is a real advantage, but it does nothing for rent that Greece has already taxed, and the 2% defence tax to 2028 raises the distribution cost. Use it for the property-management business, the brand, the booking platform and the fee income — not for the deeds.
  • Georgia — cheap, and you get what you pay for. It has treaties with Greece, Portugal and Spain, and 15% only on distribution. But it is not in the EU, its substance is thin, and no bank, notary or tax office in the EU treats a Georgian landlord entity as ordinary. It saves €3,000 a year and costs you credibility at every counterparty.
  • Singapore — governance, not tax. 17% is higher than every property country's corporate rate. What you buy is the best trust and family-office ecosystem in the world, a currency and legal system that will still be there in forty years, and a 0% dividend out. At your scale the S$10k-a-year running cost is a family-governance fee, not a tax saving.

What that table says, and it is not what the offshore brochures say

  • In Greece, the company wins on tax and loses on overhead at exactly one flat. At €30k of rent the company saves €1,454 of tax and costs €5,000 to run — you are €3,546 worse off. At €60k it saves €8,608 and the overhead is covered twice over. The line is revenue, not price: roughly €30,000 of rent per company, which is two units or one large villa.
  • In Portugal, the company never wins on income tax. The simplified regime taxes a non-resident individual on 35% of gross at 25% — an effective 8.75%. A Portuguese company pays 20% on the real profit and then 10% to get the money to Cyprus: 28% of profit. On a €60k rent roll the company costs you €6,690 a year more and never catches up.
  • The holding layer adds cost and no tax. A Cyprus company above a Greek company changes nothing in Greece and pays nothing in Cyprus. Its entire value is on the exit and the handover — Parts 3 and 4.

Part 3 — the three structuresWhat I would actually build

Structure A — two layers and a family

Local company in each country → Cyprus holding company → Maltese foundation.

The local company buys the deeds, holds the short-let licence and signs the mortgage. The Cyprus company owns the local shares, so the profit moves up as a dividend at 5% (Greece) or 10% (Portugal, treaty) and then sits in Cyprus untaxed on the way out. The foundation owns the Cyprus shares, so your death changes a register entry in Malta rather than the ownership of a Greek title deed.

Cost ≈ €15–20k/yr for three countries. Wins when the portfolio is large enough that €5k of overhead per country is noise, or when the handover matters more than the annual saving.

Structure B — lean: deeds personal, family wrapper later

Buy as an individual in Portugal and Spain. In Greece, buy personally before 1 July 2027 or through an IKE after it. Put a Maltese foundation in place when there are three units, funding it by gifting the property or the shares at that point, and accept the transfer cost once.

Cost ≈ €1–3k/yr. Wins on one to three units, and on every Portuguese property at any size — 8.75% of gross is a rate no company chain can beat.

Structure C — the operator, which is what you actually described

Run the business in Estonia, own the bricks locally. The OÜ holds the brand, the booking engine, the management contracts and the fee income; each property pays it a genuine market fee. Zero tax on retained profit means the fees compound for the next purchase. The local companies or you own the deeds.

Cost ≈ €2.5k/yr. Wins whenever there is a real operating business — and it is the piece most people miss when they say "I want company structures".

Why the exit is the whole point of Layer 2

Sell a Greek flat owned by a Greek company and Greece takes 22% of the gain plus 5% to get it out — 25.9%. Sell the Cyprus shares instead and Greece takes nothing on the share transfer, Cyprus exempts the gain on shares, and the buyer steps into the company rather than paying 3.09% transfer tax. That is the mechanism the holding company exists for.

Caveat, and it is a real one: the latent Greek tax inside the company remains, and a buyer will discount the price for it. Confirm with Greek counsel whether any real-estate-rich-company rule reaches a foreign holdco before you rely on this.

Part 4 — the family partSuccession, and the Portuguese heir who will not be ignored

This is the section that matters most and gets the least attention. You are a Portuguese citizen holding property abroad, wanting to keep it in the family. Portugal has forced heirship: a fixed share of your estate — the legítima, half of it where there is a spouse and descendants — must pass to your spouse and children, and lifetime gifts that invade it can be reduced afterwards.

Since 2015 the EU Succession Regulation governs which law applies to the whole estate, for Portugal, Greece and Spain alike. Two things follow:

  • The default is the law of your habitual residence at death. If that is South Africa, South African law governs the whole estate — including the Greek and Portuguese flats — and you have freedom of testation.
  • You can choose Portuguese law in your will instead. That election is what triggers forced heirship. Many Portuguese nationals do it out of habit. Do not sign a Portuguese-law will without advice — it is the single most expensive habit in this document.

Three things to do, and one to avoid

1
Make one will, governed by one law, deliberately. Either South African law (freedom of testation) or Portuguese law (forced heirship) — but chosen with your eyes open, in writing, in a will that your advisers in all three countries have seen. A default election by habit is how families end up in court in Lisbon and Rhodes at the same time.
2
Put the family layer in place while the estate is small. A Maltese foundation funded early, before the portfolio is worth €3m, is a governance decision. Funded at death, it is a fight with your own children and a probable clawback claim.
3
Choose a foundation over a trust, for this family. Portugal does not recognise trusts domestically and has not ratified the Hague Trusts Convention; Greece and Spain are no better. A foundation is a legal person that civil-law registrars, banks and notaries accept. Cyprus and Singapore write excellent trusts — for a family whose assets and heirs are in the common-law world.
Avoid: putting Portuguese or Greek immovable property directly into a trust or foundation. Both countries treat the transfer as a disposal, tax it, and may not recognise the entity that now claims ownership. Hold the shares in the foreign holding company — never the bricks.

Greek inheritance tax rates, the treatment of real-estate-rich-company shares, the Maltese foundation's creditor look-back period, and Portuguese stamp duty on gratuitous transfers all need to be confirmed in writing by a lawyer in each jurisdiction before anything is signed. Five traps that cost more than the tax rate

  • Greece: the 2027 date. If the 15% non-EU transfer-tax step-up lands as signalled, an individual buying after 1 July 2027 pays an extra 12.36% of the price. On €400,000 that is €49,440 — the whole company overhead for ten years, decided by a signature date.
  • Greece: the three-property rule. An individual letting three or more properties must register as a business and charge 13% short-let VAT. The structure decision is partly a counting decision.
  • Portugal: containment zones. A short-let licence is permanent and transfers with the property — outside a zone where the council has frozen new registrations. Buying a flat with a live AL licence is worth more than any holding structure; buying one without, in a frozen municipality, is buying a long-let at short-let prices.
  • DAC7. Airbnb and Booking report your gross rents to the Greek, Portuguese and Spanish tax authorities automatically. The gross-basis Greek regime and the Portuguese simplified regime are both enforced against the platform numbers now, not your own accounts.
  • Portugal: the blacklist is not a rumour. A UAE or Bahrain entity owning Portuguese property pays a 7.5% annual surcharge instead of 0.3–0.45%. It is the clearest example of a jurisdiction choice that destroys value.

Part 8 — the order of workWhat happens next, by dependency

Staged by what must be true before the next thing can be done — not by date. Everything inside a stage can run in parallel.

1
Decide residence. Are you a South African tax resident for the next five years? This one answer decides whether the company layer is a deferral tool or a governance tool, and whether the exit charge has to be planned before the first deed. Nothing else starts until it is answered.
2
Run the two purchase routes side by side on the actual property. For each candidate: individual against local company, on purchase tax, annual tax, the fixed overhead and the exit. The Greek crossing is at roughly €30,000 of rent per company; in Portugal the individual wins at every size. Use the model in Part 2 rather than a rule of thumb.
3
Settle the will before the deeds. One will, one governing law, chosen deliberately. This costs a few hundred euros and prevents the most expensive outcome in the document.
4
Build the family layer only when the portfolio needs it. A Maltese foundation at three properties, funded early. Before that, it is a €6,000-a-year ornament.
5
Stand up the operating company if the business is real. If you are genuinely running bookings, brand and management, the Estonian OÜ at 0% retained is the cheapest compounding vehicle available — and it is the part of "company structures" that actually pays.
6
Put four questions to counsel in writing, in all four countries. The answers are what turn this research into a structure.
The four questions, in the exact words I would send:
  1. Greek counsel: does the signalled 15% transfer-tax step-up reach a company, and does any real-estate-rich-company rule tax a transfer of shares in a foreign holding company that owns a Greek subsidiary?
  2. Portuguese counsel: what is the IMT treatment of a Portuguese company owned by a Cyprus holding company, and does the legítima reach lifetime gifts of shares in a foreign holding company?
  3. Maltese counsel: creditor look-back period for a foundation funded by a non-resident founder, and its recognition by a Portuguese notary.
  4. South African adviser: for this fact pattern, is the foreign business establishment defence available to the Greek operating company, and what does ceasing residence cost if it happens before the property is bought?
