SECTION 1 OF 8

The answer

Buy a finished two-bed in Albufeira, not a project. Refresh it, furnish it, and put a 12% co-host on it.

Buy a resale two-bedroom apartment in Albufeira for about €310,000, spend €12,000 refreshing it and €18,000 furnishing it, and let a co-host run it at 12%. All-in €374,293. That pays €21,726 a year after management, running costs and tax — 5.8% on the money in, 7.2% a year over ten years once a 5% annual rise in values is added.

That is a good result for the Algarve in 2026, not a spectacular one, and the reason is arithmetic: a non-resident now pays a flat 7.5% transfer tax, so roughly €32,000 of the €374,293 never becomes property. Add 12% co-hosting, utilities, condominium fees and 8.75% effective income tax, and a €310,000 flat grossing €47,000 a year keeps about €21,700 of it.

The three alternatives each lose for a different, measurable reason:

  • Auction. Real discounts of 15–30% exist on the e-leilões platform and bank repossessions, but you pay the balance inside about 20 days, inspect nothing, and inherit whatever tenant or debt sits inside. On a first purchase made from South Africa, the discount is payment for risk you cannot see.
  • Fixer-upper. The highest income yield on the list at 6.3% — but you own a building site for a year, earn nothing in it, and hand a remote contractor a €76,000 budget you cannot walk through. Over ten years it returns 5.9% against 7.2% for the finished flat.
  • New build. In Lagos, where the market is strongest: €450,000 for the unit, €515,635 all-in, and only 4.5% net. You are paying the developer's margin and the same 7.5% transfer tax on a bigger number.

Why the town hardly matters

Revenue tracks price per square metre almost exactly. Cheap towns are cheap because their guests pay less.

The four Algarve markets that matter for a two-bed all earn between 9.6% and 11.0% of the property's price in gross Airbnb revenue. That spread is 1.4 points — smaller than the gap between a 20% manager and a 12% co-host, and smaller than the difference between a dated unit and a renovated one.

The practical consequence: you cannot buy yield by buying a cheaper town. You buy yield in two places only — a lower purchase price, or a better unit. The second is real and repeatable: the pool, the second bathroom, the air-conditioning, the 4.8-star reviews and the eight-minute walk to the sand are worth more than any municipality on the coast.

The auction route, priced honestly

Auction lots in the Algarve do clear 15–30% under open-market value, and for a cash buyer that is real money. But the mechanics are hostile to a remote first purchase: registration with a deposit, a fixed bidding window, the balance due within roughly 20 days, no conditional clauses and usually no survey. Then the same 7.5% non-resident transfer tax applies to whatever you paid. If you want the discount without the blindfold, buy a bank repossessor instead of a court lot — same distressed seller, but an agent, occasional viewings and a normal deed timeline.

The buy box

  • Albufeira, Olhos de Água, Santa Eulália or Praia da Oura. Walking distance to a beach, not a car ride. Real T2 stock trades at €3,250–3,600/m² against a municipal median of €4,173 — the discount is in the old blocks, which is where you want to be.
  • 85–95 m² with two proper bedrooms and two bathrooms. The second bathroom is worth more in nightly rate than it costs.
  • Condominium pool, parking, lift, air-conditioning. The pool is the single largest ADR lever in a summer market where August afternoons are 32°C.
  • An alojamento local registration already attached to the property. Licences are permanent and transfer with the sale outside containment zones. That single line removes the licensing risk from the deal — confirm it in writing with the câmara before you sign anything.
  • Price discipline: €250,000 is the ceiling at which the income still clears a 7% net yield. Above that you are buying appreciation and calling it income. At €310,000 you are paying for a view and a location — fine, as long as you know it.

Borrowing does not help here

Non-residents can get 70–80% loan-to-value at roughly 3.8–4.8%. The problem is the tax regime: under the simplified arrangement you are taxed on 35% of gross revenue, and the interest is not deductible. So the mortgage costs you 4.3% while the property yields 5.8% — and you never get the interest back. A 70% loan turns a 5.8% return on €374,293 into roughly 2.4% cash-on-cash on €157,293 of equity. Pay cash or do not buy.

The floor, if short-letting ever stops

A long-term let of the same flat earns about €1,250 a month. After costs and the 25% flat tax on rental income for a non-resident, that is roughly €6,358 a year — 1.7% on the all-in cost. It covers the bills. It does not pay for the flat. That is the real downside of buying property whose value rests on a tourist licence.

What kills it

Five ways this deal loses money, in the order they are likely to happen.

Risk 1Buying a unit you cannot license. Municipalities may suspend new alojamento local registrations in "containment zones" — Lagoa already has a regulation in force, Faro has one pending, and Albufeira is the most short-let-saturated municipality in Portugal, which makes it the most likely next candidate. Mitigation: buy only where a registration already exists and transfers with the deed, and get the câmara's confirmation in writing before you sign the promissory contract.

Risk 2Supply. Active listings in Albufeira are up 28% year on year and revenue per listing is down 4.9%. More flats chasing the same summer is a slow price cut nobody announces. Mitigation: the premium end of the market and a genuinely better unit survive a supply glut; a dated one-bedroom conversion does not.

Risk 3Seasonality. August can run at 90% occupancy and January at 30%. Twelve months of fixed costs — condominium, insurance, IMI, accounting, utilities — meet a four-month earning window. Mitigation: price for the shoulder months, keep a monthly-cost buffer of six months' costs, and do not model a 12-month season.

Risk 4Construction and distance. Every euro of refurbishment is supervised by someone 8,000 km away. Budgets slip; in the Algarve, timelines slip more. Mitigation: it is why this brief recommends a finished flat with cosmetic work only, capped at €12,000, on fixed-price quotes.

Risk 5The exit. Selling costs about 6% and the capital gain is taxed at 28% for a non-resident. On a ten-year hold with 5% annual appreciation, the flat roughly doubles to €504,957 and the state takes €33,689 of the gain. Mitigation: none — just price it into the IRR, which is what the 7.2% above already does.

What happens next

Four things worth doing, in this order. None of them costs money until the third.

  1. Appoint a Portuguese lawyer and a fiscal representative before you look at a single listing. The lawyer checks the registo predial, the caderneta predial and whether the alojamento local licence is live and transferable. Budget €1,500 for the search and €3,800 for the deed.
  2. Shortlist five units inside the buy box — Albufeira, 85–95 m², pool, existing AL registration, €290,000–315,000. I can build that shortlist against live listings and score each one on the same yield model used here.
  3. Get the câmara's written position on alojamento local for each building, then commission one structural survey on the finalist. That is the point where money starts being spent, and it is the cheapest €800 in the whole process.
  4. Line up a co-host, not a full-service manager. The difference between 20% and 12% is worth about €4,664 a year on this flat — more than the gap between Lagos and Tavira, and it compounds.

One decision is yours before any of that: is this an income asset or a place you want to use? At 5.8% net it is a modest income asset that happens to appreciate. If you want the flat for two months a year, the model loses eight weeks of peak-season revenue and the net yield drops to about 4.4%. Both are defensible — but they are different purchases.

Assumptions

  • Buyer is a non-resident individual, pays cash, holds for ten years and sells.
  • Acquisition: 7.5% IMT, 0.8% stamp, 1.0% + VAT legal, €1,300 notary and registration, €1,500 due diligence.
  • Operating: 12% co-host fee + VAT, 3% channel fee, utilities €3,800, condominium €1,400, repairs 4% of revenue, cleaning and linen €1,400, insurance €700, IMI 0.35% of a rateable value set at 85% of price, accounting and fiscal representation €1,100, supplies €1,300.
  • Tax: simplified regime, 35% of gross taxable at 25% = 8.75% of revenue; capital gains 28% for a non-resident; exit costs 6%; revenue and costs grow 3% a year; property values 5% a year; year one at 75% of stabilised revenue.
  • Not modelled: Portuguese inheritance and gift-tax exposure, US-style depreciation (Portugal has none), VAT recovery on refurbishment if registered as a business, or any mortgage. All of these move the number by less than the two assumptions above — the management fee and the purchase price.

What this is

A modelled investment case, not an appraisal and not advice. Prices move, licences are granted locally, and the tax position of a non-resident buyer changes with every budget. Every figure is reproducible from the assumptions listed in section 8 — and every one of them should be re-run against a specific building before an offer is signed.
