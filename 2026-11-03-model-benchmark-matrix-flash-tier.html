Five flash-tier models, one matrix

Beam 1.0, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017, MiniMax M3.1 Flash and MiMo V2.6 Flash, scored on every benchmark the five of them publish, plus the two independent scorers. Short version: DeepSeek 1017 is the strongest — Beam is not close on benchmarks, but it is free right now.

The 60-second answer

  • Best model overall: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017. BenchLM 65.7/100, rank #35 / 228 — ahead of MiniMax (59.7), MiMo (54.8) and Beam (43.1). It wins the coding rows outright: DeepSWE 77 vs 73.7 MiniMax / 67.9 MiMo / 43.3 Beam.
  • Beam is mid-field, not the bargain its "free" tag suggests. Its own card puts DeepSWE at 43.3 — 33.7 points under DeepSeek. BenchLM ranks it #130 / 216. What it does win is efficiency: 327.1 tok/s and 79M output tokens per index run, roughly half of DeepSeek's 170M.
  • Best value is MiniMax M3.1 Flash. 59.7 for $0.10 blended — it actually beats DeepSeek on automation (62.2 vs 60.5) and on HLE without tools (43.4 vs 42.6). Its weakness is verbosity and the fact its weights are still not published.
  • The 1017 question is easy: it is the same architecture, retrained. Every published benchmark went up (average +5.3 points), outputs got shorter, price did not move. On Ollama you have both tags; use 1017-cloud.

One caveat that matters. Vendor benchmark tables are not a level playing field — each lab picks its own harness and its own rows. Where a number was not published I left it blank rather than infer it. The two independent scorers (Artificial Analysis, BenchLM) are the only rows where all five models are run the same way.

DeepSeek V4.1 Flash vs V4.1 Flash 1017 — what actually changed

Nothing structural. Same 552B MoE, same 1M context, same MIT licence, same $0.30 / $1.20 per million tokens. 1017 is the October 17 checkpoint: the same model retrained with self-feedback reinforcement learning, tuned to finish tasks in fewer tokens. DeepSeek calls it "a Pareto improvement in performance and cost" and the numbers back that up — every row it published moved up, average +5.3 points.

Beam 1.0 — what it is, and where the "free" claim holds up

Beam is Reflection AI's first open-weight model: 501B total / 23B active, text-only, Apache 2.0, released 16 October. It is genuinely on Ollama Cloud free for a limited time, which is why it is worth testing even though it does not win the matrix. Its own launch table claims 80.9 on SWE-bench Verified, 80.1 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 97.7 on AIME 2026 — respectable rows. The hole is DeepSWE: 43.3 against 77.0 for DeepSeek 1017, which is the benchmark closest to "can it hold a real agentic coding task together". Reflection sells the model on efficiency — 3–4× less inference compute than GLM-5.2 for comparable reasoning — and the independent runs support that part: 327.1 tok/s, 79M tokens per index run, $0.23 per task.

Read it as: a fast, cheap, concise workhorse for high-volume text work while it is free — not as a DeepSeek replacement for serious coding agents. Also: no vision. The other three take images.

Method and sources

  • Independent scorers. Artificial Analysis — Beam 1.0, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017, MiniMax M3.1 Flash, MiMo V2.6 Flash; BenchLM Beam, DeepSeek 1017, MiniMax, MiMo.
  • Vendor cards. Reflection — introducing Beam, DeepSeek 1017 model card, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on Ollama, Xiaomi MiMo V2.6 benchmark appendix, Beam on Ollama.
  • What I did not do. No model was benchmarked locally for this page — a single Mac mini cannot reproduce DeepSWE or Terminal-Bench, and a home-grown score would not compare to the published ones. Everything numeric here is a published figure, traced to the source named in the row.
  • Known soft cells. MiniMax's 59.0 on SWE-bench Pro comes from launch coverage rather than its own ledger; Beam's DeepSWE is the BF16 model-card figure (43.3) while its launch table shows 44.4 — the card value is used because BenchLM tracks the same one. Different OSWorld variants (2.0 vs Verified) were dropped rather than mixed.
  • Verdict weighting. "Best overall" follows the independent BenchLM aggregate, then checks the vendor rows agree. It does not follow any single benchmark, because each lab curates the rows it publishes.
