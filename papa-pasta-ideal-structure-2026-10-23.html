1 · The allocation

Four buckets, and only one of them is brave

The mistake most owner-operators make is treating the cash pool as one thing. It is four things with four different jobs, and the moment you can name the job you can price the rate. The shop is the only bucket that can lose money.

2 · The structure

Four decisions that are worth more than the rate you negotiate

None of these is a financing trick. Each one is a piece of the structure that either captures or forfeits real money, and three of the four have to be decided before the purchase, not after.

1 · Register for VAT before you buy

On a R2,500,000 ticket, the VAT is up to R326,087 — recoverable only by a registered vendor, and only if the seller is a vendor and the sale is not structured as a zero-rated going concern. Register the company and activate the vendor number before the agreement is signed. After the fact, it is gone.

2 · One company, not a group

The small business corporation scale taxes the first R99,000 at 0% and only reaches 27% above R550,000 — worth R88,030 a year at R500,000 of profit. A holding company above the operating company ends it: the shareholders must be individuals, and the company cannot sit inside a group. Ring-fencing each site into its own company costs you that R88,030, every year, for a protection a single growing company can live without.

3 · Keep the pool outside the company

The tax is close to a wash: 45% personally against 27% plus 20% dividends tax on extraction. The decisive reason is exposure — cash inside the operating company is cash your landlord, your supplier and any judgment creditor can reach. It sits in your name, in your accounts, and the company's balance sheet never shows it.

4 · No debt at opening, and no surety ever

The shop opens with zero borrowings, which means no fixed instalment in the months you are learning the site. More important: no facility is signed personally. The moment a surety exists, the R4,250,000 pool is collateral again, and the structure has bought nothing.

3 · The sequence

Seven stages, in the only order that works

Each stage unlocks the next and several cannot be reordered without losing money. Nothing here needs a date on it; what it needs is the discipline not to skip forward.

  1. Four answers from the accountant. Is the price inclusive of VAT? Is it a going concern or an asset sale? Does the company qualify as a small business corporation, and what does section 12E let you write off in year one? Is there transfer duty on any property component? These four answers change the ticket by up to R326,087 before a single rate matters.
  2. Register the company, activate VAT, open the business account. All three before the purchase agreement. The vendor number is the thing you cannot retrofit.
  3. Pay the R2,500,000 from the pool, in cash. No facility, no surety, no instalment. Claim the VAT and put the credit back into the working-capital bucket where it belongs.
  4. Fund the buffer. R750,000 into the company as equity, parked same-day. R1,000,000 of the personal pool moved into the reserve account and left alone.
  5. Ladder what is left. R3,250,000 across 32-day notice and partial-access notice, at two institutions, with the reserve in a third. Blended 7.58%, R322,275 a year.
  6. Trade two quarters before you ask any bank for anything. Three tests: EBITDA at or above R125,000 a quarter, three months of cash cover, and no shareholder top-ups. Miss any of the three and the answer is not a loan, it is a better site or a tighter cost base.
  7. Then shop two, on the bank's money. R2.5m at prime + 1 or better, 60 months, no early-settlement penalty, secured on the second site's cash flow — inside the same company, so the tax scale survives.
4 · The five rules

What you agree to now, so you do not argue with yourself later

1 · The pool never guarantees the company

No surety, no pledge, no cession of the investment accounts. A bank that needs your cash as collateral is telling you it does not believe the shop can repay — believe it.

2 · One company until the tax scale runs out

Shops two, three and four go into the same company while turnover stays under R20m. Restructure only when the scale is exhausted or a lender demands a special-purpose vehicle.

3 · Debt buys proof, never potential

A loan is only ever raised against a unit that has already delivered two quarters of the return it promised. The first shop is funded by equity because it has nothing to prove yet; the second is funded by debt because it does.

4 · Twenty percent or no second site

Every site must clear a 20% pre-tax return on its R2,500,000 of capex. Below that the machine still works, but it works slowly — and a slow machine with fixed instalments is how operators lose the first shop.

5 · The reserve is not an investment

R1,000,000 earns 7.29% when 7.75% is available. That is not a mistake; it is the price of same-day access. Never ladder it, never lock it, never lend it to the company.

6 · The ladder is re-read every quarter

Rates move at every MPC. Re-price the ladder each quarter and re-check the two numbers the whole plan turns on: the business lending margin and the 32-day notice rate.

The ideal structure ends five years R971,454 ahead of paying cash and stopping, and R1,063,612 ahead of the borrow-and-invest plan you started with. It wins at every return down to 10%, which is the important part: the structure does not depend on the shop being exceptional, only on it being viable.

Two of those three numbers come from the same source — the second site. A shop earning 20% on R2,500,000 throws off R500,000 a year; the loan against it costs R293,750 in its first year and less after that. The gap between those two is the machine, and it repeats for every site you open on the same terms.

6 · The triggers that change it

What would make this structure wrong

A structure is only ideal for a size and a stage. These are the four events that end this one — know them now so the change is a decision rather than a discovery.

Turnover approaches R20m

The small business corporation scale stops applying. That is the moment to build the holding company and the per-site companies, and to accept the R88,030 of annual tax as the cost of ring-fencing. Not before.

A lender demands a special-purpose vehicle

Some funders insist the financed site sits in its own company. Price it before you accept: it costs the tax scale on that site's profit for as long as it exists.

A second site underperforms

One company means shop one's assets stand behind shop two's instalments. That is the accepted price of the R88,030 — but it means a failing site must be closed fast, not carried out of loyalty.

Prime stops being the benchmark

The SARB's proposal to replace prime with the repo rate would reprice every margin you have negotiated. Ask for the replacement-benchmark clause in writing on every facility, before signature.

7 · What breaks it

The five failures, in order of how often they happen

  1. Buying without the VAT registration. R326,087 lost in an afternoon, and unrecoverable. It is the cheapest mistake on this list to avoid and the most expensive to make.
  2. Sizing the buffer by optimism. R750,000 is four to five months of operating cost, not two. A fit-out that runs 20% over budget eats R500,000 on its own — which is why the buffer sits in the company, same-day, before the doors open.
  3. Signing the surety because the rate looked better. A hundred basis points is R25,000 a year on R2,500,000. Your pool is R4,250,000. The trade is not close.
  4. Opening shop two on a good feeling. Two quarters, three tests, then debt. The instalments start in month one of a site that has never traded, and they do not pause while it learns.
  5. Rent above 12% of turnover. At that level no financing structure saves a food site. Check the lease before you check the loan.
The one-line version

Buy the first shop with certainty, keep the pool in your own name and out of reach, take the tax the law offers before you take the rate the bank offers, and let the bank's money buy only what you have already proved.
