Two doors in Durbanville

A deli-restaurant or a kids play destination — and the two properties you already have your eye on. Who owns them, what they cost, what is actually available, and which one deserves your wife's name above the door.

The short answer

Bloemendal is the prize, and the door is not the one you thought. The farm sold this year — Rawson's own listing marked the March 2026 tender as Sold at its R180 million guide, before the lot was taken off the portal. You do not buy it. You lease the closed restaurant inside it, because a new owner sitting on 238 hectares and a 40–50 hectare development precinct needs an operator, not another lawyer. Willowbridge is the safe trade, but Matrix is not the lever — the landlord is. The Matrix shop is one bay in a centre owned by Amrich Property Group with Castleview Property Fund, and two Village units of 242 m² and 300 m² were already being marketed at R260/m² from July 2026. Ask the general manager what WN15B's lease actually says before you build a plan on a shop that may simply be a tenant having a quiet quarter.

What I would do with your money

  1. Chase the Bon Amis lease at Bloemendal first. Kitchen, terraces, parking, views and a mountain-bike park that already charges the public R75 a head to ride in. The building is standing empty and the trail café proves the foot traffic.
  2. Keep Willowbridge as the fallback, not the headline. A deli in a centre anchored by Food Lover's Market is a knife fight in a phone booth; a deli on a wine farm with bikes outside is a Saturday.
  3. Do not buy a farm to open a deli. The 57-hectare listing on the wine route at R37 million is a real option and a real millstone in the same sentence.

Chapter 01

The brief, read properly

Three ideas arrived in one sentence: a deli, a restaurant, a kids play area. They are not the same business. They have different buildings, different licences and different ways of losing money.

ConceptWhat the building must doLicences and consentsWhere it fails
Deli (retail-led, counter service)60–120 m² of frontage, visible parking, cold room, delivery accessCertificate of acceptability, health certificate, food premises registrationThin margins on resale goods; dies without passing trade
Restaurant (seated, licensed)150–600 m², commercial kitchen, extract, grease trap, ablutions, 60–120 seatsLiquor licence, health certificate, land-use consent if on agricultural landRent and labour are fixed; a wet winter empties the room
Kids play (destination)300–1,500 m², 4 m clear height indoors or a fenced outdoor yard, parent sightlines, parkingPublic liability insurance, health and safety, zoning for public assemblyWeekend-only revenue; one injury claim ends the year
Deli + restaurant + play (the actual idea)500–1,000 m² plus outdoor space, dual access, separate play exitAll of the above, plus parking and traffic impact if it growsComplexity: three cost centres, one cash flow

1.1 Two different bets

Convenience is a shop in a centre: you rent passing trade, you open when the centre opens, and you compete on price with a supermarket anchor. Destination is a place people drive to on a Saturday: you rent space, atmosphere and a reason to stay three hours. The margins are better at the destination and the risk is higher, because you are manufacturing the reason.

Bloemendal is a destination play. Willowbridge is a convenience play. They are not variations of the same decision, and the financial model in chapter 05 treats them separately for that reason.

1.2 The wife test

One practical filter before any spreadsheet: which of the two would she be happy to run on a Sunday when the staff phone in sick? A 300 m² deli in a mall is a retail shift. A farm restaurant with a play area is an events business with weather in it. Both are good businesses. They are not the same life.

2.2 The restaurant that closed

The building you saw is Bon Amis, the family restaurant on the estate — a large, child-friendly room with terraces and lawns, which the estate's own marketing described as having five event spaces. It has been listed as temporarily closed for some time; the estate's website now resolves to an empty WordPress placeholder, which is what a hospitality operation looks like when the owner is selling rather than trading.

The second venue, 360° Bloemendal, is a function and tasting venue higher up the property, reachable on +27 72 416 1611 and bookable through Dineplan. It has also carried maintenance closures. Two venues, one estate, both running below their capacity while the sale dragged: that is the gap.

2.3 The trail park is the traffic proof

Behind the restaurant, the mountain-bike operation is a separate business — Bloemendal Trail Park (Pty) Ltd — and it is the most useful thing on the property, because it tells you who already drives there:

  • Day permits: R75 adult MTB, R50 junior under 18, R40 trail running.
  • A café and bar on site, open to 21:00 on Thursdays for the night rides, serving beer on tap, coffee and take-aways.
  • A push-bike trail laid out so parents can watch children from the café — the exact customer your wife wants.
  • GrannyGear runs bike rental, servicing and a kids' academy from the site; BioSport runs coaching.
  • Free access for Tygerberg Mountain Bike Club members, which is one of the largest clubs in the country.

Read that as a revenue line: a trail park that charges per head, a café that already sells coffee to those heads, and a bike shop that sells them service. A deli-restaurant next door is not a cold start — it is the missing third leg.

2.5 Why it could bite

  • Agricultural zoning. A restaurant on farm land needs consent use or an existing approval; confirm which, in writing, before spending on design. The Western Cape's rural land-use guidelines treat restaurants and function venues on agricultural land as consent uses, not rights.
  • Liquor. A Western Cape liquor licence is a separate application with its own timelines; a restaurant without one is a café with better chairs.
  • Services. Water, septic, electricity and refuse on a farm are the landlord's problem until the lease says they are yours. Put a services schedule in the lease.
  • Access. Tygerberg MTB has previously reported the cement road up to the disused restaurant being closed to public access. Confirm the access route and the right to use it.
  • Sale risk. If the new owner's development starts, your terrace could become a site office. Ask for a lease term that survives a phasing plan, or a break clause with compensation.
  • Weather and season. Winter rain on an exposed hill is real. Model 60% of summer covers in July.

2.7 Deal structures, cheapest first

  1. Straight lease of Bon Amis with a fit-out contribution. Best if the new owner wants the asset trading.
  2. Lease plus turnover rent — lower base, percentage of sales. Shares the risk while the estate settles.
  3. Joint venture with the estate: they put the building, you put the brand and the operation.
  4. Management agreement for the estate's F&B, including 360°, if they want one operator across both venues.
  5. Buy a portion — the 57 ha remainder is the smallest bite, and still tens of millions. Only if the family wants land, not a deli.

3.2 Is Matrix actually dying?

This is where I have to push back on the premise, gently. Matrix Warehouse is still listed at Unit 15B, Willowbridge Village on its own Cape Town store page, with the branch number 021 949 1400. 3.3 The trade logic

Good: the catchment is the northern suburbs' most concentrated family belt, the centre has 80 stores and 14 restaurants, and the Village has its own grocery anchors. Bad: Food Lover's Market is in the same centre. A deli that sells what Food Lover's sells is a kiosk with better lighting. A deli that sells what Food Lover's cannot — prepared meals, a counter, coffee, a sit-down plate, a butcher's cut you cannot get there — is a different business.

The centre also imposes shopping-hours trading. A restaurant that wants to serve dinner until 22:00 needs that in writing, not inferred from the mall's closing time.

3.4 What to ask the general manager

  • What is the lease expiry and renewal status of Unit WN15B, and is Matrix in arrears or in a renewal negotiation?
  • Are the 242 m² and 300 m² units still available, and are either of them WN15B?
  • Is there a restaurant consent for that line of shops, and what extraction and grease-trap work is permitted?
  • What is the trading-hours envelope, and what signage rights come with the bay?
  • What fit-out contribution or rent-free period is the landlord offering on a five-year term?

4.4 Pricing signals to design against

  • Play: R100/hour at the small farm venue, R190 for a full day at the big one. A farm destination can hold R80–R120 per child for a two-hour slot without argument.
  • Parties: the market runs from about R299 per child for a private-room package to R5,950 for venue hire, depending on what is included.
  • Deli basket: the northern suburbs will pay for prepared food and provenance; they will not pay a premium for the same bananas as Food Lover's next door.
  • Restaurant: breakfast and lunch carry the family trade; dinner is a separate fight and needs a liquor licence and parking.
Chapter 05

The money

Two models, both built from the rents and rates above and from named assumptions. These are planning numbers, not quotes — the fit-out figures need three contractors before anyone signs anything.

5.1 The build benchmark

New retail construction in Cape Town runs at about R15,255 per m² excluding VAT and professional fees, per the AECOM 2025/26 base rates as published by Setout's Cape Town cost guide (retail/shop, worked example at 400 m²). A fit-out inside an existing shell should come in below that; a kitchen-heavy restaurant fit-out often does not. I have modelled fit-out at R7,500–R9,000 per m² and flagged it as the number most likely to be wrong.

5.5 What would change the answer

  • Rent. At Willowbridge, every R20/m² on 300 m² is R72,000 a year straight off the bottom line.
  • Covers, not spend. A deli dies of too few transactions, not of small baskets. Model 120 covers a day before you model a bigger menu.
  • Play attach rate. If only one in five restaurant tables buys a play session, the play zone is a cost centre with a fence.
  • Weather. A farm site needs 60% of summer trade in winter to hold the base case. If it cannot, the lease needs a seasonal rent step.
  • Staff. Fourteen people at Willowbridge and roughly eighteen at Bloemendal; both are guesses until a labour schedule exists.
The honest comparison: Bloemendal returns more on the same capital because the play stream is high-margin and the building already exists. It also carries a sale, a zoning question and the weather. Willowbridge is the lower-variance business with the lower ceiling — and its biggest risk is not Matrix, it is Food Lover's ten metres away.

6.1 The rate context

Northern suburbs retail is asking R120 to R300 per m² across sixteen units currently marketed, with floor plates from 87 to 799 m², per Anvil Property Smith's retail listings. Willowbridge at R260/m² sits at the top of that band, which is what a centre with Woolworths and Dischem charges. A farm lease at R90/m² would be a different market entirely — which is exactly why the Bloemendal conversation is worth having.

6.2 The model that already works here

Anna Beulah Farm in Durbanville runs a petting zoo, a deli selling its own produce, kids parties and a farm stay, and rents its play space to Sensory Seekers at R100 an hour. That is your concept at small scale, ten minutes from your house, and it has survived for years. The question is not whether the market exists. It is whether you can do it bigger, with a kitchen and a wine licence, on a site with better views.

Chapter 07

What happens next

Five stages. Each one ends on a yes or a no, not on a date. Nothing gets spent before the stage before it clears.

Stage 1 — Find out who actually holds the keys

Call Rawson about Bloemendal's new owner and Bon Amis availability. Call Salome Thonnard about WN15B's lease and the two vacant units. Call the trail park about its agreement and its traffic numbers.

Exit when you can name the owner of Bloemendal, say whether Bon Amis is leasable, and quote WN15B's lease expiry from the landlord's own mouth.

Stage 2 — Price the two builds

Three shopfitting quotes on the 300 m² unit, three on Bon Amis, one kitchen equipment schedule, one extraction and grease-trap quote. Ask each landlord for a term sheet: rent, escalation, fit-out contribution, rent-free period, permitted use.

Exit when two real quotes and one signed term sheet exist, and the capex range has narrowed to within 15%.

Stage 3 — Test the concept with real people

A weekend coffee and pastry stand at the trail park, and a Saturday play-and-brunch pop-up. Count heads, count baskets, ask twenty parents what they would pay and how far they would drive.

Exit when 200 paying customers have come through a temporary setup and the average basket is known.

Stage 4 — Clear the legal ground

A land-use opinion on the chosen site, the liquor application path, the health certificate requirements, the public liability cover for the play area, and the lease clauses on services, access and development phasing.

Exit when a town planner and an attorney have both put in writing that the use is lawful and licensable.

Stage 5 — Commit, with your signature and nobody else's

Final lease, final build contract, final funding decision. I prepare the pack; you sign, pay and trade.

Exit when you have approved the capex and signed the lease. Not before.
