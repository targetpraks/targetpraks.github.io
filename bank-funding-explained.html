Bank Funding, Explained

There is no such thing as "a bank loan". There are seven distinct shapes of funding, and each one asks a different question of your business. This is the map: what each shape is, what it costs, what the bank takes as security, and which one fits which need.

Prepared for Pamela Fourie (CFO) — funding applications, multiple banks and funders
Rate anchor: SARB prime 10.75% (repo 7.25% + 350bp) · September 2026

§1

The first question: whose money is at risk?

Every funder sits somewhere on one ladder — from money you never repay at the top, down to money secured against the business itself at the bottom. The further up the ladder, the harder it is to qualify and the longer it takes. The further down, the faster and easier — but the more you pay, and the more you personally stand behind.

RUNG 1 — CHEAPEST

Grant

Non-repayable lump sum. No interest, no capital returned. Usually capped, competitive, and tied to a policy goal (jobs, youth, township, export).

YOU CARRY: nothing
RUNG 2

Blended finance

Part grant + part concessional loan in one package. The grant portion reduces how much loan you actually carry.

YOU CARRY: nothing on the grant part
RUNG 3

Soft / concessional loan

Below-market fixed rate (e.g. 5% fixed), longer terms, moratorium on repayments for the first months. The DFI route.

YOU CARRY: capital + low interest
RUNG 4

Secured asset finance

The asset bought is the security. Equipment, machinery, vehicles, fit out. Best commercial rate because the bank can repossess the thing.

YOU CARRY: capital + interest, asset pledged
RUNG 5 — DEAREST

Unsecured / cash-flow loan

No asset behind it. Priced off your trading record and projections — or off a personal surety. Highest bank rate.

YOU CARRY: capital + interest + personal risk
§2

Then: does it fund the P&L, or the balance sheet?

This is the distinction that decides the shape of the money — a lump sum that sits on your balance sheet for years, or a revolving facility that breathes with your cash flow.

Balance-sheet funding

CAPEX funding

Money for a thing you buy and keep: the extruder, the wok cookers, the fit-out, the van, the freezers. Drawn as a lump sum, repaid over a fixed term. The asset usually secures it.

  • Term loan — fixed monthly instalments, 12–84 months
  • Asset finance / instalment sale — the asset is the security
  • Commercial property finance / leasehold improvement loan
BEST WHEN → the spend is one-off, large, and the thing bought can be sold again if it fails.
P&L / cash-flow funding

Working-capital funding

Money for the trading cycle: stock, wages, the gap between paying a supplier and being paid by customers. You only pay for what you draw, and you can draw it again.

  • Overdraft — interest on the drawn balance only
  • Revolving credit facility — access again once 15% is repaid
  • Invoice discounting / debtor finance — funded against your book
  • Merchant cash advance — repaid from card sales
BEST WHEN → the need repeats and is unpredictable. Never fund a permanent asset with an overdraft.

The seven shapes you will actually be offered

These are the products Pamela will meet across the big four banks, the DFIs (SEDFA, NEF, IDC, Land Bank), and the alternative lenders. Each carries a different repayment shape.

only pay for what you draw

A pre-approved limit on your current account you can dip into and clear as cash flows. Interest runs only on the drawn balance. Most banks want 6–12 months of business banking history and 12 months of management accounts. Limits commonly R10k – R5m.

SHAPE 06Bullet

Interest-only / balloon loan

pay the interest, the capital waits

You service only the interest each month; the full capital falls due at the end of the term. It protects cash flow during a build, but it defers the hardest part — and it costs the most in total interest of any shape here.

SHAPE 07DFI direct

Development finance (SEDFA / NEF / IDC / Land Bank)

the state lending ladder

Direct lending from the development finance institutions, priced below commercial banks and structured for businesses the banks decline. SEDFA lends R50k–R15m directly; the NEF R250k–R75m; the IDC from R1m; the Land Bank for agriculture and agribusiness.

Shape

Cost

Security

Turnaround

Pricing is the point: 8–12% vs 15–20% at a commercial bank on the same ask.

§5

Worked example: what the bank does with a franchise ask

Standard Bank's franchise funding rules are the useful template, because they are public. A franchisee is expected to put in roughly 50% of the total investment in unencumbered own funds, with the bank financing the remainder — and often taking security over up to 100% of the loan.

Papa Pasta Store 1 — the shape of the ask

Entry ticket R1 800 000 = R300 000 licence + R1 500 000 capex. Under the 50% own-funds rule the bank would want to see roughly R900 000 of the applicant's own money, and finance the other R900 000.

At R600 000/month mature store revenue at a 20–25% EBITDA band, the secured structure is the one that survives a slow opening quarter. Structure to the cash flow, then optimise the rate.

§6

What goes in the pack

Every bank and funder asks for the same documents in a different order. Assemble them once and you run every application off the same shelf.

The applicant

Who you are

  1. CIPC registration and shareholding certificates
  2. Directors' IDs and a CV-style background note
  3. Personal balance sheet — assets and liabilities, signed
  4. Personal credit record and debt-to-income picture
  5. Proof of the origin of your own contribution
The business

What it earns

  1. 2–3 years of signed financial statements, or management accounts if younger
  2. 6–12 months of bank statements on the trading account
  3. Valid SARS Tax Compliance Status PIN
  4. 12-month cash-flow projection, plus 3-year projections
  5. A business plan you wrote and can defend yourself
The specific deal

What the money buys

  1. Equipment breakdown with a rand value per item
  2. Quotes or a signed agreement of sale for the assets
  3. Lease agreement or offer to lease for the premises
  4. Franchisor approval letter (pending finance), where franchise
  5. A schedule of exactly how the loan will be spent
§7

Four traps that cost real money

Funding a permanent asset on an overdraft

An overdraft is repayable on demand and reviewed annually. Fund a five-year asset with it and a single bad review can call the money while the asset is still in the store.

Mistaking the balloon for cheap

The interest-only structure has the lowest monthly and the highest total. It costs R687 500 in interest on R1m — more than double the secured loan — and leaves the full capital standing at the end.

Signing a personal surety without pricing it

Most owner-managed loans ask the director to stand surety personally, and for franchises the security demand can reach 100% of the loan. That is not paperwork — it is your house on the line. Price it as a cost of the deal, every time.

Taking the cheapest rate that ignores the ramp

A short, cheap-looking term with no moratorium can be worse than a longer, dearer one. Match the repayment schedule to the revenue ramp — a store that opens in November should not be at full instalment in December.

§8

What I would actually do — the funding priority order

Everything above is the map. This is the route. Work down this list and stop at the first rung that genuinely qualifies. The order is not a preference — it is the price of money, cheapest first. Skipping a rung costs real rand.

1

Grant — free money, ask first

Best possible

Non-repayable. Nothing to service, nothing to give back. SEDFA Asset Assist (to R250k for machinery and equipment), NYDA for owners under 35, dtic incentives, the SAB Foundation.

Ask for: Asset Assist · current dtic incentive rounds · any grant programme open now. Why first: every rand granted is a rand you never borrow — it shrinks the loan before you apply for it.

2

Blended finance — part grant, part cheap loan

Best priced

SEDFA's TREP for township and rural businesses: a grant component up to R100k plus a loan portion to R1m at 5% fixed, over 36 months, with a 3-month moratorium.

Ask for: TREP · Integrated SMME Finance. Why here: 5% fixed against a bank's 13.75% is the cheapest capital on the market — and the moratorium is exactly what an opening store needs.

3

Concessional DFI loan — the state lending ladder

Half the bank's price

SEDFA direct lending (R50k–R15m), the NEF, the IDC, the Land Bank. Reports put SEDFA pricing at roughly 8–12% against 15–20% at a commercial bank for the same ask, with no collateral on smaller amounts.

Ask for: SEDFA direct lending. Why here: a concessional loan halves your interest cost versus the bank and is built for businesses the banks decline.

4

Secured asset finance — for the CAPEX line

Cheapest commercial rate

The extruder, cookers, warmers, refrigeration, POS, the vehicle. The asset is the security, so this is the lowest commercial rate you will be offered — around prime + 1 (11.75%).

Ask for: instalment sale / asset finance, sized against the equipment breakdown. Why here: it prices the kit properly and keeps the monthly at R22 118 on R1m rather than R27 201.

5

Working-capital facility — for the trading cycle only

Right tool, narrow job

An overdraft or revolving facility for stock, wages, and the gap between paying suppliers and being paid. Interest runs only on what you draw — R4 583 a month on a R400k draw, not R11 458.

Ask for: overdraft or revolving credit, sized to your monthly turnover. Never use it to fund a five-year asset — it is repayable on demand and reviewed annually.

6

Unsecured term loan — only if 1 to 5 fail

Last resort

No asset behind it, so it carries the highest bank rate — prime + 2% to + 5% — and almost always a personal surety from a director.

Ask for: this only after the cheaper rungs have been declined in writing. Price the surety as a real cost of the deal before you sign it.

The one to refuse

An interest-only / balloon loan. It shows the lowest monthly of anything on this page and is the most expensive loan you can take — R687 500 in interest on R1m, with the entire capital still standing at the end. Only consider it with a concrete, already-agreed refinancing plan.

The three rules, if you remember nothing else

  1. Cheapest money first. Grant → blended → concessional → secured → unsecured. Always.
  2. Match the shape to the need. A permanent asset takes a term loan; a cash-flow gap takes a revolving facility. Never swap them.
  3. Judge the total, not the monthly. Convert every quote to a monthly and a total, and compare it on one line. The lowest monthly is often the dearest loan.

How to use this document

Work down the ladder in §1 and stop at the first rung the application genuinely qualifies for, then run the priority order in §8. Use §4 to sanity-check whatever a bank quotes you: convert it to a monthly and a total, and compare it on the same line. Never send an application that has not been put through §6.
