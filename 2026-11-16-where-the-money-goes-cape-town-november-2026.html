Where the money goes

Every option we looked at, on one yardstick, with the numbers that decide it. 20 candidates. 15 clear the bar, which turns out to be the easy part. The hard part is owning one of them without lying awake.

The conclusion, in four lines

  1. The bar is 5.31%, not 9.00%. The retail bond pays 9.00% and the tax takes 41% of it. Anything we buy has to beat 5.31% after its own costs, or we have done work to end up behind a bank account.
  2. Two properties pass both screens: Brackenfell Central flat at 7.20% with 426 violent contacts per 100,000, and Maitland flat at 5.70% with 813. Everything else fails one screen or the other, and the reasons are written out below.
  3. The yield is in the cheap stock, not the good addresses. The nine bargain units net a median of 7.20%; the nine well-located suburbs net a median of 3.85% and only 1 of them clears the bar. 4 of the bargain units clear the money screen and fail the safety one — the biggest number on the page, 14.40%, sits where 1 684 violent contacts are recorded per 100,000, 1.4× the median.
  4. If we want a hotel unit, the only defensible one is the one that has actually traded. The Rockefeller nets 8.92% on four years of published occupancy and needs only 42% of room revenue to match the bond. The Delaporte pays more on paper, 9.00%, but it is a guarantee, not a trading record.

01

What we are actually deciding

This is not “property versus shares”. It is a narrower question: is there anything we can buy in Cape Town that pays us more than 5.31% a year without putting the family in a place where the crime statistics say it should not be?

The yardstick matters more than the candidates. A 9.00% retail savings bond is available to anyone with a bank account, has no tenant, no levy, no maintenance and no agent. Tax at 41% takes it to 5.31%. Inflation runs at 4.30%, so in real terms the bond returns 1.01% — barely holding its place.

The first surprise is that the bar is low. 15 of the 20 candidates we priced clear it. Property in Cape Town, bought at the right price, comfortably beats a taxed bank account. That is not the interesting question.

The second surprise is that the price decides, not the property. The nine bargain units net a median of 7.20%. The nine well-located suburbs — Sea Point, Tamboerskloof, Rondebosch, Green Point — net a median of 3.85%, and only 1 of those nine clears the bar at all. Same city, same asset class, same tenant. The difference is what we paid.

You cannot buy your way into a good address on yield. The income lives in the cheap stock, and the cheap stock mostly sits where the crime statistics are worst. Two units escape that trap, and they are the whole answer.

Two findings from this that changed the conclusion:

The market prices safety almost exactly up to the point where it stops paying

Rondebosch is one of the safest precincts in the city at 149 per 100,000 — and it nets 5.19%. It misses the bond by a rounding error. Sea Point is safer still and nets 3.68% on a R4 850 000 flat.

So the safe suburbs are priced at roughly the bond rate, and the yields we want live where the violence is. That is not a market inefficiency waiting to be exploited. That is the market telling us the truth.

Total crime is the wrong measure. Sea Point ranks 33rd of 63 on total reported crime but 15th safest on violent contact. If we screened on headline crime we would have removed the safest places on the list and kept the most dangerous.

These are total returns, not yields — a different animal. They are not a substitute for rental income, but they are the measure of what we give up by owning bricks.

The wrapper is worth more than the choice of asset

A tax-free savings account takes R46 000 a year per adult, up to R500 000 each over a lifetime, from 1 March 2026. Inside it, the same investment is untaxed — the 41% haircut on the bond simply does not apply.

Put plainly: 9.00% untaxed is worth more than 5.31% taxed, and it is the same instrument. Before we buy a single building, both of us can be earning the full bond rate instead of 5.31%.

2 of the eight beat the best commercial yield. Both of those are promises rather than trading records, and only one of them has years of actual occupancy behind it — The Rockefeller reports 76% average occupancy across four years of trading. The rest — including every micro-unit — need a management deal nobody signs.

The number none of them publishes

Eight products, four investor brochures, one question: what share of room revenue does the owner keep? Not one of them states it. It is worth more than the difference between any two suburbs in this document — three points of net yield on a single product.

Ask for it in writing before anyone signs anything. If it is not in the agreement, the number you were shown was an illustration, not a term.

And the market is turning. Cape Town hotel occupancy fell from 66.6% to 62.8% while supply grew 2.7% and demand went -0.6%. RevPAR growth slowed from +14.4% to +8.2%. We would be buying into a market that is getting busier to supply and less busy to fill.

09

What we would do, in order

Staged by dependency rather than by date. Each stage is finished when its condition is true, not when a calendar says so.

Stage one

Fill the tax-free accounts before buying any building.

  • Both adults can put R46 000 a year into a tax-free savings account, up to R500 000 each over a lifetime.
  • The return is not taxed at all — no 41% haircut, no dividends tax, no capital gains tax on the way out.
  • Against a taxed bond at 5.31%, an untaxed 9.00% in the same wrapper is worth 3.69% more for doing nothing different.

Exit condition: Done when both tax-free accounts are funded to this year's limit.

Stage two

Put every candidate through the two screens, and let the losers die.

  • Screen one is money: does it beat 5.31% after its own costs?
  • Screen two is safety: is the precinct's violent-contact rate at or below the metro median of 1 224 per 100,000?
  • A candidate that fails either one is out. Not ranked lower — out. This is the whole reason the list is short.

Exit condition: Done when the shortlist holds only names that pass both, and each rejection is written down with its number.

Stage three

Buy the boring thing that survives the pessimistic case.

  • Kalkfontein (3 units) is the only residential deal that clears the bank with zero capital growth and sits under the safety line.
  • The Delaporte is the strongest paper in the hotel lane and it still clears the bank if the guarantee turns out to be gross.
  • Neither needs the market to do anything. That is the point of them.

Exit condition: Done when a written offer exists on a unit whose worst case still beats the bond.

Stage four

Only then take the commercial or listed exposure.

  • Commercial asks R1 850 000 for 100 m² before you own anything real, and the tenant pays the rates.
  • Listed property gives the same income with an exit in one click, and it is the only lane where you can change your mind on a Tuesday.
  • This is the last stage because it is the one that can wait.

Exit condition: Done when the equity allocation is set as a percentage you can both say out loud without flinching.

10

Twelve questions before anything is signed

These are the questions that separate a term sheet from a brochure. Ask all of them, in writing, and keep the answers.

01

Show me the rental pool agreement, and read me the clause about the split.

This single number decides whether a pool unit beats the bank or loses to it. If it is not in writing, it is not a term — it is a mood.

02

Is the guarantee net or gross, and who is actually guaranteeing it?

Net means the developer owes you a number. Gross means a revenue floor with your costs still underneath. And a guarantee is only worth the balance sheet behind it.

03

What is the levy, what does it cover, and how much has it risen in three years?

Three of the eight hotel sellers do not publish one. The levy is the largest cost you control least.

04

Is this unit rated residential or commercial for property rates?

From 1 July 2027 a unit let for more than half the year is rated commercial — roughly three and a half times the residential bill.

05

Who pays for the furniture, and is the pack compulsory?

On one development it is compulsory and priced on request. On others it is included. That is a real difference in what you hand over on day one.

06

What were the actual distributions for the last twelve months, not the projection?

Ask for bank statements or owner statements. A projection is an opinion with a typeface.

07

What is the vacancy rate in this building, and who else is selling?

If several owners are exiting the same block, you are being shown the door, not the opportunity.

08

What is the transfer duty or VAT position, and are there any other costs to register?

New builds are usually VAT-inclusive with no transfer duty. Resales below R1.21 million also carry none. Everything in between has a bill attached.

09

What does the selling agent earn, and from whom?

If the agent is paid by the developer, they are the developer's agent. That is allowed. It should just be said out loud.

10

What happens to my income if the operator walks away?

A hotel unit with no operator is a small flat in a strange building with a commercial rates bill.

11

Who buys this from me, and at what yield?

Your exit is someone else's entry. Work out today what they would need to earn for your price to make sense to them.

12

What is the worst realistic year, in rands?

Not the base case. The one where occupancy falls two points, the levy rises ten percent and the pool pays nothing for a quarter. Can the household carry that without selling?

What I do not know

Every model in this document has assumptions in it, and they are listed here rather than buried in a footnote. Where I chose a number, I have said so.

The two weakest points in the whole document are the commercial sample — two nodes is not a market — and the hotel splits, which nobody publishes. Both are flagged wherever they appear. Neither changes the ranking at the top of the list.

And the one thing this document cannot tell you: whether a tenant who stops paying, a levy that doubles, or a guarantee that quietly expires is something the household can absorb in a bad year. That is not a model question. It is the conversation to have before the first offer, not after.
