The central kitchen — hygiene, not finish

240 m² of industrial unit making dry pasta, nine core sauces and pre-portioned ingredients, under HACCP. This is the room where the ceiling is a hygiene surface with a legal minimum, and where nobody will ever look up at it except an inspector.

What the regulation actually asks for

R638 of 2018 — the regulations governing general hygiene requirements for food premises — requires floors, walls and ceilings to be smooth, durable, impervious, washable and in good repair, with no flaking paint, cracks or gaps that harbour dirt. It also calls specifically for decorative wall or ceiling finishes that are easy to clean. A painted plaster ceiling is smooth and it is not impervious, and in a sauce kitchen it is neither durable nor in good repair for long.

Why neither of your two options is right here

Flush plaster

It moulds, then it flakes

Nine sauce lines put steam into that room every day. Rhinolite and an emulsion paint film are porous and hold that moisture. The ceiling moulds around the kettles, the paint film lifts, and flaking paint above open product is a foreign-body hazard and an R638 non-conformance — not a maintenance note.

Stretch canvas

Right surface, wrong room

The membrane is genuinely washable and seamless, so it passes the surface test. But it is 240 m² of a finish nobody sees, at roughly R1,300/m² — and you still need fire certification and access hatches to the extraction duct, the valves and the board. Heat near the kettles is a real softness risk.

What to build instead

  • A suspended T-grid with wipeable pans over the cook line and wash area: stainless, aluminium or vinyl-faced. Every tile lifts out, so the extract duct, the isolators and the valves are reachable without a plasterer. It is the standard commercial-kitchen answer for the reason that it is the only one that survives a wash-down.
  • Moisture-resistant board with food-grade epoxy in dry storage, the packing area and the corridor, if you want the flush look where there is no steam. Coved junctions, sealed penetrations, no ledges.
  • Condensate control above the kettles — a proper extraction canopy with a grease filter and a drain, sized to the line, not a bathroom extractor. The ceiling is downstream of the canopy, not a substitute for it.
  • Hatches and a service plan at day one. Access is cheap now and expensive every time after.

The number that settles it. A washable grid costs roughly R12,000–R72,000 more than plaster across 240 m². The membrane costs about R252,000 more. You are being asked to pay a quarter of a million rand for a ceiling in a room with no customers in it. Don't.

The concept store — the ceiling is the brand

42 m² of glass-fronted mall food court, a bronze-die extruder in the window, dark surfaces, precision LED and self-lit menu boards. Here the ceiling is not a surface — it is the thing that makes the dark, expensive look work.

Answer

Stretch canvas, matte

Seamless, dead flat, no joints to crack under grazing LED light, and it goes up in one night without a gram of sanding dust in a food court.

Not gloss

Matte, not mirror

A glossy membrane turns the ceiling into a mirror of the queue and every fingerprint on the menu board. Dark and gloss together punish a small room. Matte reads as the brand, gloss reads as a nightclub.

Not plaster

Dark paint shows every joint

Joint lines, screw pops and skim waviness are invisible in white and merciless in charcoal under a grazing light. You would be paying a plasterer to build a surface the lighting is designed to expose.

Why the money works at this size

42 m² is small. The membrane premium over plaster here is about R44,000 on a fit-out that is already a six-figure number — and it buys an overnight install in a mall that charges for shutdown hours, no dust, and a ceiling that takes the downlights and linear LED after the fact instead of before the skim.

Four things to put in the order

  • The fire certificate, in writing, to the landlord. A mall food court will ask, and the answer has to name the membrane, not the brand.
  • Hatches where the services are — extract, the drying cabinet, the distribution board. A membrane that hides the isolator is a membrane you cut.
  • A real extraction canopy over the woks. The membrane is washable, but it is not a grease trap: keep the discharge away from the ceiling face.
  • An access plan for the tenant above. If the mall's slab leaks, a membrane buys you a water bag and a warning instead of a ruined service counter — but only if someone looks up.

The grow room — plaster will not fix the mould

Short answer: no. Skimming plaster onto those drywalls will not fix the mould, and in a fruiting room at 85–95% humidity it will make it worse. The mould is not living in the paint. It is living where the air meets a cold surface.

Three reasons plaster is the wrong repair

1

Gypsum paper is food

The board is faced with cellulose paper. Mould eats it. A plaster skim and a paint film slow it down and do not remove the food supply, which is why mould comes back through a fresh coat of paint.

2

The film stays wet

In a room held at 85–95% RH the paint film sits at the dew point all day. It blisters, then flakes — and flakes falling over open trays of microgreens and mushrooms are a contamination event.

3

It hides, it does not clean

Skimming over moulded board seals live spores under a skim. You have not repaired the wall; you have given the problem a nicer surface to come back through.

The physics, and it is not negotiable

Condensation happens when a surface is colder than the dew point of the air around it. In a room at 22 °C the dew point is 20.3 °C at 90% RH and 13.9 °C at 60% RH. So a microgreens room at 40–60% is forgiving, and a mushroom fruiting room at 85–95% condemns every surface below 20.3 °C — which in a converted 15–25 m² room means the single-skin wall, the roof underside, the roller door and the cold-water pipe, every winter night.

The one-line diagnosis. Find out where the mould is before spending a rand, because each location has a different fix. On the walls or ceiling → it is the dew point, insulate and seal. On the plants → it is air movement and VPD, not surfaces. On the substrate, floor or trays → it is water handling and drainage. Treating all three as one problem is why grow rooms keep moulding after a repaint.

What to do, in the order that works

In the order that stops it, cheapest leverage first. Steps 1 and 2 are the ones that actually remove the mould; the rest stop it coming back.

  1. Cut out the moulded porous material. Gypsum board paper and a mouldy paint film cannot be cleaned, only removed. Replace that board — do not skim over it. This is the step people skip and then wonder why it returns in six weeks.
  2. Seal and insulate the envelope so no surface drops below the dew point. The right product for a 15–25 m² grow room is an insulated panel system — PIR or PUR sandwich panel, foil or laminate faced, cold-room style. It insulates and vapour-seals in one move, and the face is washable. Panel material runs roughly R200–R650/m² on African import benchmarks, plus framing and install — get two local quotes; it is still cheaper than a second mould remediation.
  3. Move air across every surface. Condensation needs a stagnant boundary layer. Circulation fans that put 0.2–0.5 m/s across the walls and ceiling cost nothing to run and stop the corners going wet first.
  4. Size dehumidification to the crop, not the room. A tray of microgreens and a block of mushrooms put litres of water into the air every day. You need a unit rated on that load, with a permanent drain — a domestic plug-in box will run all day and lose.
  5. Make every surface cleanable. Coved junctions at wall-to-floor and wall-to-ceiling, no ledges, no exposed fixings, sealed penetrations. If a cloth cannot get there, mould will.
  6. Change the clean, and add a dry-down. TLF's protocol already mandates a weekly deep clean through the app — upgrade the chemistry to a peroxide-based sporicide rather than a general sanitiser, and leave a dry-down window between crops. Mould does not survive a surface that is dry for 24 hours.
  7. Log the gap, not just the humidity. The TLF app already watches temperature and humidity. Add one alarm that matters: surface temperature minus dew point, alerting as the gap falls under about 2 °C. That single parameter predicts mould before it appears, and it is the same number a commercial grower runs on. It is a small app change with a large operational payoff — and it is what makes the franchise rooms repeatable.

What to say to the franchisee. The ceiling is not the problem. The room is running a climate the building envelope was never designed for, and the mould is the building telling you so. Fix the envelope, then the finish, in that order. Anything else is a repaint with extra steps.

Three rooms, three specifications, one rule

Papa Pasta — central kitchen

Washable grid over the cook and wash zones. Plaster only where there is no steam, and then with food-grade epoxy. Never a membrane across 240 m².

Papa Pasta — concept store

Matte stretch membrane, fire certificate to the landlord, hatches where the services are. It is the cheapest part of the brand impression you are buying.

TLF — grow room

Cut out the moulded board, seal and insulate the envelope, move the air, size the dehumidifier to the crop, and add a dew-point-gap alarm to the app.

The rule underneath all three

Decide what the room is for before choosing a surface: hygiene, brand, or climate. A ceiling that is right for one is wrong for the other two, every time.
