For Lauren · Donatello · 20 minutes a day

Don's Daily Five

Five short things, every day, in the same order. About twenty minutes of work and ten minutes of bedtime reading. Same five here as at Ricardo's — that's what makes it stick.

Nothing to plan, nothing to mark, nothing to print. The book travels with him.

Don is going into Grade 4 next year, and Grade 4 is where school stops teaching you how to read and starts expecting you to read in order to learn. The reading itself is fine — what he needs now is volume and writing.

So this is deliberately boring. The same five things, in the same order, every day. He should stop needing to be told what comes next — that's the whole point of the repetition.

There's no test, no mark and no chart on the fridge. Just the five things.

The five

1

He reads out loud to you

10 minutes · the one that never gets skipped

He reads, you listen. When he gets stuck on a word, count to three and then just say it for him. Don't ask him to sound it out, don't wait him out — say the word, he carries on.

At the end, ask him one thing: "tell me what just happened." Then one more: "why?" That's it.

2

He writes three short paragraphs

15 minutes · same three headings every day

Thank you for (two sentences) · What happened (three sentences — First, Then, After that) · The story (three sentences, made up).

It's all in one notebook that lives in his school bag. The three headings never change, so he's never deciding what to write — only what to say.

3

Eight spelling words

5 minutes · the same eight all week

Look at it, say it, cover it, write it, check it. Three times each word. On Friday you say them and he writes them without looking.

The week's list is printed and lives in the back of the notebook, so it travels with him.

4

Three minutes of handwriting

3 minutes · stop at three, even if he's enjoying it

One letter family a day, ten of each, then two words that use it: c a d g q · l h b k · i j t f · m n r p · u v w y.

5

Bedtime book — his choice

10 minutes · no target, and nobody asks him about it

Whatever he wants. Dog Man, comics, a dinosaur fact book, a football annual. He reads it himself or follows along with an audio story.

Please don't test this one. It exists so reading stays something he does for himself.

What you actually have to do

Three things

  • Listen to him read, and hand him a word when he's stuck.
  • Ask him what happened, then ask why.
  • Correct one thing in his writing. One. Not the spelling, not everything — one.

And what you don't

  • No printing, no planning, no preparing.
  • No marking and no scores.
  • No reporting back to Ricardo — if a day goes wrong, it just goes wrong.

If he asks how to spell a word

Write it on a scrap of paper so he can copy it. Try not to spell it out loud — the letters don't land the same way for him, and it breaks the sentence he's holding in his head.

What he should be able to do by the end of Grade 4

These are the targets, not a report card. For context: South Africa's published minimum for the end of Grade 4 is 70 correct words a minute, and 90 by the end of Grade 5.

SkillTargetWhy it matters
Reading out loud 110 correct words a minute The 70 floor is the national minimum. 110 is where reading stops being effort and starts being useful for schoolwork.
Reading every day 400 words aloud, rising to 600
2,000–3,000 a week		 Volume is what moves a reader. Words, not minutes — minutes reward slow reading.
Stamina A 60–80 page short chapter book in a week This is the jump from picture books to real books, and it's the jump that decides Grade 4.
Picking a book 19 out of 20 words without help The easy test for "is this book right for him". Harder than that and it becomes a fight.
Understanding Tells back what he read, and answers one "why" Grade 4 asks questions about the text, not just about the words in it.
Writing Three paragraphs, 60–100 words, with a beginning, a middle and an end This is the shape Grade 4 writing is marked on.
Spelling Six of the eight words right on Friday Six is the pass. Three wrong on Monday is completely normal.
Handwriting Even letter size, sitting on the line Legible enough that a teacher isn't guessing — which is a confidence thing as much as a writing thing.

If it goes wrong

He's too tired for the whole thing

Do the short version, about 18 minutes: read out loud for 10 minutes, one sentence under each of the three headings, the eight words once. Reading out loud is never the part you cut.

He refuses

Don't argue and don't bargain. Just do the reading out loud — that's the one that matters most — and leave the rest for tomorrow. A fight costs more than a missed paragraph.

The book is too hard

Use the 19-out-of-20 test. If he's stumbling on more than one word in twenty, the book is a shelf too high and it should go back. Dog Man and comics absolutely count as reading.

Free reading, if you need more of it

Storynory has over a thousand free audio stories — he can listen and follow the words at the same time. Book Dash has free South African picture books you can download and print.

What if we miss a day?

Then it's missed. Don't double up on the next day — that turns the whole thing into homework he's behind on, and then he stops wanting to do it.

His head hurts or his eyes are sore

Stop. He gets eyestrain from reading and it's a real limit, not an excuse. Short blocks, good light, and cream paper rather than bright white. If it happens often, tell Ricardo.

Questions, or something isn't working — just message Ricardo.
