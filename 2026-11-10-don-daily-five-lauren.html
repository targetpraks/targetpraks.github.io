Start here: Monday–Friday, allow 30 minutes after school and 10 minutes of enjoyable reading at bedtime. The aim is clearer reading, confident sentences and a habit that works in both homes.

One folder travels: the journal, current book, pencil and weekly word sheet. Mark the next book page and word-set number before it changes homes. A lined notebook works just as well as the printed pages.

Friday has its own rhythm: a longer story replaces the daily journal; a short word check replaces spelling practice. At weekends, keep the enjoyable reading. No catch-up pile.

01

The five

In this order on weekdays. Steps 1–4 take 30 minutes altogether; step 5 is for bedtime.

1

He reads out loud to you

10 minutes · about 7 reading + 1 rereading + 2 talking

He reads, you listen. Give him a brief chance to use letter sounds or a familiar word part. If he is still stuck after a few seconds, say the word, let him repeat it, then reread the sentence. Avoid guessing from the picture.

Pick 2–4 sentences to reread. Model a natural voice, then let him try. Finish with: “What happened first, next and last?” and “Why do you think that? Show me a clue.”

Try roughly 300–400 words read aloud, building towards 400–600 when comfortable. These are planning guides, not quotas: keep the whole block to 10 minutes. Record pages instead of counting every word.

2

He writes three short paragraphs

12 minutes · 2 to plan + 8 to write + 2 to reread

Gratitude, what happened, a story. Say the ideas aloud first. Begin with 1–2 sentences under each heading and grow towards about 60–100 words altogether. An adult can write 2–3 needed words in a word bank. See the prompts and completed example.

3

Eight words

5 minutes · the same eight all week

Read all eight, then practise four with look, say, cover, write once, check. Revisit the same four later in the week. Same list at both homes. The weekly rhythm and all 12 sets are below.

4

Three minutes of handwriting

3 minutes · careful, comfortable, readable

Follow the letter formation taught at school. Choose two letters from today’s words, make each carefully three times, then copy one short sentence.

Example: practise b and d, then “The duck is back.” Check letter size, spaces and sitting on the line.

5

Bedtime book — his choice

10 minutes · his choice, for enjoyment

He chooses: a comic, a story or a fact book. An adult can read aloud while he listens, he can follow an audiobook, or he can read for himself.

Please don't test this one. It exists so that reading stays something he does for himself.

02

The journal, exactly

Date at the top. Three short entries on Monday–Thursday. A small drawing can help him plan or illustrate an idea.

1 · Thank you for

Build towards two sentences · notice something and explain why

I am grateful for ______. It mattered to me because ______.

2 · What happened

Build towards three sentences · past tense · order

First, ______. Then, ______. After that, ______.

3 · The story

Build towards three sentences · imagination

Someone in a place. A problem. What happens next. Pick a starter below or continue yesterday’s story.

See a complete journal entry · 78 words

An invented example, not a record of Donatello’s day.

Thank you for
I am grateful that Dad helped me fix my bike. I felt happy because we could ride together again.

What happened
First, we looked for the missing bolt in the garage. Then, I found it under a dusty box. After that, we fixed the wheel and rode to the park.

My story
A tiny dragon was hiding inside my helmet. It sneezed and turned my bike into a rocket. I grabbed the handlebars and flew over the tallest tree in the park.

More ideas for gratitude and explaining

Gratitude: Who helped you? What made you laugh? What small thing did you enjoy? Add why it mattered.

Explaining: How did you build it? How does your game work? How did you solve a problem? Use first, then and finally. This can replace “What happened” for the day.

Which one thing you correct. Choose one useful improvement together. Praise a specific idea before editing.

MON Capital letters and full stops — "every sentence starts big and ends with a dot"
TUE Joining ideas — “can you join two ideas with because, but or so?”
WED Past tense — "this happened yesterday, so walked, not walk"
THU One precise detail — “what did it look or feel like?”
FRI The Friday Story — order, and a title

Friday — the Friday Story

Replace the whole journal block with one story from the week. Plan a beginning, middle and end; then add a problem, an action and an ending.

Build towards 6–8 sentences. Use a new paragraph for each part. Keep the same 12-minute block; a clear short story is enough. Page 9 of the workbook has a planning area and writing lines.

03

The words, week by week

Six pattern words and two everyday words in each set. This is a suggested practice sequence, not a Grade 4 spelling standard. School or tutor words can replace it. If a set is already secure, move on; if still learning, repeat it. Agree the same set at both homes.

What to write each day

Monday: read all eight, then cover and write words 1–4 once.
Tuesday: read all eight, then cover and write words 5–8 once.
Wednesday: recall words 1–4 again; revisit mistakes.
Thursday: recall words 5–8 again; use one in the journal.
Friday: adult says each word, uses it in a sentence, then repeats it. Don writes it with the list covered. Check together.

Say the sounds and notice the letters that represent them. Mark any surprising part: in said, ai represents the /e/ sound. Everyday words are not all irregular.

Check, repair, revisit

After a mistake, show the correct word, notice the part to fix, cover it and try once more. Stop at five minutes and use unfinished words first next time. No rows of copying.

Choose up to two words from Friday to revisit next week. Replace up to two new words with them, rather than adding extras. Page 10 is the reusable practice-and-check sheet.

04

What he reads

Let him choose between two suitable books. As a rough guide, he should read about 95 out of 100 words without help and explain what he read. Easier books are useful too.

Funny and illustrated: try Dog Man or The Bad Guys. Comics and fact books count.

Short chapters: try Dragon Masters, Owl Diaries or Press Start! Borrow or sample a page first; choose by comfort and interest.

Free: Book Dash has stories to read online or print. No book-per-week deadline: carry on from the bookmark and enjoy finishing it.

05

What the adult does

Before: open the current book and word set; let him say his journal ideas.

During: help with unfamiliar words, ask about the meaning and keep the pace calm.

After: name one thing he did well, improve one part of the writing together, and mark the next book page.

The weekly sheet in the PDF keeps the place for both homes. A few ticks are enough. Keep completed pages in the folder so progress is visible in his own work.

06

Clear goals, with the right context

These home goals guide practice. They are not pass marks or a prediction of school readiness.

ReadingFive 10-minute sessions a weekStart around 300–400 words; build towards 400–600 when comfortable. The chat stays inside the time.
UnderstandingRetell + explain one “why”Use a clue from the text. For facts, tell two things learned and explain one.
JournalBuild towards 60–100 wordsStart with 1–2 sentences per heading; grow towards 2 + 3 + 3. Say the ideas first.

What the published Grade 4 benchmarks actually say

Writing: English Home Language CAPS lists Grade 4 essays of 100–120 words in 2–3 paragraphs, and short stories of 120–140 words in 3–5 paragraphs. These are school task lengths to build towards, not daily journal quotas. Source: DBE CAPS.

Reading: the DBE’s English First Additional Language benchmark is 70 correct words per minute at the end of Grade 4, developed largely from no-fee-school data. It is not a universal English Home Language standard or proof of readiness. Ask the teacher to interpret fluency, accuracy and comprehension together. Source: DBE benchmarks.
