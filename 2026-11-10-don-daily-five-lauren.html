For Lauren · Donatello · Grade 4 prep

Don's Daily Five

Five things, every day, in the same order. Twenty minutes after school, ten at bedtime. The same five at our house as at Ricardo's — that is what makes it stick.

10 min read aloud 15 min journal 5 min words 3 min letters 10 min bedtime
The five The journal The words Targets Print pack

Don goes into Grade 4 next year, and Grade 4 is where school stops teaching you how to read and starts expecting you to read in order to learn. The reading is fine — what he needs is volume, and a lot more writing.

So this is deliberately boring. The same five things, in the same order, every day. He should stop needing to be told what comes next, and that is the whole point of the repetition.

There is no test, no mark, and no chart on the fridge. Nothing to plan, nothing to prepare, nothing to print — except the pages in the print pack, if you want them.

01

The five

In this order, every day. One to four after school. Five at bedtime.

1

He reads out loud to you

10 minutes · the one that never gets skipped

He reads, you listen. When he gets stuck on a word, count to three and then just say it for him. Don't ask him to sound it out and don't wait him out — say the word, he carries on.

At the end, ask one thing: "tell me what just happened." Then one more: "why?" That's the whole of it.

2

He writes three short paragraphs

15 minutes · the same three headings every day

Grateful, what happened, a story. About eighty words all told. The headings never change, so he is never deciding what to write — only what to say. The exact wording is below.

3

Eight words

5 minutes · the same eight all week

Look, say it, cover it, write it, check it — three times each word. Same list at both houses, in the same week. All twelve weeks are below.

4

Three minutes of handwriting

3 minutes · stop at three, even if he's enjoying it

One letter family a day, ten of each, then two words that use it:

c a d g q · l h b k · i j t f · m n r p · u v w y

5

Bedtime book — his choice

10 minutes · no target, and nobody asks him about it

Whatever he wants. Dog Man, comics, a dinosaur fact book, a football annual. He reads it himself, or follows along with an audio story.

Please don't test this one. It exists so that reading stays something he does for himself.

02

The journal, exactly

Three headings, in this order, every day. Date at the top. One drawing allowed — in the margin, after the writing, never instead of it.

1 · Thank you for

Two sentences · gratitude

One thing I am grateful for today is ______ because ______.

2 · What happened

Three sentences · past tense · order

First, ______. Then, ______. After that, ______.

3 · The story

Three sentences · imagination

Take one starter from the list, or carry on yesterday's. Three sentences. Give it a title if you want.

Which one thing you correct. One per day, never more. Everything else that is wrong today gets left alone, on purpose.

MON Capital letters and full stops — "every sentence starts big and ends with a dot"
TUE Joining words — "can one sentence start with because or but or so?"
WED Past tense — "this happened yesterday, so walked, not walk"
THU One describing word per paragraph — "what did it look or feel like?"
FRI The Friday Story — order, and a title

All fifteen story starters. One a day, no repeats inside a fortnight. He can also carry the same story for three or four days — that's better than starting fresh, because it makes him hold a thread.

  1. The day the LEGO city came alive.
  2. A robot that only speaks in football scores.
  3. The drum that nobody was allowed to hit.
  4. I found a door behind the bookshelf.
  5. The night the moon went out.
  6. My dog learned one word and used it against me.
  7. The smallest dinosaur in the world.
  8. We swapped places for a day.
  9. The treehouse had one more room than we built.
  10. A goalkeeper who was afraid of the ball.
  11. The puddle that was a portal.
  12. Everything I touched turned to jelly.
  13. The last child on Earth, and the Wi-Fi still worked.
  14. A snail that wanted to win a race.
  15. The secret the ants were keeping.

Friday — the Friday Story

Instead of paragraph three, he takes the best story of the week and writes it again, longer: six to eight sentences, a beginning, a middle and an end, and a title.

It is the only piece each week that gets rewritten, and it is how three sentences turn into a real story by the end of the year. There is a page for it in the print pack.

Five steps, five minutes

Look at the word and say it. Cover it. Write it from memory. Uncover and check. If it is wrong, do it once more and move on.

Friday: you say the eight words, he writes them with no looking. Whatever he gets right is what he got right — no mark, no score, no "you should have got that one". Three wrong on Monday is normal. One on Thursday is normal.

04

What he reads

He picks. The only rule is the nineteen-out-of-twenty test: he should be able to read nineteen words in twenty on a page without help. Harder than that and it becomes a fight; easier than that and there is nothing to learn.

All of these count: Dog Man and anything by the same author, Captain Underpants, The Bad Guys, Narwhal and Jelly. Pictures do half the work and two hundred pages go past in a week — that is not a lesser book, it is the book that makes a reader.

For the step up: short-chapter series with big type and a picture every couple of pages — Dragon Masters, Owl Diaries, Usborne Young Reading. One chapter a night is a full read on this shelf.

Free: Storynory has over a thousand audio stories — he can listen and follow the words with his finger. Book Dash has South African picture books you can download and print.

05

What you actually have to do

This is genuinely all of it.

Three things

  • Listen to him read, and hand him a word when he is stuck.
  • Ask him what happened, then ask why.
  • Correct one thing in his writing. One. Not the spelling, not everything.

And what you don't

  • No printing, no planning, no preparing.
  • No marking, no scores, no chart on the fridge.
  • No reporting back to Ricardo — if a day goes wrong, it just goes wrong.
  • Don't spell words out loud. Write it on a scrap for him to copy — the letters don't land the same way for him, and it breaks the sentence he is holding in his head.
06

What he should be able to do by the end of Grade 4

These are the targets, not a report card. For context: South Africa's published minimum for the end of Grade 4 is 70 correct words a minute, and 90 by the end of Grade 5.

SkillTargetWhy it matters
Reading out loud 110 correct words a minute The 70 floor is the national minimum. 110 is where reading stops being effort and starts being useful for schoolwork.
Reading every day 400 words aloud, rising to 600
2,000–3,000 a week		 Volume is what moves a reader. Words, not minutes — minutes reward slow reading.
Stamina A 60–80 page short chapter book in a week This is the jump from picture books to real books, and it is the jump that decides Grade 4.
Picking a book 19 out of 20 words without help The easy test for "is this book right for him".
Understanding Tells back what he read, and answers one "why" Grade 4 asks questions about the text, not just about the words in it.
Writing Three paragraphs, 60–100 words, with a beginning, a middle and an end This is the shape Grade 4 writing is marked on.
Spelling Six of the eight words right on Friday Six is the pass. Three wrong on Monday is completely normal.
Handwriting Even letter size, sitting on the line Legible enough that a teacher isn't guessing — which is a confidence thing as much as a writing thing.
