Part one

The ask, and what it actually is

You sent a reel of a café owner running four little tools on one tablet, and said we need something for the internal kitchen that does our recipes. Here is the build, and here is what I am deliberately leaving out of it.

It is one small system, not a platform. The recipe manual becomes the database; the tablet is the front door.

Three jobs, in this order. First, tell the cook what to make right now and how to make it, at the bench, with the machine running and the wifi off. Second, prove what was actually made — who, when, how much, and whether it passed the cook test. Third, surface what is about to run out before service finds out for us.

Why those three. The reel's four tools are the right shape and the wrong spec. Its prep recipes and its prep inventory are the two that earn their keep in a pasta kitchen; the hourly tidy nag and the "email the owner" alert are the two its own comment section argued against, and I agree with the commenters.

What it is not. It is not the SOP and training record — you decided on 26 October that Zoho Learn holds that, with a name and a timestamp per version, and that decision stands. It is not a POS, a payroll system, or an invoice scanner. It does not replace the costed model, which keeps the master percentages.

The one-line product. A kitchen that can open one screen and answer: what do I make now, how, and what is short — without asking a person who is not there.

Why a real system and not a shared folder of PDFs. The manual already says "record all results in the batch production log; flag any deviation to the franchise QC lead". That instruction has no system behind it today. A folder cannot hold a batch log. It cannot hold a flag raised at 07:20 by a cook who has since gone home. It cannot tell you that the sorghum bin was empty on Tuesday and nobody noticed until service.

Part two

Two laws that decide the whole build

Everything in Part four onward follows from these two sentences. If we get them wrong, the software is either a leak or a toy.

Law one — percentages stay in the costed model; the bench gets weights for one batch

The formula is the thing an ex-employee could copy. On 26 October you set the rule: method in the manual, arithmetic in the model, NDA over both. A tablet that can show a baker's-percentage column, or export a full recipe, undoes that rule in one screenshot. So the bench screen shows only the weighed amounts for the batch size being made — no percentage column, no export, no share button, every printed batch card stamped with the batch id and the person.

That is a real constraint on the software, and it has a cost: the scale engine has to be exact, because nobody at the bench can check it against the percentages. It gets a reconciliation line instead — the weights must sum to the batch total, and the screen shows that sum.

The worked example the engine must pass

Two ranges appear in this one recipe — water and salt. The app must not pick a number silently. It asks, at batch start, and records the reason ("tender noodle, top of range"), because the manual says the top of the water range is what makes a tender fresh noodle and the barrel must never exceed 55 °C.

Law two — offline is the normal state, not an error state

Flour on the screen, gloves on, a cold room with no signal, and a store whose line is the only thing between a batch and a lost day. Any design that needs a round trip to a cloud to tell a cook the salt weight is the wrong design.

So: the app is installed on the tablet, its data lives on the tablet, and it writes to the tablet first. When it sees the network it pushes up. Every bench action carries a client-generated id, so a queued write that gets replayed twice is still one batch — not two, and not a doubled stock decrement.

This is not a greenfield guess. Four formulas are already verified, costed and written as a method — in a document a cook cannot use at a machine.

All four run on the same machine: Gustotek GT Line GUSGT108, 16–20 kg/h, so a 10 kg batch extrudes in roughly 30–40 minutes. Surface-dry moisture spec is 28–32% — the fresh spec, not the 12.5% dry spec. The cook test is al dente in 3–5 minutes with no mushy exterior.

The method is already written, per recipe

Each one has a real sequence we can read straight into the app: dry blend 10–15 minutes with a three-point sample check; knead in the extruder with the barrel at 40–55 °C, never above 55; extrude at maximum pressure on a bronze die with active cooling and cut immediately; surface-dry on covered mesh racks with fan-assisted airflow; store-cook at 1 L water per 100 g and 10 g salt per litre, reserve 100 ml pasta water, serve immediately.

The four gaps this system closes

1 · Nobody opens a document at a machine

A 54 KB HTML manual is a thing you read once. At 06:40, at the extruder, with the water already measured, a cook needs one number per line and a next step. Nothing today does that.

2 · The batch log is a sentence, not a system

The manual requires a production log and a deviation flag. There is no log. So there is no yield history, no variance, and no defence on the day someone asks who ran the batch that came out wrong.

3 · Empty bins are discovered by service

Baobab, flax, lupin, inulin, the TLF powders — each one is a stop-work item. A cook notices, tells someone verbally, goes home, and the next person starts the batch and finds out.

4 · Scaling is mental arithmetic

A store that needs two batches does 2× in its head, including water ranges and salt. That is where a formula drifts — not in the percentages, in the multiplication.

Part four

The model, and the ten rules that make it a kitchen system

Twenty small tables, no cleverness. The rules are the product — a system that breaks one of these is a notes app with extra steps.

The entities

Supplyingredient · supplier · price_history · stock_receipt · stock_count · stock_flagWhat we buy, from whom, at what price, and what the kitchen believes it has
Workbatch · batch_ingredient_lot · prep_template · prep_run · checklist_template · checklist_runWhat was made today, what needs making, what needs cleaning

The ten rules

#RuleWhy it is a rule and not a preference
I1A published version never changes. Editing a live recipe creates a new version; every batch pins the version id it ran.Six months from now, "what did we actually make in March" must have one answer.
I2Weights are computed, never stored. Weight = baker's % of total dry × scale, rounded to unit precision, and the sum is displayed and must reconcile.The bench card has no percentage column, so the sum line is the only check a cook has.
I3Ranges stay ranges. Water 32–34% and salt 1–1.5% are bench decisions; the batch records the point chosen and a reason.A silent midpoint is how two stores end up making two different pastas.
I4A batch cannot close without its QC checks — cook test result, and a deviation note when anything was substituted.This is the paper instruction from the manual, made into a gate instead of a wish.
I5Stock is derived, never hand-decremented. Receipts minus closed batches plus counted corrections. A flag is a claim with a name and a time.The reel's inventory tool asks staff to decrement by hand; the first person in a hurry makes the number a lie.
I6Nothing is deleted. Recipes retire, versions archive, corrections are new events.A batch log that can be edited is not a batch log — it is a story.
I7Offline is the default. Bench writes land locally with a client id; replay is idempotent.See Law two. A doubled batch on a retry is a stock error you will chase for a week.
I8No version goes live without allergens and nutrition attached.Chickpea already carries a note: declare it, and add "may contain traces of peanut" where co-processing happens.
I9Nudging is bounded — a per-station interval, a quiet window, and one escalation. Never a loop.The most-liked comment on the source reel was "please remove that tidy check, it's going to drive your staff crazy". It was right.
I10Agents may draft; humans publish. Anything AI-drafted is labelled and cannot be set live by an agent.The kitchen's word is the legal record. A model does not sign a food-safety document.

Part five

The six surfaces a cook and a manager actually touch

Six screens, nothing more, in the order the day meets them. Each one answers a question in under ten seconds or it is off the build.

1 · Bench — make it now

Today's production plan, biggest tap targets on the tablet. Tap a recipe and you get the batch card: the scaled weights for the batch size chosen, the method steps one at a time, a timer per step, the QC check at the end, and a close button that will not work until the check is answered.

Question: what do I weigh and what do I do next?

2 · Prep board — what is short

Every open flag: what ran out, who said so, when, at which station. A cook claims it, makes it, and the flag closes itself. No flag is a question for the owner — it is a task for the next person on shift.

Question: what needs making before the next batch?

3 · Batch log — prove it

One row per batch: recipe, version, scale, operator, start and end, weights used, cook test result, deviations. This is the screen an inspector or a franchisee reads, and the screen that makes yield variance visible.

Question: what did we make, and did it pass?

4 · Checks — cleaning and opening

Opening, hourly or two-hourly station checks and closing, each stamped with a person and a time. Bounded nudging (I9): a due check appears once, waits out its interval, and escalates once — it never chases a human in a loop.

Question: what is due, and who did it?

5 · Alerts — broken, low, help

A one-tap raise from any station. It goes to the manager on shift, on their phone, and escalates after a set time if unclaimed. Not an email to an owner's inbox at 19:00 — the reel's version of this sends mail and calls it an alert.

Question: who needs to know, right now?

6 · Manager — cost and truth

Cost per portion from live ingredient prices against the version's weights, yield variance per batch, flags raised versus closed, and the week's production against plan. Reads the same tables, no second calculation.

Question: are we making money on this plate?

Part six

Build or buy, with the real prices

Three credible products already do recipe management for restaurants. All three are cloud-first, priced per location or per user, and none of them is shaped like a bench card for one pasta line.

Apicbase No price on its plans page — quote-gated. A third-party listing shows "from $330 per user, per month"; treat that as unverified. — Genuinely strong recipe and allergen engine, multi-outlet by design, priced and scoped above one kitchen's need.
Galley No public price — sales-gated—Commissary-grade culinary resource planning. We would be buying a system to manage suppliers we have not signed yet.
Our own buildNo licence. A small codebase, one server per store, one tablet. Running cost target near R0 — the store's own hardware The only option where the bench screen, the offline rule and the formula law are design requirements rather than a feature request.

The honest arithmetic. One location on MarketMan Starter for three years is $8,964 — R149,239. The commissary plan, which is the closest match to a central kitchen feeding stores, is $499/mo: three years at R299,077. That is the number our build is measured against, and it is not the reason to build. The reason is Law two and Law one: no cloud on the bench, and no percentage column on a screen staff can photograph.

Where buying still wins, and we should say so. If the requirement were purchasing, invoice scanning, supplier EDI and POS actual-vs-theoretical variance across six stores, MarketMan at R4,145/month would be cheaper than anything we build. This PRD is deliberately not that product. If v1 lands well and the stores multiply, the honest question returns — and the answer will be evaluated on the invoice side, not the recipe side.

Part seven

The build order, by dependency

Stages, not a calendar. Each stage is small enough to demo in a day and has an exit test that can fail in front of you.

StageWhat shipsExit test — the thing that must be true
0 · Recipe truthThe four verified recipes loaded as versions: ingredients, method steps, QC checks, allergens, nutrition, timings and costs from the Fresh-Daily manual. A script renders every recipe's 10 kg and 40 kg batch card, and the weights match the manual to the gram — including the water and salt ranges.
1 · Bench card + scaleThe tablet screen, installed, working offline. Batch size picker, weights, one-step-at-a-time method, timers. Airplane mode on. A cook who has never seen the app makes one batch of Bokgat from the screen, and the 30 kg card reconciles to 30.00 kg.
2 · Batch close + stockThe QC gate, the batch log, and derived stock. Closing a batch is the only thing that moves a bin. Close a batch, kill the tablet mid-sync, bring it back: one batch, one decrement. Not two.
3 · Prep board + flagsRaise, claim, complete. The manager sees a flag on their phone inside two seconds on the store network, and it survives a sync. Raise flax as low at 07:20, close the tablet, open it at 13:00 — the flag is there, still owned, still timed.
4 · Checks + alertsOpening, interval station checks, closing. One escalation path, quiet window, no nag loop. Leave a check unanswered for a full shift: it appears exactly twice — once due, once escalated — never more.
5 · Manager costingCost per portion from live ingredient prices, yield variance per batch, flags raised versus closed, production versus plan. Change the baobab price and the cost per 140 g serving of Bokgat moves by exactly the expected amount, with the old costing still readable.
6 · Multi-storeOne instance per store, a central roll-up for production and stock, formula percentages never leaving the central model. Pull a store instance off the network for a day: it keeps producing batches, and the central view backfills without duplicates.

Where the store-opening date bites. Store one opens on 13 November 2026. Stages 0 to 2 are the ones that make the first week survivable — the recipes in the cook's hand and a log that exists — and they do not depend on anything after them. Stages 3 to 6 can land behind live production. I would not hold the opening for this software; I would make sure the tablet in the kitchen is not showing a PDF on day one.

Part eight

What only you can decide

Six questions. Five of them change what gets built; one of them is money and is therefore yours by rule.

#DecisionMy recommendation
D1Does v1 serve the central kitchen only, or store one as well?Both, in the same build: the central kitchen is the test bench, store one is the proof. Same code, one extra instance.
D2Does the bench card show gram weights only, or grams plus baker's percentages?Grams only. Percentages stay in the costed model — that is the 26 October rule, and it is what makes the tablet safe to lose.
D3Which tablet, and who signs for it? moneyA 10-inch Android tablet in a splash-proof wall case, one per kitchen. Your call and your card — I will bring you two options with prices.
D4Who may publish a recipe version?You, plus one named operations person. Everyone else proposes; two names go live.
D5Is the cleaning check hourly, two-hourly, or per station at set times?Per station at set times, with closing as the hard check. Hourly nagging is the one thing the reel's own audience rejected.
D6Do ingredient prices come from supplier invoices in v1, or are they typed in?Typed in, with a price history. Invoice capture is a different product and it is the one worth buying later, not building now.

If you only answer two: D2 and D4.

Those two decide how much of the formula leaves the safe and who can put a version in a cook's hand. Everything else in this document I can move on without you, and will.
