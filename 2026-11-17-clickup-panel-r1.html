The ClickUp panel — five answers in full

Five models, one evidence pack, no cross-reading. Round one verbatim, then the chair's signed verdict. Verdict summary: ClickUp, judged.

Chair · Kimi K3.1 Pro — the panel verdict

1. Right and protect

The panel’s unanimous verdict: no-go for a 40-person rollout in its current state. This is an uninhabited scaffold with one live reporting workload, not an operating system. The failure is not primarily cosmetic: ownership is absent, REP cannot execute its documented close, and an automation is corrupting due dates.

Protect these elements:

  • One Space per operating unit, with separate REP, EXCO and MANCO spaces. These are useful ownership and governance boundaries. Do not consolidate the business into one giant space.
  • REP as the single reporting system of record. One list per cadence, period as parent task and unit as sub-task gives every unit a specific obligation for every period. The 116 parents and 1,624 sub-tasks are worth preserving, although the design is not yet operationally proven.
  • The six workspace-level fields. Brand, Business Unit, Requested By, Engagement Tier, Work Format and SLA Service Tier are the correct shared reporting dimensions. Define once and reuse; do not recreate them per space.
  • The distinction between Briefed-In, Recurring and Projects. Those work formats have genuinely different operating behaviour.
  • Controlled workflow variation. FXL’s scoping/UAT/live stages can be legitimate software-delivery stages. Do not impose identical statuses everywhere merely for visual consistency.
  • The brief as the intake contract and the three public announcement/reporting channels.

The genuine disagreement is over the tier hierarchy. Some panellists would protect HOF/ZOR/COR/ZEE as part of the fixed skeleton; the stronger position is to protect tier as data, not as a mandatory folder dimension. The full tier × brand × discipline × work-format cross-product is precisely what produced MKT’s 169 empty lists.

2. Structurally wrong

  • There is no ownership system. The workspace has one member and 14 empty groups. Team assignment, group mentions, accountable unit ownership and team-based notification cannot operate. DID has a team without a space; NVP has a space without a team; the team cap prevents simply completing the diagram.
  • REP cannot perform the documented close. No Submitted or Reviewed state exists, and REP has no saved submission queue, review board, exception view or dashboard. Ricardo cannot reliably distinguish missing, drafted, submitted and reviewed reports without opening tasks and chasing people.
  • A live automation is falsifying commitments. Overwriting new tasks’ due dates with “today + 5” destroys the distinction between a real deadline and an arbitrary timestamp. This is active data corruption.
  • The intake hierarchy will cause misfiling. MKT alone contains 45% of the workspace’s lists. Several plausible homes for the same request guarantee inconsistent filing, broken workload views and unreliable roll-ups once real work arrives.
  • REP has competing sources of truth. BUS Unit and Business Unit will split filters. Twelve narrative fields duplicate description content, allowing contradictory versions of the same report. Cancelled Quarter fields and _probe_* fields pollute every field picker and view builder.
  • The operating contract is absent from the workspace. Zero docs means staff cannot discover the filing, ownership, reporting or escalation rules where they work. Fifty-three private channels will instead hide requests and decisions in conversations.
  • The platform constraints make sequencing important. Statuses require manual space-settings work; teams cannot simply be deleted or extended; field deletion is unavailable through the API. These are reasons to stop building more structure now.

The panel disagrees about whether REP’s junk fields are permanently undeletable. The evidence does not establish permanence—only that API deletion is unavailable and the UI could not be opened headlessly. Hide and deprecate them immediately; attempt manual UI deletion where supported. Do not let field cleanup delay the operational fixes.

It also disagrees about adding Submitted/Reviewed to all 15 spaces. REP first is the right decision. Those states implement REP’s reporting handshake; they are not automatically appropriate stages for every delivery unit.

3. What fails first at 40 users

Work routing and ownership fail on day one. If staff are invited without repairing the ownership model, team assignment and @fin/@mkt-style mentions still reach nobody, unit accountability is undefined, and most chat channels remain inaccessible. Staff find hundreds of empty lists rather than a clear queue of owned work.

Misfiling and unreliable deadlines follow immediately. MKT users will choose different lists for equivalent briefs; other units will improvise around empty shells. New tasks receive fabricated due dates, so workload and deadline views cannot be trusted.

The first reporting close is the visible organisational failure. Owners cannot perform a reliable Submitted handoff, Ricardo has no Submitted-but-not-Reviewed queue, and missing or late units are not surfaced as exceptions. The close degenerates into chat chasing and manual inspection of unit sub-tasks. Leadership will blame adoption, but the system never implemented ownership, state control or exception management.

On sequencing, four panellists put membership first and one put the automation first. I back immediate automation containment, followed immediately by a staffed pilot—not a big-bang invitation to all 40. The automation takes minutes to contain and is actively damaging data; membership is the first structural change because nothing else operates without actors.

4. Five highest-leverage changes, ranked

  1. Disable the due-date automation, freeze broad task creation, and repair affected REP dates.

This comes first because it stops continuing corruption. Building more work or reporting on fabricated deadlines only increases the repair bill.

  1. Establish membership and accountability, then run a controlled pilot.

Resolve the DID/NVP mapping and team-cap constraint, confirm the plan supports the intended membership model, populate the required groups, name every unit owner and appoint a backup reviewer for Ricardo. Start with Ricardo and two unit owners; verify assignment, mentions, notifications and channel access before expanding. This outranks workflow polish because no workflow can operate without identifiable actors.

  1. Turn REP into an exception-driven period-close system.

Manually configure Draft/In Progress → Submitted → Reviewed in REP. Build an owner submission queue, Ricardo’s Submitted-but-not-Reviewed queue, a period view exposing missing, late and blocked units, and an EXCO/MANCO dashboard. Use Submitted By, Reviewed By, audit history and operating procedure to control review; a status name is not a permission. Run one complete weekly close before broader rollout. This is third because REP is the only live business process and the governance loop depends on it.

  1. Collapse the intake hierarchy before real work arrives.

Retain unit Spaces, but create only the Briefed-In, Recurring and Projects lists each unit actually uses. Reduce MKT drastically and use Brand, Tier, Function and discipline as fields and saved-view dimensions rather than a prebuilt folder cross-product. Archive empty shells. This comes before data-contract polish because misfiling becomes expensive as soon as habits and real tasks exist.

  1. Apply and publish one minimum operating contract.

Standardise on one Business Unit field, native Priority and Due Date, the shared metadata fields and a controlled brief template. Keep report narrative in the description template; hide duplicate, cancelled and probe fields from active templates and views. Publish one concise workspace doc covering intake, ownership, deadlines, review and escalation, and require commitments and decisions to land on tasks rather than chat. This sustains the working design; it cannot substitute for the first four changes.

5. The one thing everyone would get wrong

They would tidy the workspace and still confuse task completion with governance. REP must operate as a state machine: a unit sub-task is that unit’s obligation for a specific period; Completed is not a proxy for Reviewed; and the period parent cannot close until every required unit is reviewed or explicitly excused. Missing and late submissions must remain visible exceptions. A cleaner hierarchy with prettier dashboards but ambiguous close states would produce exactly the same unreliable governance.

On Monday morning, the CEO should instruct Ricardo to disable the due-date automation, freeze further task creation and audit the dates it changed; cancel the planned 40-person invitation; appoint two unit owners and a backup reviewer; resolve the team-cap and DID/NVP decisions; then manually configure REP’s Submitted/Reviewed workflow and submission, review and exception views and run one complete weekly close with those two owners. Only after assignments, mentions, deadlines and that close all work should Infinity Brands invite staff in stages and begin collapsing MKT and the other empty intake shells.

Verdict: a beautifully drawn map of a country with no roads, no citizens, and no border control.

This workspace is a schema with zero operating reality. 370 of 373 lists are empty, one human exists, every team has zero members, and the single space that carries actual work (REP) has no views, no reporting statuses, and 40 custom fields of which roughly a third are junk. 1. What is structurally right — protect it

  • One Space per operating unit, engagement tier as the folder spine, work format (Briefed-In / Recurring / Projects) as the list layer. This is a genuinely good taxonomy. It maps the business, not someone's org chart fantasy. Keep it.
  • The six workspace-level fields (Brand, Business Unit, Requested By, Engagement Tier, Work Format, SLA Service Tier) are the correct layer for cross-space reporting. That they exist at workspace level rather than being re-created per space is the one sign of real architectural thinking here.
  • The REP cadence model — period as parent task, unit as sub-task, one list per cadence — is sound and already proven: 116 tasks, 1,624 sub-tasks built in a day. The reporting *rhythm* works; it's the reporting *surface* that doesn't.
  • Naming discipline (BU 01 FIN, consistent handles, @fin/@exco groups). The names are right even though the groups are hollow.

2. What is structurally wrong — will break under load

  1. The Submitted/Reviewed handshake does not exist. The documented governance model ("owner sets Submitted, only Ricardo sets Reviewed") has no status vocabulary anywhere. The one process this workspace was built to enforce cannot run. And statuses can't be written via API — this is a manual, per-space settings change across 15 spaces, and it hasn't been done even once.
  2. MKT is a structural failure, not a style choice. 68 folders, 169 lists — 45% of the workspace — replicating 8 disciplines × 4 tiers × 3 brands. At 40 users, nobody will find anything; every discipline lead will fight the taxonomy; and ClickUp list views degrade badly past a few hundred lists per space. This is premature dimensional modelling. Work should enter flat and be sliced by custom fields (which already exist at workspace level!) — the tier/discipline matrix should be *views*, not folders.
  3. The REP custom-field pile. 40 fields, including BUS Unit vs Business Unit (same thing twice), a cancelled Quarter cadence, three _probe_ test fields, and twelve text fields duplicating the task description. Fields cannot be deleted via API — ever. Every junk field is permanent clutter in every field-picker, every view builder, every import mapping, for the life of the workspace. This is the one debt class that compounds.
  4. Governance is a façade. 14 teams, all empty; 13 spaces mapped to teams with nobody in them; DID has a team and no space; NVP has a space and no team; the plan caps additional teams at 10 and 14 are used — so even the mapping is over budget and can't be extended. 53 of 56 chat channels are private in a workspace with one member — channels scope to people, not teams, so when 40 people arrive, nobody can see anything and nobody can be mentioned.
  5. REP has no reporting surface. Two views: overview and conversation. No list view, no board, no dashboard, no filter. The space whose entire purpose is reporting is the least usable space in the workspace.
  6. A rogue automation is live and unaccounted for. It overwrote due dates on every new task with "today +5" during the REP build. The API can't see automations. That means an invisible actor is mutating data and you cannot enumerate or disable it from the tooling you have. Find it in the UI Monday or it will corrupt every due date 40 people set.

3. What fails first with 40 daily users

Assignment and notification, on day one. One member, zero-populated teams, 53 private channels. The first thing 40 people do is look for their work; nothing is assigned to them, no group mention reaches them, and the channel they need is private to a workspace that doesn't include them. The workspace is socially sealed.

Second failure, within the first reporting cycle: the Submitted/Reviewed handshake. Unit owners will submit reports and there is no status to mark it, no view to see what's submitted, and Ricardo becomes the single bottleneck reading 1,624 sub-tasks through an unfiltered overview view. The governance model collapses into "Ricardo asks people on chat" — which is exactly what the system was meant to replace.

Third, quietly and permanently: the field debt. Every new user building a view wades through 40 REP fields including _probe_num and two Business Units, forever, because deletion is impossible.

4. Five highest-leverage changes, ranked

  1. Populate the teams and fix the team/space mapping (including the 10-team cap decision). Nothing else matters if work can't be assigned or notifications can't fire. This is also the only change with a hard plan constraint — you may need to consolidate (e.g., one retail team for EXP/TLF/PPP) before adding people, because you cannot create team #15.
  2. Manually set the status vocabulary in every space: add Submitted and Reviewed (and strip FXL's ten-status sprawl down to something a human can use). This is the governance engine. It's tedious, it's UI-only, and it's the difference between a reporting system and a shared to-do list. Do it before the first reporting cycle, not after.
  3. Find and kill or fix the due-date automation in the UI. An unenumerable mutation of every new task is an integrity problem, not a convenience. One hour in the browser, prevents months of corrupted dates.
  4. Collapse MKT from 169 lists to a handful, moving tier/discipline/brand into filters on the existing workspace-level fields. MKT is 45% of the navigation surface and 0% of the work. This is the biggest usability win per hour invested, and it must happen before users learn the wrong paths.
  5. Build REP's actual surface: one saved list view per cadence filtered on Feedback State, one dashboard, and strip the description-duplicating fields out of every view (they can't be deleted — hide them). The reporting space must be usable by people who aren't Ricardo.

5. The thing almost everyone gets wrong

They would start by cleaning up the cosmetic mess — the _probe_ fields, the duplicate BUS Unit, the empty folders — because it's visible and satisfying. Don't. Fields can't be deleted via API, but they *can* be hidden from views and ignored; that debt is annoying, not fatal. The fatal errors are the invisible ones: empty teams, missing statuses, a live rogue automation. An empty workspace tempts you to perfect the schema; the actual job is to make it *operate*. The schema is 90% fine. The people, the statuses, and the automation are 100% broken.

Monday morning, first action: open the ClickUp UI as admin, go to automations, find the due-date rule, and kill it. Then populate @fin, @hrm, @exco and the rest with real members — because until a second human can receive an assignment, this is not a workspace, it's a diary.

Mistral Large 4

Verdict: a reporting database wearing an operating system's clothes

The workspace has one real workload (REP's 1,740 tasks) and 370 empty shells. That is not a rollout in progress — it is a reporting template that was built first and everything else was scaffolded around it. As an OS for 40 daily users, it will fail in week one, and the failure will be assignment and status, not tidiness.

1. What is structurally right — protect it.

The Space-per-unit model with the HOF/ZOR/COR/ZEE tiering and Briefed-In/Recurring/Projects work formats is the correct skeleton; don't flatten it. The six workspace-level custom fields (Brand, Business Unit, Requested By, Engagement Tier, Work Format, SLA Service Tier) are exactly the right shared vocabulary — one definition, inherited everywhere, that's the thing most setups get wrong. The REP period-task → unit-sub-task pattern works and is already carrying real volume. The named user groups (@fin, @exco…) are the right handles. Protect the skeleton and the shared fields; everything else is replaceable.

2. What is structurally wrong.

  • Zero members in 14 groups, one workspace member. No task can be assigned, no mention notifies anyone. This alone makes the workspace non-functional as a work system.
  • No status vocabulary for the actual workflow. Eight spaces share the default six statuses; PPP/NVP have two; FXL has nine. There is no submitted/reviewed anywhere, so the documented monthly handshake cannot execute. And status writes are API-refused, so this can only be fixed by hand, per space, 15 times.
  • REP has no reporting surface — two auto views, no list view, board, dashboard, or filters, in the one space doing all the work.
  • MKT is a 45%-of-workspace replication (169 lists) that will collapse under maintenance the first time a discipline or brand changes.
  • Plan cap already binding: 10 of 10 additional teams used, NVP has no team, DID has a team with no space, teams can't be deleted. The mapping layer is frozen in a wrong state.
  • Field rot: 141 space-level definitions, 40 in REP alone, including duplicates (BUS Unit/Business Unit), cancelled-cadence fields, and _probe_* test junk — and deletion is impossible via API, so this only grows.

3. What fails first, and why.

Assignment. On day one, 40 staff open ClickUp and find they cannot be assigned work, mentioned, or notified — because every group is empty and Ricardo is the only member. The second failure, same week, is status: people in PPP see "to do/complete" while FXL sees nine statuses, nobody can set submitted, and the monthly report review Ricardo is supposed to control has no mechanism. The due-date automation (every new task stamped today+5) will quietly corrupt SLAs from the first task created.

4. Five highest-leverage changes, in order.

  1. Populate the 14 groups and add the 40 staff — without membership nothing else matters; it's the difference between a database and a workplace.
  2. Fix statuses by hand in all 15 spaces — one vocabulary including submitted/reviewed; API can't do it, so it's 15 manual passes, but it unblocks the reporting handshake and every workflow.
  3. Give REP a real surface — saved list views per cadence, a board, a dashboard, filters on Period/Unit — the space carrying 100% of the work currently has no way to see it.
  4. Kill the due-date automation and audit REP fields — stop the silent SLA corruption now; mark the 40 REP fields deprecated (can't delete) and standardise on the working set.
  5. Rationalise MKT's 169 lists into one template with brand/tier as field values, not list names — removes 45% of the maintenance surface before 40 people start touching it.

5. The thing everyone gets wrong.

They'll treat this as a cleanup job — delete fields, merge lists, tidy folders — and miss that the plan cap and the API limits mean the structural mistakes are permanent unless fixed in the UI by a human with admin rights. Nobody will "get" that status changes, team fixes, and field deprecation are manual, one-space-at-a-time operations on a free-tier plan, so the real first move isn't tidying — it's deciding whether this tier can even host the operating model, before 40 people depend on it.
