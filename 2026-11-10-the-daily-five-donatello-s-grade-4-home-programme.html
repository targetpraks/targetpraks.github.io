Grade 4 preparation · daily practice · both houses

The Daily Five

Five things, every day, in the same order, about 30 minutes of work. Same five at our house and at Lauren's. The repetition is the point — he should be able to run it without being told what comes next.

Donatello · turning 10 in February · prepared 10 November 2026

Start here — what the numbers actually say

His last reading check put him at 90 correct words a minute. South Africa's minimum benchmark for the end of Grade 4 is 70, and the end of Grade 3 is 50 — so on speed he is already past Grade 4's floor and roughly a year ahead of the national minimum. That is a real win and he should hear it.

What it means practically: more speed is not the job. Grade 4 is where the work switches from getting words off the page to understanding them and writing them down. So this programme loads volume (words read), comprehension (tell it back) and writing (three paragraphs a day) — and treats speed as a by-product.

The honest other half: against international norms for his age, 90 words a minute sits near the 25th percentile, and the Grade 4 median is 133. So he is not behind his own country's standard and he is not yet comfortable in a standard chapter book. Both things are true. The daily reading is what closes that.

01

The five

In this order. After school does 1–4 (about 33 minutes with a break in the middle). Bedtime does 5. Never reorder — the order is the habit.

1

Read aloud to an adult

After school · 10 minutes · the one that cannot be skipped

He reads out loud, you listen and follow the words with your finger or eye. When he pauses on a word for three seconds, you say it for him — no coaching, no "sound it out", no waiting. He carries on. The word count still counts.

Every passage is read three times on the days it appears: same passage Monday, Wednesday and Friday. The second and third reads are where fluency is actually built.

400 words a day, rising to 600 adult supplies hard words
2

Journal — three paragraphs

After school · 15 minutes · same three headings every single day

Grateful, what happened, a story. Your idea, and it is the strongest part of the programme — gratitude, sequencing and imagination are three different muscles and he gets all three in one sitting. Each paragraph is two or three sentences, so the whole thing is 60–100 words.

The headings never change. That is what makes it doable on a tired afternoon: he is not deciding what to write, only what to say.

3 paragraphs · 60–100 words one correction per day
3

Eight words

After school · 5 minutes · the same eight words all week

One list per week, printed from the table further down, identical at both houses. Look at the word, say it, cover it, write it, check it — three times per word. On Friday he writes the eight from dictation, no looking.

This is the only part that touches his spelling directly, and it works because it is eight words, not eighty. Six follow a sound pattern, two are the awkward words that break the rules.

8 words · Monday to Friday dictation Friday
4

Three minutes of handwriting

After school · 3 minutes · one letter family a day

Three minutes, on lined paper, one letter family: c a d g q · l h b k · i j t f · m n r p · u v w y. Ten of each letter, then two words that use it.

Stop at three minutes even if he is enjoying it — the point is a short daily dose, not a penmanship lesson.

3 minutes, hard stop
5

Bedtime book — his choice

Bedtime · 10 minutes · no target, no counting, no adult needed

Anything he wants: Dog Man, comics, a dinosaur fact book, a football annual, a free Book Dash picture book. He reads it himself or follows along with an audiobook — Storynory has a thousand free stories.

The only rule is that nobody tests this one. It exists so that reading stays something he does for himself, which is the thing that survives long after the programme stops.

uncounted on purpose

On a bad day — the short version, 18 minutes

Read aloud 10 minutes + journal, one sentence under each heading + the eight words once. Reading aloud is never the thing you cut. If his head hurts from the reading, the journal and the words are enough that day — and say so, so he does not learn that a bad day means failure.

02

The journal, exactly

Three headings, in this order, every day. The frames below are what he copies until he does not need them. Date at the top. One drawing allowed, in the margin, after the writing is finished — never instead of it.

1 · Thank you for

Two sentences · gratitude

One thing I am grateful for today is ______ because ______.

Not "my family" every day. Push once for a specific thing: the goal he scored, the dog, that it stopped raining, that supper was lasagne.

2 · What happened

Three sentences · past tense · order

First, ______. Then, ______. After that, ______.

These three openers carry the whole paragraph. They force the past tense and they force a sequence — which is exactly the bit that goes missing when he writes. Something real from the day, not a made-up one.

3 · The story

Three sentences · imagination

Take one starter from the list, or carry on yesterday's. Write three sentences. Give it a title if you want.

He can continue the same story for three or four days — that is better than starting fresh, because it makes him hold a thread. Only rule: it must have a person in it and something must happen.

Fifteen story starters. Use one a day, no repeats inside a fortnight.

  1. The day the LEGO city came alive.
  2. A robot that only speaks in football scores.
  3. The drum that nobody was allowed to hit.
  4. I found a door behind the bookshelf.
  5. The night the moon went out.
  6. My dog learned one word and used it against me.
  7. The smallest dinosaur in the world.
  8. We swapped places for a day.
  9. The treehouse had one more room than we built.
  10. A goalkeeper who was afraid of the ball.
  11. The puddle that was a portal.
  12. Everything I touched turned to jelly.
  13. The last child on Earth, and the Wi-Fi still worked.
  14. A snail that wanted to win a race.
  15. The secret the ants were keeping.

Friday — the Friday Story

Instead of paragraph three, he takes the best story of the week and writes it again, longer: six to eight sentences, a beginning, a middle and an end, and a title. That is the only piece each week that gets redone, and it is how three sentences turn into a real story by the end of the year.

Before the pencil goes down — four checks, out loud

1. Does it make sense if I read it back to myself? 2. Capital letter at the start, full stop at the end of every sentence? 3. Finger spaces between the words? 4. One word I could swap for a better one.

03

The adult's job — five lines

This is the whole of it. It is the same five lines at both houses, which is what makes it work in two places.

  • Say it out loud before he writes it. He tells you the sentence, you nod, then he writes it. Talking first removes the blank-page stall.
  • You hold the pen for the word bank. Before he starts, write four or five useful words on a scrap — the names, the tricky words — so spelling never blocks the idea. He copies them.
  • Correct one thing per piece. One. The mechanic for that day is on the card. Everything else that is wrong today gets left alone, on purpose.
  • If he stalls, you scribe. He talks, you write, then he reads it back and changes one word. That still counts as his writing.
  • Read it back together at the end. He reads his own piece aloud. That is the moment he hears whether it works — you do not need to tell him.

The mechanic rotates by day, so a week trains all of them without any of them being a big deal.

How many words he reads

This is the number you asked about. It climbs in three steps across twelve weeks, and it is counted in words read aloud — not minutes, because minutes reward slow reading and words do not.

Counting without counting: once, at the start, count the words on one full page of his book and write that number inside the front cover. From then on you count pages, not words. One page is one page forever, which is much easier than arithmetic at the kitchen table.

After he reads — sixty seconds, out loud

"Tell me what just happened, in your own words." Then one more question, and only one: why did that happen, or how did he feel. That single follow-up question is comprehension practice, and it costs you a minute.

05

The book ladder

Three shelves. He picks the book — the only rule is the nineteen-out-of-twenty rule: he should be able to read nineteen words in twenty on a page without help. Below that it is frustrating, above it there is nothing to learn.

Shelf 1 · easy wins · 5–10 minutes a book

Dog Man, and everything like it

Pictures do half the work, the text is short and funny, and 200 pages go past in a week. This is not a lesser book — it is the book that makes a reluctant reader read 200 pages. Also worth trying: Captain Underpants, Narwhal and Jelly, The Bad Guys.

In stock locally: Exclusive Books, Dog Man from R309. Free and South African: Book Dash — downloadable and printable picture books.
Shelf 2 · moving up · 10–15 minutes

Short-chapter series

The step between picture-heavy books and a real chapter book: large type, a picture every page or two, chapters of 300–500 words. Scholastic's Branches line is built for exactly this jump — Dragon Masters and Owl Diaries are the two that hold boys this age. Usborne Young Reading does the same job with myths, which is already his thing.

One chapter a night is a complete read for this shelf. Keep the same book going across both houses so the thread does not break.
Shelf 3 · by ear · 10 minutes

Listen and follow

He listens to a story with the words in front of him and follows along with his finger. He gets the story, the vocabulary and the sentence shapes without the decoding cost. This is the single cheapest thing on this page and it is how a child who reads slowly still ends up knowing how books work.

Free: Storynory (over a thousand free audio stories). Paid, better quality: any Roald Dahl audiobook with the paperback open beside it.
Shelf 4 · worth knowing about

Books made for readers like him

Barrington Stoke is a UK publisher that designs every book to counteract reading difficulty — tinted paper, a typeface chosen for it, short lines, chapters that are genuinely short. Their Little Gems range is written at a reading age of about 6–7 with content for 8–12, which is his exact position.

Honest caveat: I could not confirm South African stock — order through Collins or ask Exclusive Books to import one before committing to a set.
06

The Sunday minute

One minute a week, Sunday evening, and it is the only measuring anyone does. A passage he has not read before, read aloud for exactly one minute. Count the words he got right, minus the ones you had to give him. Write the number down.

Say this before you start: "This is just to see if the practice is working — it is not a test and there is no mark." Then, whatever the number: "That was n words. Last week was n." Nothing else. Never a classmate, never his brother.

The target: 110 correct words a minute by the end of Grade 4, up from 90. That is a rise of about one word a minute per week, which is what steady practice produces — and it puts him at the comfortable line for a Grade 4 reader doing school work in English.

08

Two houses, one system

The failure mode is not the work — it is the admin. So there is exactly one book, one card, one list, and nothing to telephone about.

The kit — buy or print once

  • One A5 hardback notebook. One. It travels in his school bag, it lives there, and it is the journal at both houses. Two notebooks would mean two half-stories and an argument about which pages count.
  • Two laminated cards with the three headings — one taped inside a kitchen cupboard here, one at Lauren's. Printed from the appendix of this document.
  • The week's eight words, printed Sunday and tucked into the back of the journal, so it travels with the book.
  • A pencil and a sharpener in the bag. That is genuinely where it breaks.

What Lauren actually has to do

  • Twenty minutes: he reads aloud, she listens and gives him a hard word after three seconds.
  • He writes the three paragraphs. She says the sentence back to him if he is stuck, and corrects one thing.
  • The eight words, once.
  • No printing, no planning, no marking, no reporting back. If the day goes wrong, it goes wrong — nobody gets a message about it.
  • She never needs to know what is on this page beyond the card.

Your job, and it is small: Sunday evening, ten minutes. Flip through the week without marking anything, do the one-minute check, write the number in the log. Once a month, glance at the handwriting and see whether the letters have got smaller and steadier — that is what the three minutes is for.

09

What not to do

These are the things that quietly turn a working programme into a fight. They matter more than the exercises.

  • Don't correct spelling mid-sentence. He is holding the whole sentence in his head; stopping to fix a word loses it. Spelling is what the eight words are for.
  • Don't time him. One minute on a Sunday, nothing else, ever. A visible timer turns reading into a performance.
  • Don't compare him to anyone. Not a classmate, not his brother, not a norm. Only to his own last number.
  • Don't redo a page he is proud of. If he likes it, it is finished. The Friday Story is the one place a piece gets rewritten.
  • No reading when his head or eyes hurt. He gets eyestrain and headaches — that is a real limit, not an excuse. Short blocks, good light, cream paper rather than white, and stop at the first sign of a headache.
  • Don't make up a missed day. A missed day is missed. Doubling up teaches him that the programme is a debt, and then he stops wanting it.
  • Don't add anything. Five things, in this order, for twelve weeks. More is how it dies.

One thing to keep separate: his reading programme at the reading centre is the phonics spine, and this is the volume-and-writing layer on top. If the centre gives home practice, that comes first and paragraph three of the journal is what makes room.
