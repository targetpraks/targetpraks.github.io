The buyer behind the ACDC approach: Deneb, and the parent that pays for it

01The verdict in six lines

  1. Deneb is real, and smaller than the room implies. A JSE-listed industrial holding company with R4.34bn revenue and about R480m EBITDA — roughly a fifth of HCI's revenue and a tenth of its EBITDA, and worth only R1.25bn on the market against a deal that will most likely cost more than that. Deneb IAR 2026
  2. The money sits one level up. HCI owns 84.2% of Deneb — not the 86% quoted in the meeting — and HCI is the balance sheet: R24.2bn of revenue, R5.4bn of EBITDA, R12.4bn of market value. HCI IAR 2026
  3. Their model is documented, not improvised. Deneb bought 80% of Dawning Filters for R80m cash in October 2025, left the sellers 20%, and gave them a put four years out at six times after-tax profit. That is the template they will bring to ACDC. Dawning SENS, 9 Oct 2025
  4. Cash is genuine; their shares are not currency. Deneb trades at a 40% discount to net asset value and turns over 0.9% of its shares a year. HCI trades at a 51% discount. Accepting paper from either would be accepting a claim on assets at half price. Sharenet: DNB at 284c
  5. They can self-fund about R650m of debt; beyond roughly R1bn it becomes HCI's cheque. That flips the deal into a related-party transaction requiring a fairness opinion and an independent minority vote in Deneb — a gate the 15.8% free float controls. JSE transaction categories
  6. "Six months" is the least reliable sentence in the meeting. A deal of this size is a large merger at the Competition Tribunal, plus a JSE Category 1 circular and a shareholder vote — each a gate, and the Competition file is the long pole. Competition Commission thresholds

Sizing note: ACDC is private and publishes no accounts. Everything about ACDC revenue or profit in this report is a labelled estimate built from public headcount, footprint and listed-peer multiples — never presented as a filed figure.

Two things matter for an ACDC seller. First, distribution is already their biggest revenue line but one of their weakest margins — R1.98bn of turnover producing R109m of operating profit, a 5.5% margin. A wholesale electrical business at scale would roughly double that segment overnight and lift its margin profile. Second, the distribution portfolio is thin on industrial goods: toys, games, stationery and digital codes (Prima Toys, Prima Interactive, Butterfly Products, Seartec, HTIC in Hong Kong). ACDC would not be a bolt-on to an existing electrical platform — it would become the platform.

The 2026 result is flattered, and they know it

Their own commentary concedes that a R29m credit came to book while once-off costs and new-business costs offset it — cost growth of 4.8% is the "fair reflection". Underlying core operating profit rose 27.8%. Treat FY2026 HEPS of 39.26c as a good year, not a trend line. A seller negotiating an earnings multiple off a peak year should expect the buyer to normalise it — and should do so first, in its own numbers.

HCI's net asset value is R25.3bn against a market value of R12.4bn — it trades at a 51% discount to its own assets, at 7.3× headline earnings, on a 1.2% dividend yield. That discount is the single most important fact about the buyer group. It means HCI's paper is worth half of what its balance sheet says, and it means HCI's own shareholders have been agitating for years for something to close the gap.

The portfolio, and where Deneb sits

  • Gaming and hotels — Tsogo Sun and Southern Sun, the group's largest listed assets.
  • Media — eMedia Holdings, owner of e.tv and Openview.
  • Transport — Frontier Transport Holdings, the Golden Arrow bus business.
  • Resources — HCI Coal, where headline earnings rose sharply in FY2026.
  • Industry — Deneb, at 84.2%, the manufacturing and distribution arm.

HCI is not a private-equity house with a fund life. It is a permanent-capital holding company that has owned assets for decades, does not distribute its subsidiaries' cash beyond dividends, and lets operating management run their own businesses. Read it as a home rather than a flip — which is exactly the pitch the meeting reported.

Two facts a seller should weigh

Ownership quality is a genuine asset

HCI reports B-BBEE Level 1 for 2026, having been Level 2 in 2025. For an electrical supplier selling into Eskom, municipalities, Transnet and large contractors, moving from a family-owned entity to a Level 1 group is a commercial upgrade, not a compliance formality. It is the strongest non-price argument in HCI's pitch.

The founder is leaving

Johnny Copelyn, chief executive for 29 years and the architect of the group, will retire in the 2028 financial year. Kevin Govender has been appointed joint CEO as his successor — and Govender already sits on Deneb's board. Succession is orderly, but the deal-maker who built HCI's reputation for buying family businesses will not be signing the next decade of them.

On succession: Financial Mail, "HCI: Bye-bye Johnny, bye-bye Johnny's discount?" (Aug 2026) and Billionaires.Africa.

The put option is the clause to argue about

Six times after-tax profit is not six times EBITDA. For a business earning a normal 27% tax rate, six times PAT works out to roughly 4.4× EBITDA — versus the 6–9× EBITDA a competitive sale of an electrical distributor would attract. Worked on three profit levels:

The stub is worth materially less than the shares sold on day one — because the multiple is lower and the cash arrives four years later. At a 15% cost of equity the R144m put is worth R82m today. If a 20% stub is unavoidable, argue for the entry multiple (not 6× PAT), a shorter window (year two, not year four), a defined dividend payout ratio on the stub, and no cap tied to a fixed rand amount.

What is genuinely good in their model

They keep founders in place and let them run the business; they buy for keeps; they have a record of funding growth rather than stripping it; and they pay cash. For a family selling a 40-year-old business, that combination is rare and worth real money in the negotiation. The argument is not whether they are good owners. It is what the last 20% is worth, and when.

The shape of the group

  • Wholesale: head office and national distribution centre at Longmeadow, Edenvale, with branches in Germiston, Pinetown, Cape Town and — new in 2026 — Windhoek, Namibia. The business describes itself as South Africa's largest wholesale electrical supplier.
  • Franchise retail: ACDC Express launched in 2007; stores are independently owned, source centrally from ACDC Dynamics, and pay an initial fee of R172 500 including VAT plus ongoing service fees. That is the asset a buyer is really acquiring: the brand, the royalty stream and the supply lock-in.
  • Manufacturing: in August 2025 ACDC bought Blue Nova Energy from Reunert, cleared by the Competition Commission with conditions — vertical integration into lithium-iron-phosphate battery packs and inverters.
  • Brands carried: C&S Electric switchgear, Siemens PLC automation, Conlog prepaid meters, Wera tools, CRC/Q20, Terasaki — a distribution book that depends on supplier consent to any change of control.

Our own exposure — read this before anyone signs an NDA

Infinity Brands handles franchise development and onboarding for ACDC Express. If control of the franchisor passes to Deneb, three things become live at once: whether our franchise-management agreement survives a change of control, whether it is assignable, and whether a new owner with a listed-company playbook decides to in-source franchise operations into Deneb's own structures. None of that is answered by an introductory meeting — but all of it should be resolved before a sale agreement is signed, not after. The seller's leverage over that point is at its maximum before closing and near zero afterwards.

Why a family business would sell now

The trading backdrop has turned. Load-shedding has been suspended through 2026 and the grid has held, which has cooled the backup-power surge that inflated 2022–2024 earnings across the sector. Reunert's own explanation for selling Blue Nova was that battery demand slowed "as power cuts became less severe" — ACDC bought that asset at the bottom of the cycle. Meanwhile behind-the-meter solar in South Africa has passed 7 GW of installed capacity and Africa is on track for a record solar year in 2026, up 45%. The long-term electrification story is intact; the short-term earnings base is not what it was in 2023.

That is the honest case for selling into a larger group: the next decade of growth — grid capex, data centres, private generation, industrial automation — needs capital and a Level 1 owner more than it needs a family's balance sheet. It is also the honest case for not selling cheap: the buyer knows the cycle has cooled, and will price the last three years as if they were normal.

06What it is worth — and what that implies for Deneb

No reliable ACDC revenue figure exists in public. What follows is a transparent range, not a valuation: it shows what the buyer can credibly pay, and how large the cheque becomes against its own size.

Listed peer anchors

  • ARB Holdings — the closest comparable, a Durban electrical wholesaler with ~R2bn revenue, taken private by Masimong and Sabvest at an 800c cash offer to minorities, valuing it at R1.8bn on 9.3× earnings and a 4.3% yield.
  • Hudaco Industries — branded industrial distribution, trading at roughly 8–11× earnings through 2026.
  • Deneb itself — 7.2× earnings and 3.2× EBITDA, the discount a listed holding company carries.
  • Deneb's own entry multiple — about 6.4× after-tax profit on Dawning. That, more than any peer, is the number they will open with.

07Can they actually pay — and who signs the cheque

Deneb's own balance sheet is healthy but small. The gap between what it can borrow and what this deal probably costs is where HCI enters — and where the regulatory complexity begins.

So the funding answer splits cleanly. Below about R900m, Deneb funds it itself with bank debt and stays a straightforward acquisition. Above that — and most of the scenario matrix sits above it — HCI must inject equity or lend, and the transaction changes character:

  • It becomes a related-party transaction under the JSE Listings Requirements, requiring a fairness opinion and independent board oversight.
  • HCI, as the related party, cannot vote. The decision passes to Deneb's 15.8% free float — roughly R198m of shares — which becomes an effective veto on the deal's structure, if not on the deal.
  • Any share issuance to HCI at a 40% discount to NAV would be value-destructive to that minority, and will be fought. The cleaner route is a cash loan or a rights issue priced at or near NAV.

The structural trap in the "one pot" idea

The meeting reports Deneb wanting all related assets consolidated into a single vehicle to avoid conflicts between ownership layers. Sensible in principle. But consolidating ACDC Dynamics (wholesale), the ACDC Express franchisor and Blue Nova into one pot creates a single entity with three different risk profiles — cyclical wholesale, royalty income, and early-stage battery manufacturing — priced at one multiple. A seller should insist that the three be valued separately, even if they are bought together, and that the put option apply per business, not to a blended number the buyer controls.

08The hurdles, in the order they bite

1. Competition approval — the long pole

South Africa's thresholds moved on 1 May 2026: a merger is large where the combined turnover or assets of the acquiring and target groups reach R9.5bn and the target reaches R280m; intermediate where the combined figure reaches R1bn and the target R200m. A large merger is notified to both the Commission and the Tribunal; an intermediate one only to the Commission.

On the face of it, Deneb's R4.34bn plus ACDC's estimated R2–3bn lands below R9.5bn — an intermediate merger. But the acquiring group is controlled by HCI, whose own turnover is R24.2bn. On an acquiring-group basis the combined figure clears R9.5bn comfortably and the deal is a large merger with a Tribunal hearing. Counsel must settle which basis applies before anyone promises a date, because it is the difference between a Commission-only process and a public Tribunal process with a hearing date.

Thresholds and fees: Competition Commission; the change and its timing effects: Werksmans. Filing fees: R220 000 intermediate, R735 000 large.

2. JSE categorisation and the Deneb shareholder vote

A transaction worth 30% or more of Deneb's market capitalisation is a Category 1 transaction: a JSE-approved circular, a general meeting, and shareholder approval. A transaction of 5–30% is Category 2 — an announcement only, which is how the R80m Dawning deal was handled. Everything in the scenario matrix except the smallest corner is Category 1. Add a related-party fairness opinion if HCI funds it, and the timetable stretches by construction, not by accident.

Categorisation: Cader & Co; the 30% test: Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr.

3. Third-party consents

  • Suppliers: ACDC's distribution rights for C&S Electric, Siemens PLC, Conlog, Wera and Terasaki typically carry change-of-control clauses. Losing one of them after closing destroys the thesis the buyer paid for.
  • Franchisees: a change of franchisor triggers consent rights and, in a well-drafted agreement, termination rights. In a 34–42 store network, even a handful of exits changes the royalty base.
  • Landlords and banks: routine, but slow.

4. The related-party loan the meeting flagged

One of the transcript's open questions — the structure of the loan to related parties on the balance sheet — is the most important due-diligence item on the list. Family-business balance sheets routinely carry shareholder loans, property held in a trust, or trading balances with related entities. Each one changes equity value, and each one must be settled, capitalised or carved out before completion rather than negotiated after. Get this answered in writing, with the loan agreements, before an indicative price is discussed.

10Risk register

What the buyer is taking on

  • Cycle risk. ACDC's recent earnings were inflated by the backup-power boom; the grid has held through 2026 and battery demand has normalised. Deneb would be buying at a cyclical midpoint, paying a multiple on cooling earnings.
  • Integration of a family culture into a listed group with a light-touch but formal reporting regime — the classic failure mode in "we leave management alone" acquisitions.
  • Blue Nova. A distressed asset bought from Reunert in a soft battery market. It is a manufacturing bet inside a distribution business, and it needs capital.
  • Inventory. 70 000 SKUs and a 1 100-page catalogue imply substantial slow-moving stock and obsolescence exposure. Diligence should measure aged inventory by category, not in aggregate.
  • Credit. Contractor and municipal receivables in a weak construction market.
  • Their own discount. Deneb's shareholders have watched a 40% discount persist. A large acquisition that adds complexity without closing it may be punished, not rewarded.

What the seller is taking on

  • Loss of control for a 20% stub with no board control, no exit until year four, and a put priced below entry.
  • Encumbered retained shares — pledged for warranty claims for five years in the precedent.
  • Earn-out and warranty exposure against a professional buyer with lawyers who do this weekly.
  • Franchisee and supplier fallout if consents are mishandled during the transition.
  • Information asymmetry. They asked for the updated information memorandum and 2026 budget before giving an indicative valuation. That is normal, but it means they see everything first and name a price second. Stage the disclosure.
  • Key-man dependency. The business is identified with its founder in the market. Any deal must price what happens when he leaves — and his leaving is usually a condition of the deal.

11Next moves — what to ask Stuart Queen

Ten questions, in the order that protects value. None of them requires disclosing financials.

  1. What is the funding structure — Deneb's own debt, or HCI equity? Answer determines whether this is a Category 1 related-party deal and adds months.
  2. Will HCI commit funding in writing before the circular, so the Competition file and the shareholder vote can run in parallel?
  3. Is the acquiring group Deneb or HCI for merger-control purposes? Which threshold do your advisers say applies?
  4. Will you give an indicative valuation range — not a price — on the basis of the last audited accounts, before we release the 2026 budget?
  5. What multiple are you applying to normalised earnings, and how are you defining normalised?
  6. Do you want 100%, or 80% with a stub? If a stub, what is the put multiple, the exercise window and the cap?
  7. What dividend policy would apply to the stub, in writing — a payout ratio, or a policy subject to board discretion?
  8. How do you intend to handle the ACDC Express franchise agreements, their consent rights, and the existing franchise-management arrangement?
  9. What happens to Blue Nova — held, funded, or carved out?
  10. How do you propose to deal with the related-party balances on the balance sheet, and by when?

The one-line position

They are a credible, permanent, cash-paying owner with a Level 1 B-BBEE profile and a documented template — and they are buying with a currency (their shares) worth 40% less than the assets behind it, from a parent whose shares are worth 51% less. Take the cash, argue the stub, and do not let the six-month promise set the pace.
