Investment brief · Cape Town · 15 November 2026

Nothing in Cape Town guarantees more than the bank.
Three deals clear it anyway — with no growth at all.

You asked for deals that guarantee a better return than leaving the money in a bank. No property does that — a guarantee is a promise, and only National Treasury makes one. But the comparison is still worth making properly, because the bank's 9.0% is not the free ride it looks like once SARS and inflation take their share. Below: nine live Cape Town listings, priced and costed from their own listings, set against what the money actually earns in a bank.

01

The hurdle: what the bank actually pays you

South Africa is an unusually good place to hold cash right now. The repo rate sits at 7.25% and prime at 10.75% after two hikes in 2026, which drags deposit rates up with it. Inflation is only 3.6%, so a nominal return in the nines is a real return near 5%. That is the number any property has to beat.

The real hurdle is not 9.00%. It is 5.31%. That is what survives SARS. And after 3.6% inflation it is 1.71% of actual purchasing power a year. A property needs to clear 5.31% net, not 9.00%, to be worth the extra work — and on that test far more Cape Town deals qualify.

The whole question is not "which deal is best" — it is "how much do you believe in Cape Town prices for the next five to ten years".

05

The bond problem nobody quotes you

Prime is 10.75%. A home loan at prime less 0.5% costs 10.25% — more than most of these properties yield after costs. Look at the fourth column from the right in the table above: every single one of the nine is cash-negative on a full bond, except Kalkfontein, which scrapes R1 209 a month.

This is the structural change from the last cycle. In 2021 prime was 7% and gearing a rental made sense. At 10.75% it does not — the bank takes the entire net yield and asks for more. Two hikes in 2026 (May and September) moved the repo rate from 7.00% to 7.25%, and the next MPC decision is on 19 November.

So the honest reading: these deals work for cash, or with a deposit of at least half. At 50% gearing, Parklands, Kalkfontein, Fisantekraal, Parow Valley and Brackenfell all stay cash-positive. Maitland and Stellendale go slightly negative. Brooklyn is negative either way — its R3 120 monthly levy eats 45% of the rent before anyone is paid.

06

How each one fails

This is the section that matters more than the yields. Every item below has bitten a Cape Town landlord this year.

  1. The tenant leaves.

    One month void plus a re-let fee is about R12 000 on a R9 000 rental — roughly a fifth of a year's net income, gone, for one ordinary changeover.

  2. The levy climbs faster than the rent.

    Brooklyn's levy is already R3 120 against R7 000 rent. Levies rise with the scheme's insurance and the management contract, not with what the market will pay you.

  3. A special levy lands.

    One roof, one lift, one repaint: R40 000 to R150 000 across the scheme. That is five years of net income on a R700 000 flat, payable in 30 days.

  4. Rates go up when the city revalues.

    Some listings show absurdly low rates — Parow Valley's is R120 a month. That is a valuation artefact, not a permanent condition.

  5. Room-by-room letting is a business, not an investment.

    Kalkfontein's 14.4% is real, but it is three tenants, three deposits, three sets of arrears and a turnover problem. Price that in honestly.

  6. Capital growth does not repeat.

    Ten years of flats: +64% in Cape Town. The last year alone: +11.2% in the Western Cape. Those cannot both continue. The 10-year number is the one to model, and it is barely above the bank on its own.

  7. You cannot get out quickly or cheaply.

    Two to four months to sell, 5% agent commission, conveyancing, and CGT at an 18% effective rate on the gain. The bank returns your money in seven days with no penalty after year one.

  8. Arrears are normal now.

    PayProp measured 16.9% of tenants in arrears in Q2 2026. Roughly one in six. Budget for it rather than being surprised by it.

08

What I rejected, and why

Four listings scored well and were cut. Listing them is the honest thing to do — a shortlist you cannot audit is a sales pitch.

  • Strand, Rome — R660 000, 2 bed. 12.1% gross on paper. Marked Under Offer: not available.
  • Bellville Central — R675 000, 1 bed, 76 m². Showed 7.5% on my first pass. It is an over-50s retirement unit — you cannot let it on the open market. Cut.
  • Strand, Guldenland — R730 000, 1 bed. 12%+ gross. Also marked Under Offer.
  • Milnerton, Rugby — R995 000, 3 bed. Looked like the best yield in Milnerton until the levies and the R995 000 price met the R6 000 rentals in that street.

Two more carry a flag rather than a rejection: Maitland is advertised at R849 000 while its own description still says R899 000 — confirm the price with the agent. Parow Central has been listed since January 2026, which usually means the price is soft and negotiable.

Assumptions I made, so you can argue with them. 5% vacancy; 8% managing agent; R4 200 a year landlord insurance; maintenance at 1% of purchase price; 41% marginal tax rate; R48 000 conveyancing and no bond registration (adjust up about R28 000 if you bond); 20-year loan at 10.25%; rents held flat in the cash-flow columns (they actually rose 9.7% in the Western Cape last year, so these figures are conservative); no allowance for special levies. Not assumed: short-term letting. Airbnb rates in the CBD run at a median R1 768 a night, but that is a hospitality business with a 25.8%-multi-host market already in it, and it is a different risk class from everything above.

Method. Nine live Property24 listings in nine Cape Town areas, each opened individually and read for price, floor size, levies, rates and listing date. Rental comparables from 1 300+ live rental listings scraped across the same areas on the same day, filtered for long-term residential lets only (short-lets, room-shares and commercial units removed) and taken at the lower quartile of the same bedroom count. All 20 rental yields and the full model are reproducible from the scrape file held with this document.

The one-line answer. Nothing guarantees more than the bank's 9.0%. But 9.0% shrinks to 5.31% after SARS and 1.71% after inflation, and at that hurdle three of these deals win on cash flow alone and eight win if Cape Town keeps compounding at its ten-year rate. Buy one with cash, or not at all — at 10.75% prime, a bond turns every one of them negative.
