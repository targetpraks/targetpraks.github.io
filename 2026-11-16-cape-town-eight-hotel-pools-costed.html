02

Four kinds of promise, only some worth money

These products are sold with four very different instruments, and the table above flattens them. It should not.

Net guarantee — a number the developer owes you The Delaporte, Quivertree
Gross guarantee — a number, minus costs you still own BlackBrick Gardens, KLEI at Devonbosch
Pool, no guarantee — a share of whatever comes in The Rockefeller, Lagoon Beach Hotel, WINK Aparthotels, The Onyx

A net guarantee is a liability on the developer's balance sheet. A gross guarantee is a revenue floor with your costs still underneath it. A pool is neither — it is a participation right, and its value depends entirely on a split that, in eight products and four brochures, not one of them publishes.

Brochure: 10% 5.27%
Difference +1.92 points

The same three-year guarantee is 8% in the press and 10% in the brochure. At 8% it loses to the bank; at 10% it does not. Ask which one is in the sale agreement.

Two products, two ambiguities, and both are worth more than any rent difference between suburbs I looked at in the residential work. The word “net” is not decoration — ask for the schedule that shows what was deducted.

04

The pools: what the operator has to give you

For the 4 products with no guarantee, invert the question. Do not ask what they pay — ask what share of room revenue they would have to hand over for the unit to match the bank.

The Rockefeller · R1 950 000 42% of room revenue
Lagoon Beach Hotel · R3 150 000 43% of room revenue
WINK Aparthotels · R1 450 000 60% of room revenue
The Onyx · R2 850 000 60% of room revenue

Room revenue at published occupancy and class ADR, adjusted for unit size; costs are the same stack as above. A typical hotel management deal leaves the owner well under half of room revenue, which is why the two on the right need a deal nobody signs.

The Rockefeller needs only 42% because R2 500 of levy on a R1 950 000 unit is cheap and the unit is large enough to earn real room revenue. That is the whole trick in this asset class: low fixed costs per rand of price, and enough floor area to sell nights. A 19 m² micro-unit cannot do the second, whatever the operator promises.

Cape Town hotels are busier than the national average but getting less busy: metro occupancy fell from 66.6% to 62.8% while supply grew 2.7% and demand went -0.6% — RevPAR growth slowed from +14.4% to +8.2%.

This is why The Rockefeller is the most defensible pool product in the set: it has traded for years, it publishes its occupancy, and 76% is a measured number rather than a projection. Everything off-plan here is a bet on a building that does not exist yet, in a market where occupancy is falling.

06

What the brochures leave out

Four things that cost money and appear nowhere in the headline

1. The furniture pack. KLEI at Devonbosch makes it compulsory and prices it on request. BlackBrick Gardens and The Rockefeller include it — which is a real difference in the entry cost.

2. Parking. KLEI at Devonbosch excludes bays from the sale and rents them separately. On a hotel unit that is a recurring cost, not a one-off.

3. The levy. Three of the eight sellers do not publish one. I have modelled those and marked them — but a levy is the largest controllable cost in the stack, and it is the number most likely to change after transfer.

4. Rates. From 1 July 2027 a unit listed for more than half the year is rated commercial: R2 818 a month on a R2m unit against R806, which is 3.5×. The Delaporte listing already carries R3 375 on a R2 395 000 unit, so this is not a future risk — it is the current bill.

And one product in the set is sold out: Quivertree at R979 000, with the cleanest wording here — 6% net guaranteed after rates and levies. Worth noting what sold: the cheapest unit with the most honest guarantee.

08

What I would do

If you want a hotel unit at all, buy the one with four years of trading and a published occupancy. The Rockefeller nets 8.92% in this model at a 60% share, and it needs only 42% of room revenue to match the bank — so even a mediocre management deal clears. No guarantee is doing the work; the building is.

The guarantees are worth what the developer's balance sheet is worth. The Delaporte at 9.00% is the strongest paper here, and it survives the pessimistic reading — 6.00% even if the 9% turns out to be gross. KLEI at Devonbosch is the cautionary one: two published guarantee numbers, and the lower one puts it below the bank.

Avoid the micro-units. WINK Aparthotels at 19 m² nets 5.03% and would need 60% of room revenue to break even with the bank. There is no occupancy level at which a room that small becomes a good buy at R1 450 000.

And the honest comparison: Lagoon Beach Hotel nets 7.89% here on the strength of a R3 700 levy and R1 100 of rates — cost structure, not glamour. Brackenfell, from the earlier work, nets 7.20% with no operator between you and the rent at all; Woodstock commercial nets 8.21% where the tenant pays the rates. 2 of these eight beat Woodstock — 1 on a guarantee and 1 on a pool that has actually traded. That is the whole argument for this asset class in one line: the paper beats the bricks only when someone else is signing the cheque.
