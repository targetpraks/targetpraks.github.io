How you actually get light through a PVC ceiling

Short answer: you don't hang anything from the film. It carries about 170 grams per square metre. Every light is bolted to the slab above, and the membrane is either lit from behind, ringed and holed for a fitting, or split into a light line. Three ways, three sections below, and the numbers that decide whether the result looks like a ceiling or a dot grid.

The one rule that explains everything

A stretch ceiling is a welded sheet of PVC pulled tight into a track. It is strong in tension and useless in point load. Hang a pendant from it and you get a permanent cone; cut a hole in it with nothing above and the springs have nothing to grip. So every lighting detail in this trade is really a question of where the structure is — and then how the film is finished around it.

What the film does

  • Holds tension, hides everything above it, and diffuses light.
  • Carries roughly 0.17 kg per m² — a paperback per square metre.
  • Takes a glued ring and a cut hole without tearing, if the ring goes on first.
  • Can be unsprung at a corner and put back — on a harpoon profile.

What the slab does

  • Carries every fitting, on platforms or threaded rods.
  • Hosts the LED field, the drivers and the cable.
  • Sets the cavity depth — and depth is the number that decides your options.
  • Has to be reachable, or you have built a sealed box with a light in it.

Three ways light meets a membrane

They are not variations on a theme — they are different jobs with different failures. Pick by what the room has to do, then let the installer price it.

1

Behind the membrane — backlit field

No holes at all

A translucent membrane is tensioned over a field of LED modules or sheets mounted to the slab. You see one glowing surface, never a fitting.

Good: Even, shadowless light. Nothing to clean around. Nothing can leak through it.

Costs you: Eats 100–300 mm of height and a lot of watts — a translucent film throws away 25–50 % of the light it is fed.

Use it for: Retail ceilings, counters, brand moments, anywhere the ceiling can become the light.

2

Through the membrane — downlights and pendants

One hole per fitting, cut inside a glued ring

The fitting is carried on a platform bolted to the slab. The membrane gets a reinforcement ring glued on, a hole cut inside it, and the fitting clips to the platform above. The film carries none of the weight.

Good: Keeps ceiling height (40 mm fittings exist), cheap per point, easy to re-lamp.

Costs you: Every hole is a place a ring can lift or a draught can whistle. A grid of them in a dark matt ceiling reads as a dot pattern.

Use it for: Back of house, walkways, storage, rooms where someone has to change a lamp.

3

Between two membranes — the light line

A continuous slot instead of holes

The membrane stops at an aluminium profile on each side. An LED strip sits in the channel between the two fields, usually 50–100 mm wide.

Good: One clean line of light, no dot grid, no rings, and the strip is reachable when a profile is unsprung. Reads as architecture, not as fittings.

Costs you: Needs a designer who can lay out lines, and the slot is unforgiving — a wavy profile is a wavy line for 15 years.

Use it for: The default for a dark, matt, high-street or food-court ceiling.

1. Behind the film — the backlit field

No holes. A translucent membrane over a field of LED modules or light sheets. Two numbers govern the result: cavity depth D, and the spacing between sources S. The rule of thumb from the manufacturers is that D should be roughly equal to S — the deeper the void, the further apart the modules can sit before you see bands or spots through the film.

  • 1Structural slab. Everything hangs from here — never from the film.
  • 2Battens or platforms on threaded hangers. These set the cavity depth D.
  • 3LED modules or sheets on the batten line, at centres S.
  • 4Translucent membrane, tensioned into the perimeter track. It only diffuses.

Two consequences worth knowing before you fall in love with the idea. First, a translucent film throws away a quarter to a half of the light fed to it — the best membranes transmit around 75 %, and 50 % is the realistic minimum for a white ceiling. So you specify output at the face of the ceiling, then double it for the LEDs. Second, the deeper the cavity, the more watts you need to fill it. A 300 mm void is the sensible working number; 50 mm is a dot grid with a story attached.

2. Through the film — downlights and pendants

This is the detail people mean when they say "through the PVC". Six steps, in this order, and the order is the whole trick: the platform goes in before the membrane is tensioned, and the hole is cut after.

  • 1Platform bolted to the slab on threaded rods — a plastic or steel disc sized to the fitting.
  • 2The platform sits just above the finished membrane line, close but not pressing on it.
  • 3Cable loop left hanging under the platform, with slack, before the membrane goes up.
  • 4Membrane tensioned, then a reinforcement / thermal ring glued flat to its face.
  • 5A scalpel cuts the film inside the ring — the ring stops the cut running away.
  • 6Fitting pushed through and clipped to the platform by its springs. Bezel sits flush.

Why the ring is not optional

The ring does two jobs at once. It stiffens the film around the opening so the cut cannot run — PVC tears along a nick under tension — and it stands off the heat of the fitting. Rings are heat-resistant plastic, good to roughly 90 °C short-term, which is plenty for LED and not remotely enough for a halogen.

Ring size is matched to the fitting's cut-out, and the bezel has to cover the glue line. That is why the fitting is chosen before the ceiling is measured, not after.

What it costs you in height

A slim GX53 or GU10 LED body is about 40 mm deep. Add the platform and the clearance and budget 60 mm above the finished ceiling line for standard downlights — a fraction of what backlighting wants.

That is the trade: downlights keep your height and give you a dot grid; backlighting gives you a surface and eats the void.

3. Between two films — the light line

The detail that suits a dark, matt shop ceiling. Instead of punching holes, the membrane is made in fields, and each field is harpooned into a profile. An aluminium channel sits between two profiles, the LED strip lives in the channel, and what you see from below is a clean band of light with no fittings at all.

  • 1Aluminium channel fixed to the slab — this is what makes the line straight.
  • 2LED strip or slim module inside the channel, on its own driver.
  • 3Membrane field one, harpooned into a profile on the left of the slot.
  • 4Membrane field two on the right. The gap between them is the light you see.

This is the one to argue for in the concept store. A 50–100 mm slot down the long axis does the work of a dozen downlights, gives you no rings to lift, no bezels to look at, and puts the LED where somebody can reach it by unsprung one profile. The catch is unforgiving: the profile has to be dead straight, because a wavy channel is a wavy line for the life of the ceiling.

The numbers that decide whether it looks good

Depth is the budget you are spending. Here is what each depth buys you, with the spacing rules the manufacturers publish: for 120° optics, spacing equals depth; for 180° optics, 1.5× the depth. Both assume a translucent film of at least 50 % transmission.

Sizing the LED field — the arithmetic

  • Decide the light at the face first. Ambient is 500–1 000 lumens per m²; a working retail or food-prep floor wants 2 000 and up.
  • Divide by transmission. 42 m² at 500 lm/m² is 21 000 lm at the face — which becomes 42 000 lm behind a film that transmits 50 %. Push the face to 1 000 lm/m² and it is 84 000 lm behind.
  • Convert to watts. At a decent 150 lm/W that is roughly 280 W at the low end and 560 W at the high end — which is why a field that size gets split into two circuits.
  • Check the film again at the end. Mock up a metre of it at the real depth before ordering. Transmission figures are averages, and the eye is unforgiving at the edges of a lit field.

The eight traps

Each of these has cost somebody a ceiling. None of them is expensive to avoid at design stage; all of them are expensive afterwards.

Nothing hangs from the film.

A stretch membrane carries roughly 0.17 kg per square metre — about a paperback. A pendant, a fan, a projector: all of it rides on a platform fixed to the slab, and the membrane is only collared around it.

The driver does not live in the void.

Every manufacturer says the same thing: keep drivers and controllers outside the sealed cavity or behind a reachable panel. A driver that fails above a tensioned ceiling means de-tensioning the ceiling.

You cannot cut a hole in a finished ceiling.

Springs need a platform to grip. Fitting a light later means unsprung the membrane at a corner, adding platforms and cable, re-tensioning, then gluing rings. Fine with harpoon profiles — impossible with clip or glazed-in edges. Decide the light layout before the membrane is measured.

Heat is what kills PVC.

A ring is heat-resistant plastic — good to around 90 °C briefly — and it is insurance, not licence. LED only, never halogen, and keep every ring at least 25–30 mm clear of the perimeter track.

Set the platform to the finished line, not the cold line.

The film is heated to roughly 40–50 °C to tension it, and it sags while hot. Platforms set too high leave springs that cannot reach; set too low and they press a dimple into the ceiling forever.

Gloss turns a dot grid into a disco.

Every module, every fitting and every reflection shows in a high-gloss film. Matt for the store, gloss only where the reflection is the point.

Split the field.

Above roughly 500 W, split backlighting into separate circuits. One failed driver should darken a section, not the whole ceiling.

The electrician signs, not the ceiling installer.

Cable in conduit clipped to the slab, joints in enclosures the electrician can reach, and a certificate of compliance at the end. Ask who is pulling the wire before anyone quotes the membrane.

The detail I would specify

  • Matt membrane, always. Dark if the design calls for it — but gloss under grazing LED shows every module and every reflection.
  • Two or three light lines down the long axis rather than a grid of downlights. Fewer penetrations, no dot pattern on a dark ceiling, and the gear stays reachable.
  • One backlit printed panel behind the counter if you want a brand moment — that is where a translucent field earns its cost, because it is the only part of the ceiling a customer looks at.
  • Drivers in the back-of-house cupboard, on a labelled circuit, not in the void above the membrane.
  • Layout fixed before the membrane is measured. On a harpoon profile you can add later, but you will pay a call-out and a re-tension for the privilege.
  • Two circuits minimum, so a failed driver darkens a band of the ceiling and not the shop.

Eight questions before anyone measures

Send these with the enquiry. A supplier who answers all eight is a supplier; one who answers three is a brochure.

  • 01

    Which membrane exactly, and what is its light transmission in writing?

    Below about 50 % you are burning watts to heat the void. Ask for the number, not 'translucent'.

  • 02

    How much depth do I get above the membrane?

    Measure from finished ceiling level to the underside of the slab and any beams. This single number decides whether backlighting is even possible.

  • 03

    What ring diameter, and which ring?

    It is matched to the fitting's cut-out. A ring that is too small flexes and shows a halo; too big and the bezel does not cover the glue line.

  • 04

    Where do the drivers and controllers physically sit?

    Answer must be a place a person can reach without touching the ceiling.

  • 05

    How is the platform fixed, and to what?

    Threaded rod into the slab, or a bracket off a beam. 'Onto the old ceiling' is not an answer if the old ceiling is a suspended grid.

  • 06

    What spacing will you use at my depth?

    Expect: spacing equal to depth for 120° optics, 1.5× depth for 180°. Anything tighter is more LED than you need; anything wider bands.

  • 07

    Can you de-tension and re-tension this ceiling later, and what does that cost?

    Get the call-out number now. It is the difference between a serviceable ceiling and a sealed box.

  • 08

    Fire classification for the membrane, and for the LED gear?

    SANS 428 or EN 13501-1 for the film — B-s1,d0 is the usual commercial target — and a valid certificate on the power supplies.
