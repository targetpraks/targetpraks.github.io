The short answer

Nothing in the "heavy metal detox" aisle has ever been shown to help a child. The one treatment that genuinely removes metal — chelation — is a prescription drug for a measured high load, given in hospital, and it killed three children in three years when it was used outside that setting. So the protocol we can run is: measure this week, act on the number, remove the source if there is one.

If a practitioner has already given you a test result and a product list, send it to me before either child takes anything — that changes the answer, and it usually changes it toward "don't".

01 Two completely different things are called "detox"

Almost every dangerous decision here comes from mixing these two up. The product shelf borrows the authority of the hospital treatment, and the hospital treatment is the one with the body count.

Real medicine

Chelation

A prescription drug (succimer, CaEDTA) that binds a toxic metal and carries it out through the urine. Used only for a confirmed high blood level, or a symptomatic child, under a toxicologist. All FDA-approved chelating agents require a prescription precisely because they can only be used safely under supervision (FDA).

The product shelf

"Detox" for kids

Zeolite, bentonite clay, chlorella, cilantro drops, "binders", foot patches, nasal sprays, clay baths, and the urine kits sold alongside them. None of it has a trial in children showing a health benefit. The FDA warned eight companies in 2010 that these are unapproved drugs, and named kidney failure and death among the risks (FDA safety alert).

Why the shelf is not "gentle": a chelator cannot tell lead from calcium or zinc. The dose that pulls metal out of bone is the dose that can empty the blood of calcium and stop the heart — which is exactly how the three children in the CDC report died, after being given edetate disodium instead of the calcium-bound form (CDC MMWR 2006). One of them was a five-year-old given his third IV chelation for autism (The Guardian, 2005).

Read the succimer row twice. A real chelator, in a real trial, in 780 children, lowered the blood level and changed nothing about how those children thought, behaved or moved. If a prescription drug cannot do it, a clay powder will not.

03 The protocol — measure first, then act on the number

Three phases. Phase one costs one GP appointment and two lab tubes, and changes nothing about their diet or routine.

Phase 1 — this week

  1. One appointment, both children, venous blood lead level. Ask for a venous draw, not a finger-prick. A capillary sample picks up lead from the skin and reads falsely high, which is why CDC always confirms it with a vein (CDC testing). Lancet Laboratories publishes a lead-exposure bulletin and runs the assay, as do Ampath and PathCare (Lancet lead bulletin).
  2. Same draw: iron studies (ferritin, Hb, MCV). Iron deficiency raises lead absorption, and it also causes the tiredness and poor focus that send parents down this road in the first place. The CDC's own action list at every level includes checking and treating iron deficiency (CDC).
  3. Answer the twelve questions below. Ten minutes at the kitchen table. This is the part that finds the source if there is one — and in a South African house it is more informative than any test sold online.
  4. Change nothing else. No product goes into either child before a number exists. That is not caution for its own sake; it is the whole finding of section 02.

Phase 2 — when the number lands

Phase 3 — only if the number is high

Cape Town has no childhood lead-screening programme to fall back on, and the country's lead-in-paint limit of 90 ppm only became effective on 17 May 2025 — so every coat of paint in a house older than that is a legitimate question, not a scare (SGS on the 2024 regulations; Mail & Guardian on the gap). A confirmed level in the 20+ band buys a paediatrician, a home hazard assessment and, at 45+, a hospital bed — not a supplement.

04 The twelve questions that find a source

Ten minutes, no cost. Answer for the house they sleep in and any house they spend a day a week in. Two or more "yes" answers and we do not need a product — we need the specific room, pipe, pot or toy.

  • 1. When was the house built, and has anything been painted, sanded, stripped or renovated in the last three years — especially window frames, doors and stoeps?
  • 2. Is there chipped or peeling paint anywhere at a child's height, inside or on the outside wall they brush past?
  • 3. What is the incoming water pipe made of, and is there any lead solder on copper plumbing? (Old Cape Town properties still have galvanised and, rarely, lead service lines.)
  • 4. Any borehole, well or rainwater tank feeding a tap they drink from?
  • 5. Do they eat or drink from market pottery, glazed mugs or imported ceramics? Lead glaze is a classic source.
  • 6. Imported spices — turmeric above all — or any traditional remedy given by a family member?
  • 7. Cheap jewellery, keyrings, toy parts, fishing sinkers or curtain weights within reach of either child?
  • 8. Batteries, e-waste, scrap metal, solder, ammunition, stained glass or a home workshop in the house or garage?
  • 9. Does anyone in the household work with paint, batteries, scrap, sandblasting or shooting — and come home in those clothes?
  • 10. How much tuna, snoek or kingklip a week does each child eat? (This is the mercury question, and it has a portion answer, not a pill answer.)
  • 11. Any industry nearby — a smelter, battery recycler, sandblaster, or a busy road whose dust settles on a bare-soil play area?
  • 12. Has a practitioner already run a hair or urine test and handed over a protocol? Send it to me before it is used.

05 What we will not do, and why

Hard no

Nothing on this list touches either child

  • Chelation — IV or oral — outside a hospital, ever
  • Zeolite, bentonite clay, chlorella, cilantro, "binders", foot patches, clay baths, "TRS", "HMD"
  • A "provoked" or challenge urine metal test — it cannot tell you the body's load
  • A hair mineral test as a diagnosis — the labs disagree with each other on the same hair
  • Any detox product from a practitioner who has not shown you a validated lab result and a registered clinician's name
The tell

How to spot the funnel in ten seconds

A test you buy from the same person who sells the treatment. A week-by-week "protocol" with before-and-after testimonials. A practitioner who will not put the diagnosis in writing on a lab report. The FDA calls this out directly: the companies selling unapproved chelation products also sell the home test kits that "diagnose" the problem the product then treats (FDA Q&A).

06 What actually moves the needle — honestly

Modest, free, and none of it sold in a bottle. I would rather give you a small true effect than a large invented one.

  • Stop the exposure. The single lever with a real mechanism. If the twelve questions surface a source, removing it is worth more than every product in section 05 combined.
  • Iron and calcium adequacy. The CDC's action list at every blood level includes diet focused on iron and calcium, and testing and treating iron deficiency. Iron-deficient children absorb more lead — and iron deficiency alone explains a lot of tired, foggy, poor-focus school days (CDC).
  • The ordinary stack, for the thing you are probably chasing. Sleep regularity, movement, and reading are real but modest for focus and behaviour. They are already in the household's health plan for both children, and they cost nothing.
Calibration, because it matters: the Cochrane review found that even household education and dust control probably do not lower a child's blood lead, and made no measurable difference to how children developed over three to eight years. That is not a reason to skip the source hunt — it is the reason not to spend money on anything else. Prevention is the only place the effect lives.
