The answer on one page

Hexagons: yes as the register, no as the map-maker. A fixed national H3 grid is the right way to record, compare and enforce Papa Pasta territories. It is the wrong way to design them, and it does nothing about the hardest problem in this file, which is whether a store trades at R600,000 or R430,000 a month. Draw territories from demand, malls and roads; store them as cells. The grid is uniform; the territories built from it must not be. Most of the country should stay unassigned: half of South Africa's 60.0 million people live in 5,442 of its 1.57 million resolution-8 cells (0.35%).

Resolution: H3 resolution 8 for ownership. A resolution-8 cell in South Africa averages 0.78 km², with an edge of about 0.55 km and 0.95 km between neighbouring centres. A 2–3 km store catchment is 16–36 cells. A territory is about 80–400 cells for 3 to 14 stores, which compacts to roughly 30–125 IDs in a contract schedule. Use resolution 7 for planning views, 6 for national screening and 9 only for site analytics. Resolution 7 cannot carry the boundaries this atlas needs: competing reference malls sit 2.49 km apart (Brooklyn Mall and Loftus Park) and 2.95 km apart (Nelson Mandela Square and Hyde Park Corner). That gap is about the width of one resolution-7 cell (2.5 km).

The 38 clusters become 35 territories plus 3 areas run by Papa Pasta. At resolution 8, allocating each cell to the nearest reference site within 5 km, the clusters take 7,288 cells:

  • 13 survive as they stand.
  • 14 need a boundary drawn through contested cells. Twenty pairs of clusters claim about 500 of the same cells; the worst pairs are GT01/GT04 (80 cells) and GT13/GT14 (71).
  • 3 need infill to become contiguous.
  • 5 are two or three towns stapled together, 19–98 km wide.
  • The 3 Local clusters fall below the six-store floor.

The 135-zone template is a clean national partition, but no zone carries more than five stores. Retire it as a territory scheme and keep it as a reference layer.

25% equity per store pairs cleanly with cells, and badly with uncertainty. A site's cell decides whose 25% it is, which is the cleanest possible link between a territory and a capital obligation. But the 25% turns every territory into a leveraged bet on store volume:

  • At R600k a month the owner earns about 24% (3 stores) to 56% (14 stores) a year after tax.
  • At Luke's Express base of R430k the owner loses money at every tier.
  • At R287k the zone layer alone loses R0.5–1.1m a year, before any share of store losses.

The fee ladder does not survive in its current form. Once equity is compulsory, the R150k–R600k fee is 8–15% of the owner's ticket. It makes no difference to whether the territory pays, and it is charged upfront for stores that may never be built. Replace it with R150,000 on grant, payable after the cooling-off, plus R35,000 per store from the fourth, paid at that store's lease signature. At full build this raises R12.15m across 35 territories, against R12.45m on the ladder (−2.4%), and nothing ever has to be refunded for an unbuilt store.

The trust account: never Papa Pasta's, never pooled, never prefunded. Money follows stores.

  • The owner's 25% for a store is called only when that store's site is approved.
  • It is paid into an independent attorney's trust investment account in the owner's name. Under Legal Practice Act s86(4) the interest goes to the owner, with 5% to the Fidelity Fund.
  • It is released in step with the operator's 75%, to that store's company, against certified fit-out spend.
  • It is refunded automatically if the lease is not signed by a long-stop date.

Prefunding the network would put R89.0m into one pot held for 38 owners (R121.7m if owners hold their flagships outright). That is the shape the Reserve Bank stopped at Sharemax. It is far beyond the Banks Act's 20-person / R500,000 allowance for informal deposit-taking, and close to the statutory definition of a collective investment scheme.

Do this first: load the 38 clusters into the platform's new territory register as resolution-8 cell sets (drafts), resolve the ~500 contested cells, and publish nothing until a cell can belong to only one territory. Every obligation in this design — the 25%, the escrow call, the per-store fee — keys off "is this site in this owner's cells". Today none of the 38 clusters has any geometry at all.

2. Hexagons versus metros and clusters: the real trade-off

2.1 What the current maps actually are

  • v7.2 is a valid partition with the wrong units. No zone carries more than 5 stores, and 120 of 135 carry 3 or fewer even at the high estimate.
    • The 77 Voronoi zones cover 6.2% of the land (75,790 km²) but hold 157–224 of the stores. Their median size is 207 km².
    • The 58 full-municipality zones cover 93.8% of the land (1,145,031 km²) but hold only 77–133 stores. Their median size is 13,318 km² and the largest is 126,839 km².
  • Voronoi names describe places; Voronoi geometry describes distance to seed points. Checked pin by pin:
    • All three East London reference malls (Hemingways, Vincent Park, Beacon Bay) fall in zone 2.0 "BCM — Mdantsane/Duncan Village". Zone 1.0 "East London Central", whose key areas list Vincent and Beacon Bay, holds 31,100 people across 496 km².
    • Westville Mall and The Pavilion fall in zone 45.0 "Cato Ridge/Outer West".
    • Nelson Mandela Square falls in 13M "Rosebank/Parkhurst/Houghton", not in 11.0 "Sandton/Morningside".
    • Galleria Mall in Amanzimtoti falls in 44A "Umlazi/Isipingo", not in 44B "Chatsworth/Amanzimtoti/Bluff".
    • Sea Point's mall and Long Street have swapped zones (53.0 and 52.0).
  • The 38 active clusters have no geometry at all. The only spatial facts are 115 reference sites with published coordinates. Luke's review says plainly that "adjacent clusters require precise suburb and road boundaries before territory rights are sold" (page 11).

2.4 Verdict, and where I disagree with Ricardo

  1. Use the grid as the ledger, not the pen. Design territories from nodes: malls, catchments and barriers. Record them as cells. A grid that "composes" territories mechanically from uniform cells will cut through Sandton's malls and tile the Karoo.
  2. National grid, sparse territories. Every cell exists in the register, but only the 0.4–0.5% of cells around real sites should ever sit inside a territory. The 35 territories in section 5 take 6,880 of 1.57 million cells.
  3. Uniform cells, non-uniform value. The proposal's appeal is comparability. Comparability comes from the weights attached to cells — residents, workers, competitors, income — not from the cells being equal.
  4. The grid does not fix the economics. Whether a territory pays depends on store volume (section 6). A perfect map of a R430k store is still a loss.

I considered three alternatives and rejected each as the primary unit:

  • Municipal wards or census areas. They follow roads and carry census data, but they are re-demarcated on election and census cycles, inside an 8 + 5-year contract. v7.2's municipal third also shows the size problem.
  • A store-spacing radius per store. The platform already has spatial_policy.minimum_store_spacing. It is right for protecting operators and wrong for allocating a regional owner's development rights; use it alongside the cells.
  • A custom equal-area hex grid. It would remove the ±11% area variation but lose an open, documented standard with libraries in every mainstream language.

3.5 Why not resolution 6, 7 or 9

  • Resolution 6 is wrong for a store.
    • A single cell (38 km²) is bigger than a 3 km catchment (28 km²) and three times a 2 km one, so a store cannot be represented as a set of cells at all.
    • A three-store territory is one or two cells.
    • The 2.5–3 km gaps between competing malls are less than half a cell's width (6.6 km), so GT01 cannot be separated from GT04, nor GT13 from GT14.
    • Each boundary decision would move a whole store's catchment.
  • Resolution 7 is right for painting and wrong for the line.
    • A cell is 2.5 km across, which is the entire gap between Brooklyn Mall and Loftus Park (2.49 km) or Nelson Mandela Square and Hyde Park Corner (2.95 km).
    • A boundary at resolution 7 is placed to within ±1.25 km, which is the difference between owning a mall and not.
  • Resolution 9 is wrong for a territory.
    • A cell is 0.11 km², about a city block.
    • A 14-store territory would be 1,700–3,900 cells, and the national grid 11 million.
    • The population data cannot support it: one 1 km WorldPop pixel covers about seven resolution-9 cells, so most cells would get a whole pixel or nothing. Weighting becomes noise, and the schedule would imply a precision the data does not have.

3.6 Recommendation

  • Contract (membership) resolution: H3 resolution 8, one resolution nationwide. The library version is pinned and recorded on every territory revision.
  • A coarser ID in a schedule is shorthand for all of its resolution-8 descendants (H3 compaction). It never means the coarser cell's own polygon, because H3 children do not exactly tile their parent. The consolidation PRD makes the same point in HEX-01.
  • Planners may paint at resolution 7 and refine edges at 8. Regional screening uses resolution 6. Site analytics use resolution 9, and only with 100 m data.
  • This differs from the consolidation PRD's starting hypothesis, "res7 urban starting point" (docs/FRANCHISE-TERRITORY-CONSOLIDATION-PRD-2026-10-28.md, HEX-02 and the decision table). I agree with resolution 7 for planning views; the saved ownership resolution should be 8.

4. Composition rules

Parameters are starting values to tune on the 35 draft territories; the principle behind each is the recommendation.

  1. One grid, one resolution. H3 resolution 8. The library version is pinned and recorded on every revision.
  2. One cell, one territory. Within a commercial scheme version, a cell belongs to at most one territory, enforced by a primary key (section 8). Every cell not in an approved territory is reserved to Papa Pasta.
  3. Grant on nodes, keep on stores.
    • At grant, a territory is the set of cells nearer to its own reference nodes than to any other territory's, within 5 km of a node. Section 5 uses this allocation.
    • At month 36 (the review's development window), the territory shrinks to the cells within 3 rings (about 3 km) of stores opened or under lease, plus any holes they enclose. The shrink is computed, not negotiated.
  4. Contiguous. Territories are edge-connected at resolution 8. The one exception is outside metros: at most two parts, each holding a reference node and later a store, with the corridor between them left unassigned.
  5. Size floor and steps. Committed stores of 6, 10, 12 or 14. Never 3–5, and avoid 7–9: the working paper's overhead schedule adds a second zone manager at store seven.
  6. The boundary band. Where two territories' 5 km reaches overlap and a mapped barrier (national or metropolitan route, river, railway, ridge) runs through the overlap, the boundary follows the barrier and each cell goes to the side holding its centre. Otherwise each cell goes to the nearest node.
  7. Retail nodes are never split. Every cell touching a mall's land parcel goes to one territory.
  8. Setback for new sites. A new store must sit at least 2 rings (about 1.9 km) inside its territory's boundary, unless the neighbouring owner consents in writing.
  9. Flagship. It is the first store opened, at the territory's highest-demand node, and at least 3 rings (about 2.8 km) from any boundary. The territory grows outward from it, so the flagship anchors the core rather than the edge.
  10. Reach cap. No cell may be more than 8 km from a node or store. This is what prevents a 13,000 km² municipal "territory" around two shops.
  11. Provinces and municipalities. Every cell carries province and municipality codes, and reports split by them automatically.
  12. A territory may cross a provincial line only where one urban area straddles it; the Vaal Triangle is the live case.
  13. Licensing follows each store's municipality.
  14. The shape test for a 14-store territory. The territory is the union of the 3-ring disks around its planned sites, plus the holes they enclose.
  15. No neck may be narrower than 3 cells.
  16. It must fill at least 60% of its convex hull.
  17. The result is a defensible shape rather than a blob: every cell is within about 3 km of a site the owner can name.
  18. Weighting is an attribute, never membership. Residents, workers, competitors and income attach to cells by dataset version. Changing a dataset never moves a boundary.
  19. Delivery. Own-channel orders are allocated to the territory of the delivery address's cell. Aggregator orders belong to the dispatching store.
  20. The contract schedule. It states the scheme, version, territory code, revision number, cell count, SHA-256 of the sorted cell list, H3 library version, the compacted ID list, a GeoJSON of the union and a map. It says the cell list governs over the map.

6.4 Is per-store equity the right instrument to pair with a hex territory?

Yes for linkage, no for timing. The cell of a site decides the territory, and the territory decides who holds 25%: a mechanical rule with no room for argument. Equity is a better partner for cells than a territory fee, because cells are about where stores go and equity is about stores.

But equity makes every territory a bet on volume. The 3 points pay for the job, at 103–127% of overhead for 6–14 stores at R600k. All of the profit comes from dividends, and dividends vanish below about R414k a month.

So pair it with five rules:

  1. A volume gate. No territory is sold before store one trades at R540,000 a month or better for six consecutive months. This is the working paper's first test.
  2. Calls per approved site, never prefunding (section 7).
  3. A spacing rule. The owner's funding obligation applies only to sites at least 2 rings from an existing store. Infill below that needs the owner's consent, because cannibalisation is now the owner's money.
  4. 25% of total store capital, as defined in section 6.1.
  5. A pace cap in the development schedule, so that no more than a set number of calls fall in any 12 months.

On the flagship: choose reading A. An owner who controls one store outright, at the territory's best node, is an operator first and an investor second. That is the profile both the review and the franchisee rules ask for.

6.5 Does the fee ladder still make sense? No. What it should be instead

Four reasons the ladder no longer works:

  1. It is the wrong size to matter. The fee is 8–15% of the ticket. At R600k it moves a 14-store owner's return by 8 points (63.8% without it, 55.5% with it). At R430k the owner loses money with or without it.
  2. It prices the wrong thing. It is priced by store count, which the equity already prices, and it rewards over-promised targets. Section 5 shows the Strategic targets imply 2.4–3.9 km spacing.
  3. It is paid at the wrong time. It is paid upfront for stores that may take 36 months or never come. After the cooling-off, every unbuilt store becomes a refund argument. CPA Regulation 2(2)(b) requires provisions that prevent "unreasonable or overvaluation of fees".
  4. It is not the business. At full build, Papa Pasta's 2 points plus the R5,000 technology fee bring in R204,000 per store a year, R63.4m a year across 311 stores, against R12.9m of one-off territory fees.

Instead: R150,000 on grant, plus R35,000 per store from the fourth, paid at that store's lease signature from the same escrow call. All amounts are ex VAT.

7. The trust account

7.1 Why the design matters more than the wording

  • Prefunding is enormous. Prefunding every store's equity at signature would hold R89.0m (reading B) or R121.7m (reading A) in trust for 38 owners.
  • Prefunding parks capital for years. Take a 14-store owner who prefunds R4,007,500 with openings spread evenly over 36 months. The balance averages about R2.0m for three years, about R6.0m-years of idle capital. Calling per site, with about three months from call to final release, ties up about R1.0m-years: 83% less. At network scale, ten stores in build at once means R2.9m in escrow, not R89m.
  • It is far outside the Banks Act exclusion. The Banks Act's carve-out for occasional deposits stops at "more than twenty persons" or "more than R500 000" in aggregate. 38 owners and R89m are far past both limits.
  • There is a precedent. On 8 February 2012 the Reserve Bank's Registrar recorded that Sharemax's property-syndication "funding models … were in contravention of the Bank's Act". It had directed repayment to investors in September 2011 and put independent managers in control. Money raised from many people for developments not yet built is exactly the shape of a prefunded zone trust.
  • It weakens Papa Pasta's solvency certificate. Regulation 3(3) requires an auditor's or accounting officer's certificate that Papa Pasta can meet its "current and contingent liabilities". A refund book of R89m on Papa Pasta's own balance sheet makes that harder to give.

7.4 Pooling, collective investment schemes, FAIS and the Banks Act

  • Collective investment schemes (CISCA, Act 45 of 2002). A CIS is a scheme in which "members of the public are invited or permitted to invest money … in a portfolio", where two or more investors contribute and hold a participatory interest, and share the risk and the benefit in proportion to it.
    • A single trust into which many zone owners pay, used to fund stores across zones or to hold a pooled 25% for pooled returns, meets every element. Sales to 38 owners through a disclosure programme make the public-invitation element arguable. CISCA reserves administering a scheme to a registered manager (counsel to confirm the exact provisions and penalties).
    • The segregated design — one owner, one account, one store at a time, shares held directly — has no shared portfolio and no shared risk or benefit. This is a real risk if pooled, and largely designed away if segregated.
  • The Banks Act (94 of 1990). "The business of a bank" includes accepting deposits from the general public as a regular feature of the business, and the occasional-deposit exception is capped at twenty persons and R500,000.
    • "Deposit" excludes money paid as an advance or part payment under a contract for the sale or provision of property or services (if repayable only in specified events), and money paid "as security for the performance of a contract".
    • A per-store subscription escrow probably fits one of those exclusions. A pooled pot of refundable money for stores without sites looks like a deposit.
  • FAIS (Act 37 of 2002). "Financial product" includes "shares in a company other than a 'share block company'", participatory interests in a CIS, and Banks Act deposits.
    • So Papa Pasta arranging 25% stakes in private store companies, as a regular part of its business, could be advice or an intermediary service in a financial product.
    • The cleanest posture is that the owner subscribes directly with each store company under a standard subscription agreement, and Papa Pasta only approves sites and operators. Whether that is enough, or an authorised financial services provider must be involved, is counsel's call.
    • The Financial Markets Act's "securities" covers shares in public companies only, so the live question is the FAIS shares limb.
  • Companies Act. Each store company is private, and its shares go to one named owner and one named operator. Do not market the programme as "invest in Papa Pasta stores"; counsel should check the offer-to-the-public rules.

  2. Import the 38 clusters as commercial scheme papa-pasta-clusters version 1, each with revision 1 of kind unmapped. This records D1 truthfully without inventing geometry, which is the posture migration 016's header already requires.

9. The one thing to do first, and the strongest case against me

First: load the 38 clusters into the 016 register as resolution-8 cell sets, as drafts, and publish nothing until the contested cells are resolved and the allocation key exists. The 25% call, the escrow release and the per-store fee all attach to one question — is this site in this owner's cells — and today it has no answer. The work is the PRD's milestone M4, estimated there at 4–7 days. Running it will immediately show Ricardo the twenty contested boundaries and six stapled clusters that the named atlas hides.

The strongest argument against this recommendation: building a national cell register before a single store has traded is precision layered on a guess.

  • The R600k volume is unproven, the Strategic targets already imply overlapping catchments, and the legal gate is shut: the disclosure document is still a template and no zone can be sold.
  • A named-suburb schedule plus a store-spacing radius would do for three pilot zones, and the cell work could wait for store one.

My answer: the cell work is days, not months. It is the cheapest way to stop the mis-quoting already in the file (R150k against R3.9m for the same streets), and it exposes the conflicts before anyone has paid for them. But I concede the sequencing point fully. The grid's precision must never stand in for proof of volume. No territory, and no equity call, before store one trades at R540,000 a month or better for six months.
