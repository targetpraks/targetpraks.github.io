Ten dimensions, both ceilings

Ten things decide it, scored side by side. Higher is better. The scores are a reading of the evidence in the table and the sources at the end — not a manufacturer's datasheet.

The one-line read. Plaster is a construction product: cheap, repairable, understood by everyone, and it cracks. Stretch canvas is a finish product: seamless, damp-proof, installed in a day, and it costs multiples per square metre. Buy plaster for the house and stretch for the room that has a problem worth paying to make disappear.

Page 3 of 6  ·  The money

What it costs, and what it costs later

Two different questions: what it costs to put up, and what it costs to live under for twenty years. Stretch looks expensive on the first and can look catastrophic on the second, because its lifespan decides whether you buy the membrane once or twice.

The number that decides the argument. If the membrane lasts 20–30 years, stretch costs roughly 3× plaster over twenty years. If it needs replacing at year 15 — which is what SA installers themselves quote as the lifespan — it costs 5–15×. Ask the installer for the membrane's expected life in writing, not the salesman's opinion. That single answer is worth more than the quote.

How each one lets you down

Both systems have a failure mode, and both are predictable. This is what actually goes wrong, and the questions that keep it from happening to you.

Plaster

Cracks along the board joints

The most common complaint in South African homes. Trusses move, the joint tape shears, and a hairline appears exactly where the plasterer's joint was. Under a gloss or sheen paint it reads from across the room.

Plaster

Water damage from above

A slow roof leak sags the board, stains the paint and eventually drops a panel — usually onto something you liked. The ceiling is the first place the damage shows and the last place it gets fixed.

Plaster

Mould in wet rooms

Board and paint film hold moisture in a bathroom without extraction. Once mould is in the plaster, painting over it is decoration, not repair.

Plaster

Waviness under sheen

A skimmed ceiling is never perfectly flat. Matte hides it, gloss punishes it, and a badly bedded joint shows as a long shadow line at dusk.

Stretch

Heat and sharp edges

The membrane softens near a hot downlight, an oven or hard afternoon sun through a skylight. A ladder, a broom handle or a child's toy tears it, and a torn membrane is a replacement, not a patch.

Stretch

A bad perimeter

The track needs a flat, sound wall line. On a wobbly plaster edge or below a cornice you get a gap, a ripple or a caulked seam — and that is what you will look at for fifteen years.

Stretch

Gloss shows everything

A glossy or mirror membrane reflects the room and every imperfection in the substrate and the light fittings. Matte and satin are far more forgiving, and most SA installs should be matte.

Stretch

It hides a growing leak

Holding water is a feature until it becomes a delay. The membrane sags under the weight and gives you days of warning — but only if you look up.

Six things the quote will not mention

  1. The perimeter is extra. Fixing a crooked wall line, removing a cornice or boxing a beam is priced per metre and rarely in the headline rate.
  2. Every downlight is a line item. Each fitting needs a ring bonded into the membrane, priced per point, and moving one later means cutting the membrane.
  3. Panels stop at about 5 m. A wider room gets a seam. Ask where it will fall before you sign.
  4. Fire classification is a document, not a claim. Commercial, rental and sectional-title work needs the membrane's rating in writing, and PVC and polyester do not behave the same.
  5. Access hatches are cheap now. Add them at install for the geyser, the valve and the wiring — after the fact they cost a re-tension.
  6. Get “removable and refittable” in writing. Suppliers contradict each other on whether a membrane can come down and go back up. If access matters to you, the answer has to be on paper.

Assumptions, stated plainly

  • No South African stretch-ceiling specialist publishes a rate card. Every SA supplier contacted online quotes per room, so the installed figures here are foreign retail benchmarks converted, not SA quotes. Expect SA labour to sit below the UK and EU numbers — get two local quotes before you commit.
  • The twenty-year model uses a 20 m² room, a standard residential ceiling and no scaffolding, cornices, bulkheads or electrical work. Each of those changes the answer.
  • The ten scores are judgement, not measurement. They are weighted toward what a homeowner notices in the first five years, which is why cracking and dust score heavily.
  • Lifespan is the weakest number in the whole comparison: SA installers say 10–15 years, premium international suppliers say 20–30. The document shows both rather than picking one.

Two decisions left, and they are yours

Decision one

Is this a budget ceiling or a finish ceiling? Budget ceiling: flush plaster, no argument. Finish ceiling: keep reading.

Decision two

Does the room have moisture, or a ceiling too cracked or too ugly to skim? Two yeses, or a double-volume you cannot reach without scaffolding, and stretch canvas earns its price. Anything else, plaster does the job for a third of the money.
