Section 01

The decision

We build. We build in Go. And we build the boring half — the part that makes an approval provable — before the clever half.

Filestage works. That is not the problem. The problem is what our own workspace says about how we work inside it: 144 reviews past due, 89 of them waiting on a named person who never decided, 56 files with no due date at all, and 47 requests for revision that never came back — the worst still open after 338 days. The software did not fail. It has no opinion about whether a review finishes.

PageProof has that opinion, and charges for it. Its per-step thresholds, gatekeeper release and per-proof audit are the three things our workspace cannot do. But a fifty-person studio with five brands does not need a subscription to a proofing platform; it needs a review system that is ours, wired into Zoho, and one we can read the source of when a client disputes a sign-off.

Why Go, and where Rust would be wrong

Almost all of this system's work is orchestration: accept an upload, hash it, render a preview, write a row, emit an event, notify the right person, and prove later that all of it happened in that order. That is I/O concurrency and a database, not compute. Go's standard library gives us HTTP, TLS, multipart streaming and S3 signing with no framework; its scheduler makes a worker pool a fifty-line file; and a single static binary with the front end embedded means one artifact to back up, hand over and reason about.

Rust would buy memory safety we do not need in a service that mostly waits, at two to three times the delivery cost and with a far smaller hiring pool in this city. The genuinely heavy work — PDF rasterising, video transcoding, image resizing — is done by poppler, ffmpeg and libvips whichever language calls them. Choosing Rust would not make a single thumbnail faster.

Where Rust earns a place, if it ever does

One function, measured before it is written: perceptual hashing and near-duplicate detection across the whole version archive — pHash, dHash and a colour-histogram distance over roughly 40k images, run at ingest. If the Go implementation cannot hash the archive inside the ingest budget, that one function becomes a Rust library called over cgo or run as a sidecar. Nothing else. No second service and no second language in the repository until a number demands it.

What “done” means for v1

  • A designer uploads a version. A client opens one link on a phone, sees the artwork, pins a comment, decides, and re-authenticates to sign. The project manager is updated without anyone retyping anything.
  • Every round has a due date, and the system chases it — the reviewer first, their manager second, the owner with a list third.
  • Any approval can be shown months later: who decided, when, from where, against which file hash, and what was withdrawn since.
  • The whole thing runs on the Mac mini and the NAS beside it, and moves to a small managed host unchanged.

Section 02

What the teardown proved

Both platforms were opened in a controlled browser, their shipping code and UI dictionaries read, and their vocabulary compared key by key. What follows is what the code says, not what the sales page says.

Six specialist reviews ran against one evidence set. Three findings change what we build.

1. The decision vocabulary is not the differentiator

Filestage does ship approved with changes, rejected and custom decisions — behind an early-access flag and a plan feature called Extra review decisions. PageProof's advantage is therefore not naming, it is arithmetic: a step that waits for all approvers, or one, or N of them. A proofing tool that cannot express “this must be signed by Legal and by the client, in that order” is a shared folder with opinions.

2. The audit log is the product

PageProof records every decision, every change of mind and every withdrawn approval, per proof, and exports it. Filestage records activity, but the commercial object — this exact file was approved by this person at this time — is first-class only in PageProof. The moment a packaging claim is challenged, that record is the only thing worth money.

3. AI review is a checklist, not a colleague

Filestage's seven agents are narrower than their names suggest: barcode and QR decoding, forbidden terms, spelling, metadata checks, image matching against a supplied reference, and text compliance against an uploaded guideline. Each is a deterministic check or a bounded prompt. None of them approves anything. That is the correct shape, and it is buildable.

The design consequence

Everything that makes a review finish is a rule the system enforces, not a feature the reviewer may use. Dates, thresholds, delegates and signatures are schema-level obligations. If a review can be created without them, our people will create it without them — that is exactly what 56 undated files and 144 overdue reviews already say.

Section 03

Product definition

One product, one primary path, five people who use it differently, and a deliberate list of things it will never be.

The product in one sentence

Signet is the room where a piece of work is shown, argued over, changed and signed off — and the record of that happening. It is not a task manager, not a digital asset manager, and not file sharing with a comment box.

The primary path — intake is a door, not a corridor

Work does not start in one system. It arrives from Cliq, from an email, from a WorkDrive folder, from a website brief, from a person typing into Signet, or from an agent that was told to pre-plan next month. Signet does not care where it came from; it cares that a thing has a name, a brand, a due date and an owner before anyone is asked to review it. Zoho Projects is one adapter among several and nothing depends on it. If Projects disappears tomorrow, this path still runs.

  1. Something arrives — a brief, a file, a link, a request in a chat. Signet records the source and who asked.
  2. It becomes an asset in a project, on a brand, with a kind: artwork, document, video or web.
  3. A first version is uploaded (or captured, for a web page). Signet hashes it, renders it, and opens a round with a due date.
  4. Reviewers are invited by role — approvers, a gatekeeper, mandatory reviewers, observers — each with a threshold, in the space the workflow template puts them in.
  5. They comment on the thing itself, and one of them decides. A decision that carries authority requires re-authentication.
  6. The engine evaluates the threshold: satisfied, return with a to-do list, or blocked. A satisfied step releases into the next space.
  7. A new version supersedes the open round without losing a comment or a decision from the old one.
  8. When the last step is satisfied the asset is approved, the signature is sealed, the audit chain is anchored, and whoever asked hears about it — in Cliq, by email, by webhook, or on the Projects task if that adapter is on.

Spaces: the shape a team works in

A space is a room with its own members, its own theme and its own idea of what is visible. Internally a studio might run a designer space, an interim-manager space and a main-manager space; the client gets a client space that looks and reads nothing like them. A template is the ordered list of steps across those spaces — each step naming a space, a role, a threshold and a service level. Design it once, and every asset on that brand walks it.

Some work needs two levels. Client work needs four. A regulated pack needs five, with a legal gate and a print check. The template is the setting; the asset follows it and never carries a second history to make the spaces work.

What Signet will never be

  • Not a DAM. It holds the versions under review. Finished artwork goes to WorkDrive.
  • Not a project manager. Timelines, budgets, capacity and client billing are not built here; where a team uses Zoho Projects it is linked, not replaced — and if nobody uses it, nothing breaks.
  • Not a public product. One organisation, many brands, invited guests. No signup, no billing code.
  • Not a mobile app in v1. A responsive web proof on a phone is the requirement; a store binary is not.
  • Not an AI that decides. Agents are members: they can plan, upload, arrange and annotate. A decision that binds the company is still a person’s, re-authenticated.

Invariants

  • A version belongs to exactly one asset. Uploading “the same file again” creates a new version, never a second asset.
  • One assignment, at most one live decision. Changing your mind supersedes the old row; it never overwrites it.
  • A round names the version it was opened against. A new version opens a new round; it does not edit the old one.
  • A closed round is immutable. Only a superseding round can change what the asset’s current state is.
  • An event is never updated or deleted. The application’s database role has INSERT on event and no UPDATE or DELETE grant; a trigger rejects both anyway.
  • Nothing is hard-deleted while a signature references it. Archive hides; retention deletes only after the signature window has passed.
  • Released implies signed. A round may only be released while a set of live, un-withdrawn signatures meeting its threshold exists. A withdrawal that breaks that set reverses the release rather than leaving it standing.
  • A round happens in exactly one space. The space is copied from the template step when the round opens; moving an asset to a different space is a new round, never an edit.
  • The template is immutable once used. Editing a template writes a new version, so a path that ran last month still reads the way it ran.
  • One history, whatever the spaces. A space changes what a person is shown; it never changes what happened. Every event is visible to the owner in every space.
  • Only a human principal signs. An agent may hold an assignment to review and comment; the decision column, the signature table and the release guard all refuse a non-human principal.
  • Every round has a due date. The column is not null with no default, so no code path can forget it.

Why spaces are a table and not a label. A label would not carry a theme, a member list or a visibility rule, and it could not answer the question that matters in a dispute: what did the client see at 14:02 on the day they approved? A space copies its visibility rules into every read, so that question has an answer that does not depend on today’s settings.

The event log

Every write that matters emits one event: version.created, round.opened, round.released, round.returned, assignment.invited, comment.created, decision.made, decision.withdrawn, signature.sealed, signature.withdrawn, asset.approved, asset.archived. Each carries prev_hash, so the chain verifies from the first event to the last without any external service.

Once a day a job takes the head hash, writes it to audit_anchor(day, head_seq, head_hash), and mails the same line to an address we do not control. That single outside copy is what makes tampering detectable: someone with database access can rebuild the chain, but cannot rewrite yesterday’s email.

Five schema mistakes to avoid

  1. Storing “approved” as a boolean on the asset. It cannot say who, when, against which version, or what it replaced.
  2. Deleting a version to save space. The proof chain breaks and the dispute goes with it.
  3. Making the due date nullable “for drafts”. Drafts become the norm — 56 undated files is what that looks like.
  4. Putting comments in the event log. Comments are content and belong in their own table; events reference them by id.
  5. Using the asset id as the review identity. Rounds come and go; the asset outlives them.

Threshold evaluation

The engine is a pure function of committed state. It runs after every write that can change a decision, and never on a schedule.

func evaluate(r Round, as []Assignment) Outcome {
    live := liveDecisions(as)                   // superseded_at IS NULL
    for _, role := range r.RequiredRoles {      // gatekeeper, then mandatory, then approvers
        mine := filterByRole(live, role)
        switch r.Threshold {
        case ThresholdAll:
            if len(mine) < countRole(as, role)          { return Waiting }
            if anyRejected(mine)                        { return Blocked }
        case ThresholdOne:
            if len(mine) == 0                           { return Waiting }
            if anyRejected(mine) && !anyApproved(mine) { return Blocked }
        case ThresholdN:
            if approvedCount(mine) < r.ThresholdN        { return Waiting }
            if anyRejected(mine)                        { return Blocked }
        }
    }
    if r.GatekeeperReleased { return Released }
    return Waiting                              // quorum met, gatekeeper has not released
}

A quorum met is a state, not an event: the round rests at satisfied until a gatekeeper releases it. Two arithmetic rules matter as much as the three below. Exhaustion blocks — if every assignment has decided and the threshold is still unmet, the round goes to blocked, never to an eternal wait. And “all” means all of the current pool, so removing a reviewer lowers the bar; that is deliberate, and it is written to the audit as a denominator change.

Three rules do most of the work. Mandatory reviewers block, they do not approve: their presence is required for quorum, and their decision value is irrelevant to release. Approved-with-changes does not release anything: it is a conditional yes, so the round enters changes_required, the clock keeps running, every change becomes an open revision item, and the step stays unfinished until a new version lands and the reviewer re-confirms. A rejection blocks the step outright — the only way out is a new version, never a majority.

Two ways to decline, and neither is silence

A decline is a fork, not a dead end. The person rejecting chooses one of two outcomes, and the engine will not accept the decision until they do:

ChoiceWhat the engine doesWhat the record saysClock
Decline and stopThe asset closes as declined; open rounds close; no new round is created; the owner is told once.A decision with a reason and the word stop; the asset keeps its history and can be reopened deliberately later.Stopped
Decline and start againA rework round opens on the same asset, linked to the rejecting decision, with a due date and the same roles; revision items are created for each objection.A decision with rework plus the chain to the round it produced — so the redo is visibly the child of the decline, not a fresh idea.Restarts on the new round

Both write a decision with a reason, both appear on the asset, and both stop the clock — but one ends the asset’s life honestly and the other puts it back into work with a countdown. What cannot happen is the thing that happens today in chat: an objection that nobody can find three weeks later.

Handing off between spaces

Release does not only move a state; it moves the work into the next room. When step n is satisfied and released, the engine opens step n+1 in the space the template names, with that space’s role, threshold and SLA, and sends the invites from that space’s theme. Four things follow:

  • The depth is the template’s business. Two steps or five; a legal gate can sit between main manager and client, and a print check after approval. Nothing in the code assumes four.
  • The client space is a different product to the client. Its own logo, its own wording, no internal due dates, no internal comments, agent findings hidden by default. Same asset, same history underneath.
  • Internal commentary never leaks. A comment is scoped to the space it was written in; an internal note is not “hidden with CSS”, it is not returned by the API to a client-space session.
  • The owner sees one asset, not four views. A single timeline shows every step, every space and every handoff, because that is the view a dispute needs.

Due dates and escalation

  • At creation: a round cannot exist without a due date. The four templates offer tomorrow, three days, a week, or a date — and a value must be picked.
  • At 80% of the window: one quiet nudge to the reviewer alone.
  • On the due date: a nudge that names the owner too, and the round turns amber on the board.
  • At +1 day: the delegate is notified if one exists; otherwise the brand manager.
  • At +3 days: the round turns red, appears in the owner’s daily list, and leads the Monday brief.
  • A closed loop: “changes required” is not an ending. The round stays open and keeps counting time, a revision item is opened for each objection, and it closes only when a new version lands and the reviewer re-confirms — or when the owner waives it with a stated reason. Nothing is marked done because the client went quiet.

Concurrency

Withdrawal is the sharp edge. When an approval is withdrawn, the system checks the invariant “released implies signed” inside the same transaction: if the withdrawn signature was part of the set that released the step, the release is marked reversed and every downstream step it enabled is marked stale before anything else happens. Silent continuation is the one failure mode that would make the whole record worthless.

Every mutation runs in one transaction that takes a row lock on the round (select … for update), writes its decision or event, then evaluates. Two reviewers deciding at once therefore serialise on that row, and the second evaluation sees the first decision. The release event is written once, guarded by a partial unique index on (round_id) where kind = 'round.released', so a retry cannot release twice.

Tests before this engine is trusted

  1. With a threshold of all and one of two approvers decided, the round does not release.
  2. A threshold of one releases on the first approval, and a later rejection pauses the next step.
  3. Approved-with-changes counts toward quorum; rejected never does.
  4. Withdrawing an approval after release pauses the next step and notifies the owner.
  5. Uploading a new version supersedes the round and copies assignments forward as awaiting.
  6. Removing an approver with a live decision supersedes it and re-evaluates.
  7. Two simultaneous approvals produce exactly one release event.
  8. A guest link cannot produce a second decision and cannot be replayed.
  9. A round past its due date still accepts a decision and records it as late.
  10. A manual approval requires a reason and is marked manual in the audit.
  11. A custom decision key not in the config is refused at the API boundary.
  12. Exhaustion blocks: three reviewers, threshold all, one rejects and two approve → blocked, not waiting.
  13. A decision against a changed version set is refused as stale and the reviewer must re-decide.
  14. The event chain verifies from genesis after a full restore from backup.

Realtime: server-sent events

SSE, not WebSockets: the traffic is one-directional and low-volume (a decision lands, a comment appears, a derivative becomes ready), it survives proxies as plain HTTP, and it reconnects on its own with Last-Event-ID where the browser resumes from the last event id it saw. The stream carries only ids and types; the client refetches the object. Nothing important is entrusted to a dropped connection.

Three limits are set on purpose: six concurrent streams per person (a seventh closes the oldest), a replay buffer of the last 1,000 events per scope, and a resync instruction when a client resumes from an event id older than the buffer — the client refetches its state and carries on. An SSE stream holds no database connection: it is fed from an in-process broker, so two hundred idle guest tabs cost sockets, not pool slots.

How a job is claimed and capped

A worker claims with SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1 inside a short transaction, sets locked_until = now() + 5 minutes, and works outside the transaction. Finished jobs NOTIFY a channel the API listens on; a reaper returns anything whose lock expired. Per-kind concurrency is capped in one table — media 2, agent 3, email 4, webhook 8 — so a hundred uploaded pages cannot starve the approvals out of the database. Every job is retried only on errors that are actually transient; a failed agent run is reported to the project, never silently retried at cost.

Media pipeline

  • Upload is presigned multipart straight to the object store; the API never proxies bytes. The completion call receives the parts, hashes the object server-side, and rejects a mismatch with the client’s claimed sha256.
  • Ingest queues one fan-out job per version. PDFs go through poppler, images through libvips, video through ffmpeg. Every derivative records the tool version that made it.
  • Failure is visible. A version whose preview failed shows “preview unavailable — original is intact”, with a retry, and the review can still proceed against the original download. We never block a decision on a thumbnail.

Go, and the one place Rust could earn its place

The service is I/O-bound; Go’s concurrency model and standard library map onto it directly. The single exception remains the archive-wide perceptual hashing described in Section 1: if the Go implementation cannot hash 40k images inside the ingest budget, that function moves to Rust and nothing else does.

  • Config from environment plus a single signet.toml; secrets only from the environment, never a file in the repo.

Data protection

  • In transit: TLS 1.3 only, HSTS, no mixed content; internal calls over the private network.

Five access-control mistakes that would hurt most

  1. Trusting the UI for authorisation. The policy check lives in the handler, and a test asserts that every mutating route calls it.
  2. Putting the brand in a query parameter. Scope comes from the token or the session, never from the client’s claim.
  3. Logging the magic link. A token in a log file is a skeleton key; redact at the logging boundary.
  4. Letting an admin “fix” a decision. Correction is a new decision with a reason, or an amendment event; never an UPDATE.

The annotation contract

An agent’s output is a comment — the same table, the same anchors, the same thread machinery as a human’s. That is what keeps it from becoming a separate panel nobody reads, and what lets a designer reply “fixed in v4” to a barcode check exactly as they would to a person.

When a person says the agent is wrong

  • A dispute is a first-class record. Any reviewer can mark an agent comment as wrong with one reason from a fixed list: not a defect, rule misread, wrong element, duplicate, out of scope. No free-text essay required.
  • Disputes are the quality signal, not a complaint box. Upheld disputes feed the golden set. An agent whose finding is twice upheld as wrong on the same rule is muted for that brand until its rules or prompt change, and the mute is visible on the project.
  • A quote must be found, not claimed. For every finding that cites a guideline clause, the server re-finds the quoted sentence in the extracted text by exact substring and stores its offset and hash. A quote that cannot be located is downgraded to “unverified” and never shown as a compliance failure.
  • Bad text in, no finding out. If the OCR confidence for a page is below 0.90 the text-based agents skip that page and say so, rather than inventing findings from garbled characters.

Guardrails

  • Confidence gates, stated per agent. Below 0.75, a model finding is held in a “possible” tray visible only to the designer; below 0.5 it is discarded silently and counted in metrics.
  • Deterministic checks never guess. A barcode either decodes or does not.
  • Dispute is one click. “Not a problem” records the agent, the finding and the reason, and feeds the false-positive metric for that agent.
  • Widening happens on evidence. An agent moves from “possible” to “always on” for a brand when its false-positive rate over that brand’s last 100 comments is under 3%.

Worked example: barcode and QR

  1. Read: the page is rendered at 300dpi, cropped around the detected symbol, and decoded. The library reports symbology, payload and a quality score.
  2. Compare: payload is checked against the brief’s expected code, and the symbology, quiet zone and module width are checked against the brand’s barcode standard (a 0.3mm module on a 40mm-wide symbol is measurable).
  3. Report: one comment pinned at the symbol — “decodes as 6001234567890, brief says …897” — with a blocking severity, and a second comment if the quiet zone is under spec.
  4. Why not an LLM: a language model reading a barcode is guessing. A decoder either decodes or fails; that is the whole value, and it is free.

Worked example: compliance against a guideline

  • Chunk by rule, not by page. The guideline is parsed into numbered clauses; each chunk carries its clause number and page.
  • Every finding quotes its rule. The prompt returns the clause id and the exact sentence the agent believes breaks it; the UI shows both, side by side.
  • No rule, no finding. If the model cannot cite a clause, the finding is dropped before it reaches the reviewer — the prompt requires the citation field, and the parser rejects a finding without one.
  • Text only, in v1. Compliance against a visual (a claim in a logo lockup, a colour that implies a health benefit) is out of scope and said so in the UI.

What an agent may do

Agents are members of the team, not a feature bolted onto a viewer. With a scoped token an agent can set up next month before anyone asks, and the work it did is visible and reversible.

  • Annotate: comment, reply, and resolve only its own threads.
  • Report: read what it is scoped to, and write a summary onto the asset or into Cliq.

What an agent may not do, and why the line sits there

  • It may not decide, sign or release. A decision binds the company and carries a signature; a signature belongs to a person who re-authenticated. An agent may recommend — “this is ready, all four objections closed” — and a human still presses the button.
  • It may not appear as a human. Every event it writes says kind = agent, and the UI tags it. No borrowed names, no borrowed avatars.
  • It may not move a due date later or abandon a round. Those change what the company promised someone; a human does those.
  • It may not post without a cited rule, decode or measurement, and may not see another brand’s content or anything outside the version it was run against.
  • It may not extend its own reach: no new tokens, no wider scope, no new members.

How the sync stays honest

  • Outbox, not fire-and-forget. An integration write is a row in outbox in the same transaction as the event. A worker delivers it, retries with backoff, and dead-letters for a human after eight attempts.
  • Two-way where it is safe. Where Projects is on, it may change a task’s due date or assignee and we mirror that onto the brief. It may not approve anything, and switching it off leaves the review path untouched.
  • Reconciliation, not hope. A nightly job compares the last 200 linked tasks with the last 200 open rounds and reports drift to the owner.

The one screen (owner)

Three columns: late (red, with who is holding it and for how long), waiting on us (amber, ours to fix), signed this week (green, with the audit link). Nothing else. If a number needs explaining, it opens a list, not a chart.

The Monday brief

Generated as text, not a dashboard: overdue rounds, rounds that returned and never came back, and anything waiting on the owner's signature. It is written to be read in ninety seconds and it links each line to the round.

The load board: an open book

Everyone on the team sees who is carrying what. Not as a scoreboard for blame — as the thing that stops the same three people being quietly buried. Visible to every staff member, never to a guest:

  • Who is holding what: open rounds per person, with the oldest first and days late beside it.
  • Who is producing: versions uploaded per person this month, and comments closed.
  • Who is overdue: a single list, everyone’s name on it, nothing hidden behind an owner login.
  • What is stuck: rounds returned with changes and no new version — the pile nobody used to see.

Two limits keep it useful rather than poisonous: it shows counts and lateness, never a quality rating, and it never appears in a client space. A guest sees their own review and its due date; they do not see which designer is busy.

What we refuse to build

  • A quality ranking of people. Load and lateness are facts; “best designer” is an opinion dressed as a metric.
  • Charts with no action. If a chart has no linked list behind it, it does not ship.
  • Vanity counts. Comments per round, files per project and “engagement” are not measures of a review that finished.

Deliberately not in v1

  • Real-time co-editing of a proof. Review is asynchronous by nature; a cursor war adds cost and confusion.
  • Native InDesign or Illustrator rendering. We proof the exported PDF, which is what the client signs.
  • Chat inside a proof. Comments are anchored to art; general chat belongs in Cliq.
  • Our own video transcoding farm. One ffmpeg worker per host, two renditions, no ladder.
  • Public links without an expiry. Every guest link dies in 72 hours or on first use.
  • A mobile app. The proof views must work on a phone browser; that is the requirement.
  • Per-seat billing, plans or trials. This is an internal product with external guests, not a SaaS.
  • A generic form builder. Briefs are structured; free-form field design is how briefs rot.

Acceptance criteria for every P0 feature

Each line is a test, not an aspiration. If it cannot be demonstrated in the running system, the feature is not done. Where two features share a test, both are named on the line.

  • R-01 — a reviewer can place a pin on page 3 at 60% zoom, and the same pin lands on the same spot for a second reviewer on a phone.
  • R-05 — a guest opens one link, sees only one brand’s asset, comments without an account, and the access is in the audit log with time and IP.
  • W-01 / W-02 — a round assigned to two approvers with a threshold of all does not release until both decide, and an observer’s comment changes nothing.
  • W-03 — creating a round without a due date is refused by the API and by the database.
  • W-04 — a gatekeeper can return a round with a to-do list, and every prior decision shows as awaiting re-confirmation.
  • W-05 — an approver can change and withdraw a decision while the round is open, and both appear in the audit in order.
  • P-01 / P-02 — a PDF and an image both open with the first page or frame visible in under a second on a 20Mbps connection.
  • P-03 — anchors survive a re-render from the same original with zero pixel drift at three zoom levels.
  • A-01 / A-02 — an approval requires step-up authentication, is bound to the version hash, and the chain verifies after a restore.
  • AC-02 / AC-03 — a guest token for brand A returns 404 for brand B’s asset, and the role matrix test covers every cell in this document.
  • E-01 / E-02 — two simultaneous approvals produce exactly one release event.
  • E-03 — uploading v4 supersedes the open round, carries assignments forward as awaiting, and loses no comment.
  • E-04 — three reviewers, threshold all, one rejects and two approve: the round ends blocked the moment the last assignment decides, and the owner is told once.
  • E-05 — the same decide call replayed with the same request id returns the first result, and a decision against a superseded version set is refused as stale.
  • SP-01 / SP-03 — a four-step template releases a proof from the design space to the client space, and a client-space session cannot read a single internal comment by API.
  • SP-02 — a two-level and a five-level template both run to approval, and editing a template mid-flight leaves running assets on the version they started with.
  • W-10 — a decline-and-stop closes the asset with a reason and creates no further round.
  • W-11 — a decline-and-start opens a rework round linked to the decision, with a due date and the same roles.
  • W-12 — approved-with-changes leaves the round open, in changes_required, still counting time, with one revision item per objection.
  • K-01 — a PDF, a DOCX, an MP4 and a URL each become an asset, take a pin or a timecode, and reach a decision.
  • AG-01 / AG-02 — a token creates a project, plans three rounds and uploads a version; the timeline marks every one as an agent; the decide endpoint refuses the same token.

Build order

Four stages, ordered by dependency and by what a person can see working. No dates: a stage is done when its own exit test passes.

Filestage stays until the last stage

This is a company-wide tool, so the old one does not go away when stage two looks good. Filestage and PageProof-class tooling stay live until all four stages have passed their exit tests, including agents, integrations, reporting and retention — and until one full monthly cycle has run on Signet without a fallback. Cut over per brand, never per person, and keep the read-only archive after that.
