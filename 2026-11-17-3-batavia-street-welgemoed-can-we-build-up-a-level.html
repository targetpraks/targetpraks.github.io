Can we build up a level at 3 Batavia Street, Welgemoed?

Erf 465, Bellville (Welgemoed), 7530 · owner R M Nova Prop (Pty) Ltd · zoning and overlays read live 16 November 2026 · the 1984 approved plan set read 17 November 2026 · revised after a five-model review

The answer

Yes — but the house is already two levels, so this is a height question with a datum inside it.

The approved plans are now in hand and they confirm your instinct: the 1984 house was built with a lower level cut into the slope. A keldervloer (basement floor) sits at 48.99 m with a playroom and stores on it, and the main floor sits at 51.68 m — 2.7 m above it. The top of the roof is 55.08 m. The erf falls about 8 m from the Totius Road side down to Batavia Road, so the same building reads as one storey from above and two from below.

That changes the shape of the answer, in your favour on one point and against you on another:

  • It is not a violation, and it is not a "two storey" limit you have used up. The by-law contains no storey count at all — no definition of "storey", no maximum number of levels. It regulates metres: 9.0 m to the wallplate and 11.0 m to the top of the roof on an erf this size. Floor space is the sum of all levels including basements — and at ~580 m² you are nowhere near the 1,500 m² ceiling.
  • So the question is only where the 9 m and 11 m are measured from — and on a site with 8 m of fall, that is the whole ballgame. The by-law answers it: from the "existing ground level", which means the level shown on the City's Ground Level Map, and the City may send a surveyor onto the erf to fix it.
  • The number to design to: a further level takes the top of the roof to about 57.8 m. That is 3.2 m above the high side of the erf and about 9.1 m above the ground at the street-side datum of 48.7 — right on the wallplate line, and 11.3 m above the lowest corner of the site. So a full-footprint third level is at risk; a level set back over the uphill half almost certainly fits.

Two gates closed since revision 2: the house was built in 1984, so it is 42 years old and the 60-year heritage permit does not apply — no Heritage Western Cape process. And the approved plans are no longer missing — we have them, so the storey count and the boundaries can be measured off a real drawing instead of guessed.

Still open: the title deed (one email), and the City's ground-level figure for this erf (one question, and the reason the enquiry matters).

The rule that actually decides it

Three definitions from the Municipal Planning By-law do all the work here, and they are worth quoting because they settle the "does a basement count as a level" question in one line each:

TermWhat the by-law saysSo what
"existing ground level"The level of the land surface as depicted on the City of Cape Town Ground Level Map. If that map does not apply, then the natural unmodified state, or the contours on a plan accepted by the City; and the City may require a registered surveyor to measure the levels.The datum is not yours to choose. It is the City's number.
"height"A vertical dimension from a specified level to another specified level, as set out in the zoning rules — with chimneys, flues, lift shafts, masts and antennas excluded.9.0 m and 11.0 m are measured, not counted in storeys.
"floor space"The sum of the floor space of all the levels, including that of basements.The lower level is already counted — and you still sit far under 1,500 m².

There is no definition of "storey" and no limit on the number of storeys anywhere in the Development Management Scheme. So the fact that the house already has two levels costs you nothing. What a new level has to satisfy is the metre envelope, and the only live question is which ground level the City measures it from.

The floor-space ceiling is not the constraint either: at roughly 580 m² today, a scheme adding an 800 m² upper level would still leave 700 m² of the 1,500 m² allowance unused. Coverage does not apply at this erf size.

Gate one — the title deed

Old Cape townships often carry conditions written into the deed at establishment: one dwelling only, no trade, sometimes a height or "single storey" limit. Where one exists, the City can relax it — but that is a separate application with its own clock. Worth knowing now.

The boundary traps, and one the slope creates

The 4 m cap, and its 12 m exception

Where a building is allowed within 3 m of a common boundary, its height is capped at 4 m to the top of the roof — regardless of erf size. The exception: within the first 12 m along that boundary, measured back from the street line, a building not set back from the boundary keeps the full height.

The 1984 drawings show the plan dimensions, so these distances can be scaled off the drawing and then confirmed by survey.

The 3 to 6 m band

The common boundary building line here is 6 m. A wall 4 m from the side line escapes the 4 m roof cap and is still an encroachment of 2 m. Building up over an existing encroachment increases the non-compliance — which is the departure trigger.

The slope creates a third trap: the 4 m cap is measured off ground that is not level

On an 8 m cross-fall, "4 m to the roof" beside a boundary can mean two different things depending on which side you measure from — and a retaining wall is not a building. This is the same datum question as the main height rule, and it is why a surveyor's levels belong in the first week, not the third.

The other way to add space — a second dwelling

Still worth testing, and the slope helps here rather than hurting. Under the by-law a second dwelling — even a third — is an additional use right in SR1, so it needs no land-use application:

  • Size: its floor space may not exceed the main dwelling's floor space without the City's approval — a ~580 m² ceiling.
  • Height if free-standing: 6 m to the wallplate and 8 m to the roof, lower than the main house may go.
  • Look: the City may require it to match the main house.
  • The real gate: certification by the municipal service departments that sewer and water capacity exists. That, not the zoning, is what kills second dwellings.

On this erf the lower garden level at 48.99 is already cut into the slope, which is exactly where a cottage would naturally sit — below the road, private, and with the services already on site. Item 25A(d) also expressly contemplates a unit above the ground floor of the main house, so stacking is written into the scheme rather than being a workaround.

The build path, in the right order

  1. Send the deed request. One email to the conveyancer; already drafted.
  2. Ask the City two questions, not five. (a) What ground level does the City use for Erf 465 under the Ground Level Map definition, given an 8 m fall across the erf? (b) Are there any approved building plans after 1984? The wording is below — copy it and send.
  3. Appoint the architect first. A SACAP-registered architect runs the feasibility: the setback scheme, the measured levels, and the siting of a possible second dwelling. An engineer briefed before a scheme is money spent on a building that may not be allowed.
  4. Then the structural engineer. This house already carries one level over a basement cut into a slope, built in 1984. A third level lands on walls and footings that were engineered for two. This is where the budget can double.
  5. Then design and submission through e-Services / DAMS — no paper — with the company resolution attached.
  6. Approval → build → occupation certificate before the new space is used, with inspections at trench, sewer and completion.

Building control is a second, separate gate

  • Fire (SANS 10400-T). A further level needs a protected escape route and a fire-rated floor below it — which usually decides where the stair goes.
  • Structure. Footings designed in 1984 for a two-level house on a slope, asked to carry three.
  • Services. Extra bathrooms and bedrooms load sewer, water and stormwater.
  • Daylight and ventilation. The existing lower level sits in a slope; anything habitable added at that depth needs the openings to prove it.

"As of right" in the Development Management Scheme does not mean as of right at the building control counter. Both gates have to open.

Timing, and the tenant

The house is let until 30 June 2027. Adding a level is a vacant-possession job — scaffolding, a stripped roof, water off, dust. A fixed-term lease does not simply yield to a notice period; the tenant's agreement or a lawful cancellation route is what gets the house back. A cottage on the lower garden level avoids the problem entirely.

What it costs, and what it returns

The November 2026 research note models R3.0m for a double storey: about 125–156 m² at R16–20k/m² plus 20% for fees and contingency, with extensions assumed to recover roughly 60% of cost on resale. At a R38,000/month Welgemoed rental, a R3m bond at prime does not clear its own debt.

The 60% is an assumption carried from that note, not evidence from this property. Holding costs, lost rent and vacancy during the build sit outside the model, and a level over an existing basement on a slope will price above a plain extension.

Permission is not the constraint — return is. This is a keep-and-live-in decision, not a build-to-sell one, and it should be tested against the second-dwelling option rather than against stacking alone. Current market view from the December 2025 comparative analysis: R6.85m – R8.89m, against R5.5m paid in January 2024.
