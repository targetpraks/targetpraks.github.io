Can we build up a level at 3 Batavia Street, Welgemoed?

Erf 465, Bellville (Welgemoed), 7530 · owner R M Nova Prop (Pty) Ltd · zoning and overlays read live on 16 November 2026 · revised 17 November 2026 after an independent review

The answer

Yes — the zoning allows it. Three things have to be checked before you spend a rand.

Erf 465 is Single Residential 1 (Conventional Housing). On a 2,050 m² erf the City's rules allow a wallplate up to 9 m and a roof up to 11 m, with 1,500 m² of floor space and no coverage limit. The house uses about 580 m² of that. A second storey would land around 6–6.5 m to the wallplate — roughly 2.5 m inside the limit.

So this is not a land-use question. No planning application or departure is needed if the design stays inside the envelope — only an approved building plan.

But the headroom is only half the answer, and revision 1 stopped there. Before anyone designs: (1) read the title deed — a "single dwelling" or "single storey" condition has to be relaxed first; (2) get the approved plans from the City — nobody has yet confirmed how many storeys the house actually has, and the estate agent's automated data says two; (3) measure the walls — anything within 6 m of a side boundary limits what can rise above it.

All three close with documents, not opinions. Two of them are one email each and both are already drafted in your mailbox.

What changed in this revision

Revision 1 answered "yes, as of right" and put the deed in the small print. Five independent models were then asked to attack it. Four of the five found the same three faults and one found a legal error. That is the review working, so here it is in the open:

  • The headline overclaimed. It said yes while the only document that can say no was unread. Now the "yes" is a yes-if, with the three gates named in the same breath.
  • The height maths assumed a single-storey house. The comparative market analysis says "estimated number of floors: 2". If that is right, the question is a third storey, not a second, and the roof limit gets tight. See the two-column table below.
  • The heritage rule was cited wrong. It is a Heritage Western Cape permit under s.34 for altering a structure older than 60 years — not a s.38 comment. Fixed.
  • The 4 m boundary rule was stated too bluntly. There is a 12 m carve-out at the street end, and the trap is wider than 3 m: a wall between 3 m and the 6 m building line still needs a departure to rise. Fixed.
  • The obvious alternative was missing. SR1 lets you build a second dwelling — even a third — as an additional use right. On a 2,050 m² erf that may beat stacking a storey. Added.

Gate one — the title deed

Old Cape townships often carry conditions written into the deed at township establishment: one dwelling only, no trade, sometimes a height or "single storey" limit. Where one exists, the City can relax it, but that is a separate application with its own clock — so it is worth knowing now, not at plan-submission stage.

Three ways to get it, cheapest first:

  1. Your transferring attorney. Bill Tolken Hendrikse Inc held the March 2024 transfer (Belinda, belinda@billtolken.co.za). They have the deed and the surveyor-general's diagram on file — a same-day email.
  2. The bond holder. If the property is bonded, the bank holds a copy of the deed.
  3. The Deeds Office. A deeds search at Cape Town (or any attorney/conveyancer with a deeds account) pulls T15018/2024 for a small fee.

The building plan application needs the deed anyway — it is item one on the City's own document list — so this is not extra work, it is work moved forward.

The two boundary traps, stated properly

Trap one · the 4 m cap, and its 12 m exception

Where a building is allowed within 3 m of a common boundary, its height is capped at 4 m to the top of the roof — regardless of erf size (MPBL s.32(6)(c)(ii)).

The exception: within the first 12 m along that boundary, measured back from the street line, a building that is not set back from the boundary keeps the full table height (s.32(6)(c)(iii)). So the cap bites from 12 m back, not at the front.

Revision 1 stated the cap with no exception. It over-restricted the front of the site.

Trap two · the 3 m to 6 m band

The common boundary building line here is 6 m. A wall 4 m from the side line escapes the 4 m roof cap — and is still an encroachment of 2 m.

Building up over an existing encroachment increases the degree of non-compliance, which is the trigger for a departure application. Both the cap and the building line have to be measured, not assumed.

The approved plans give the distances; a measured survey confirms what was actually built.

The other way to add space — a second dwelling

Nobody in revision 1 asked whether stacking is even the right shape of answer. It may not be. Under the by-law, a second dwelling — and even a third — is an additional use right in SR1, so it needs no land-use application (MPBL items 25A and 25B, inserted 2019):

  • Size: its floor space may not exceed the main dwelling's floor space without the City's approval. Here that is a generous ~580 m² ceiling.
  • Height if free-standing: 6 m to the wallplate and 8 m to the roof — lower than the main house is allowed.
  • Look: the City may require it to match the main house, and where it sits inside the same building it must still read as one dwelling.
  • The real gate: construction is subject to certification by the municipal service departments that sewer and water capacity exists for the extra dwelling. That certification, not the zoning, is what kills second dwellings.

Worth noting for the original question: the by-law expressly contemplates a second dwelling above the ground floor of the main house (item 25A(d)). Going up is not a workaround in Cape Town — it is written into the scheme as a normal arrangement. But a free-standing cottage at the back of a 2,050 m² erf might rent better, cost less per square metre, and leave the house occupied throughout. That comparison belongs in the feasibility study, not after the drawings.

The build path, in the right order

  1. Send the two emails. Deed request to the conveyancer; planning enquiry to the Northern district office. Both drafted, neither sent.
  2. Pull the previously approved plans from the City. They settle three things at once: the real storey count, the build date, and where the walls sit relative to the boundaries (submit a building plan).
  3. Appoint the architect first, not the engineer. A SACAP-registered architect runs the feasibility: options on the table (stack, extend sideways, second dwelling), the measured survey, and the heritage screen. An engineer briefed before there is a scheme is money spent on a building that may not be allowed.
  4. Then the structural engineer. Footings and walls built for one level rarely carry two. New load paths, possibly columns to foundation — this is the real cost driver, not the brickwork, and it needs to see the actual house.
  5. Then design and submission. Plans go in online through e-Services / DAMS — the City accepts no paper. Because the owner is a company, attach the company resolution.
  6. Approval → build → occupation certificate. The City inspects at trench, at sewer and at completion; the new space may not be occupied until the certificate is issued.

Small print worth confirming at submission: the municipal account must be current, and a departures or restriction-relaxation branch (if triggered at step 1, 2 or 4) runs on its own clock — months, not weeks.

Building control is a second, separate gate

Everything above is planning and zoning. The building plan itself is judged under the National Building Regulations — and a second storey brings obligations a ground-floor house never had to meet:

  • Fire (SANS 10400-T). An upper floor needs a protected escape route, and the ceiling over the lower floor generally needs a fire rating. That usually dictates where the staircase and landing go — a design driver, not a detail discovered at plan check.
  • Structural adequacy. Footings and walls built for one level rarely carry two. The engineer's assessment of the existing structure, not the new work, is where the budget can double.
  • Services. Extra bathrooms and bedrooms load the sewer, water and stormwater connection. Where capacity is short, the City can require an upgrade on the owner's account.
  • Age of the structure. If it is more than 60 years old, no building plan is approved without the Heritage Western Cape permit — and a negative heritage finding stops the plan regardless of what the zoning says.

"As of right" in the Development Management Scheme does not mean as of right at the building control counter. Both gates have to be open, and only the first one is settled here.

Timing, and the tenant

The house is let until 30 June 2027. A second storey is a vacant-possession job — scaffolding, a stripped roof, water off, dust. That means either waiting for the lease to end, or negotiating an early exit. A fixed-term lease does not simply yield to a notice period; the tenant's agreement or a lawful cancellation route is what gets the house back.

A second dwelling at the back of the erf avoids this entirely. That is the strongest practical argument for testing it first.

What it costs, and what it returns

The money side is modelled in the November 2026 research note Renovation vs Rhodes vs currency: R3.0m buys about 125–156 m² at R16–20k/m² plus 20% for fees and contingency, and extensions are assumed to recover roughly 60% of cost on resale. At a R38,000/month Welgemoed rental, a R3m bond at prime does not clear its own debt.

Two honesty notes the review forced: the 60% recovery is an assumption carried from that note, not evidence from this property, and the ~800 m² in the table above is illustrative — spending R3m buys a good deal less than 220 m² at these rates, so the floor-space ceiling never becomes the binding constraint. Holding costs, lost rent during the build and vacancy are also outside the model.

So the permission is not the constraint — the return is. This is a live-in-and-keep decision, not a build-to-sell one. The zoning work removes the "can we" question; the "should we" question stays open on its own merits, and it should be answered against the second-dwelling option rather than against stacking alone.

Current market view from the December 2025 Brickfield comparative analysis: R6.85m – R8.89m, against R5.5m paid in January 2024.
