What this PRD is. The build specification for the pack: what each page must contain, what governs it, what it may not assert, and how it is produced and checked.

What this PRD is not. It is not the pack, not a cost plan, and it approves no spend.

0.1 THE DOCUMENT FAMILY — TWO DOCUMENTS, NOT ONE

Ricardo's ruling (2026-10-20): "You would have to create two documents. The main one is our standard brand-manual colours and reflects all of it, linking to the story of the annexure. The annexure is very specific — a short PDF of the exact stuff that is needed for their colour works. Much smaller, because all the technical stuff is in the main document. Mainly a quick visual representation of that store's colour."

That is the architecture, and it is the right one — it separates the thing that must never drift from the thing that must always vary.

Main Pack Colourway Annexure
Code PP-SDP-001 PP-CWA-001, -002, -003 … (one per store)
Role The standard. Every technical answer, every material, every service, every brand constant — and the system by which colour works. The instance. This store's colour, in the colours it will actually be built in.
Size 53 pp 8 pp
Changes per store? No. One master, revised centrally, version-controlled. Yes. Generated per territory from that store's colour record.
Audience Fitter, franchisee, supplier, auditor The store's fitter, the store's franchisee, the painter, the LED installer, the sign shop
Owns The permitted colourway menu, the RGB engine's rules, the two-mechanism model The chosen colourway's values, placements, recipe and procurement
Never contains A store-specific colour presented as the brand Any technical rule — it points to the main pack instead

The linking rule — this is what makes two documents behave as one. The main pack never presents Store 1's blue as the colour; it presents it as a colourway, resolved and built, and every colour-specific line in it ends with a pointer: "as set in Annexure A". The annexure then never repeats a rule — it names the page it came from. Consequences:

  • A fitter reading only the annexure still knows the build is governed — because every line carries a page reference back.
  • A franchisee reading only the main pack learns the system, then receives their own eight pages.
  • Nothing can disagree, because both documents are printed from the same colour record (§8.4). If the annexure and the pack ever differ, that is a build defect, not a judgement call.

One naming fix forced by this ruling. The word "zone" now means two different things and would collide on the page: lighting zones Z01–Z08 (Ryan's calculated areas) and trading territories (the areas we sell a franchise into). The pack fixes the vocabulary: Z01–Z08 are lighting zones; a commercial area is a Territory with a code like CT-SOUTH-01. No page may use "zone" for a territory. This is a one-line ruling that prevents a genuinely expensive confusion — a lighting zone cannot be sold, and a territory cannot be lit.

1. WHAT WE ARE BUILDING, AND THE TEST IT MUST PASS

1.1 The brief, as given

Combine the Brand Manual and the packaging designs with Ryan Jones-Hockley's lighting design (governing on how lighting should be) and Julie Chrisopoulos's 3D renders and technical drawings (governing on everything else). Produce a full Papa Pasta store design: all external and internal specification, all technical and visual design elements, material listing with explanations, material alternatives in some sections. Visually impressive. Handover grade for a shop fitter and a prospective franchisee. Final PDF.

1.2 The three things the pack must teach, above all

A pack can carry a thousand correct details and fail if it does not settle three ideas. Everything else is in service of these:

  1. The store is a repeatable kit, not one shop. (Ruling 3)
  2. The colour is the store's, and it is changeable. Every store may choose its own colour, and the reason the design uses RGB is so that choice can be changed in an afternoon rather than a repaint and a refit. (Ruling 2, and the new §05)
  3. It ships as two documents: the standard, and your eight pages. The pack teaches the system; the annexure states this store's colour. (Ruling 5)
  4. The light is Ryan's, and 6000 K is why the colour system works. A neutral, high-output, 6000 K base is the only base on which a chosen colour reads truly. This is not a compromise between authorities — it is the technical reason the colourway engine functions at all. (Ruling 1)

1.3 The test

A pack is handover grade when a fitter can price it, order it, set it out, build it, connect it, prove it and hand it over without asking anyone a question — and a franchisee can decide to buy it, choose their colour, know what is theirs, and know what will fail an audit without a second meeting.

Open the pack at any line — a lux value, a panel dimension, a fixture reference, a colour. Can a fitter build from that line without reconciling two sources? If any line requires him to choose, the pack is a synthesis document wearing a specification's clothes.

1.4 What "visually impressive" means here, precisely

It does not mean a deck of renders. It means the pack is itself built to the brand's own law: a black-substrate document with lime as the single accent of the document, set in Montserrat and IBM Plex Mono, obeying 70/18/12 on every spread. A white corporate document about a black brand is the first thing that makes it read as a brochure.

And one deliberate design joke that proves the point: the pack is printed in black because black is the constant; the store it describes is blue because blue is a choice. The document says so on the colourway page, and the reader understands the whole system in one glance.

2.1b RULING ON LIGHTING (Ricardo, 2026-10-20)

Ryan Jones-Hockley's lighting specification governs. Where the Brand Manual and Ryan disagree on lighting, Ryan's specification is built — including 6000 K throughout, not the manual's 4000 / 3000 / 2700 K zone law.

Consequences, applied everywhere: - 6000 K is the built colour temperature in every area Ryan calculated. No zone he specified is re-tuned. The three he did not calculate — threshold, eat zone, exterior — are flagged where they appear, with the manual's written instruction shown alongside. - His four fixture families are specified as issued (CCT 6k), with no warmer variant offered. - The manual's Part 7 zone CCT schedule is superseded by ruling and recorded as a variance, not deleted — so the deviation is documented rather than discovered at audit. - 6000 K is not a compromise, it is the colourway's foundation. This is stated on the colourway page, because it is the argument that makes Ryan's spec obviously right rather than merely mandated. - Everything in Part 7 that is not colour temperature or light level — one-neon-per-surface logic, the accent allocation, maintenance access, the sign-failure audit rule — is not lighting and remains brand law.

2.1c RULING ON COLOUR (Ricardo, 2026-10-20)

"Julie's design is for a store that is themed blue. Each store can choose their colour — hence we chose RGB so we can update at any given time. This is a store pack and it needs to explain that RGB."

This ruling rewrites how the renders are read. It is the most important correction in this document:

  • Julie's blue is not a brand violation. It is Store 1's colourway. Every blue surface in her renders is that store exercising a right the network grants.
  • RGB exists to make the colour changeable. It is not decorative lighting that happens to be colourful — it is the mechanism by which a store changes identity without a repaint, a refit or a closure.
  • Therefore the pack's job is not to police the colour. Its job is to explain the colourway system, specify the RGB hardware and control that delivers it, and define exactly what a store may and may not choose.
  • The renders stay in the pack, and they are promoted: they are Store 1's Blue, fully resolved and built, not a set of images to be apologised for.

2.2 Four corrections — what the council and Ricardo found

2.1d RULING ON THE COLOURWAY MENU AND THE FAÇADE (Ricardo, 2026-10-20)

"The colourway menu is basically going to be our standard brand-manual design, because the annexure touches the specific store's colour. The façade obviously will change, the logo will change. The black can remain — actually, no, the black can't remain. The black and the lime of the façade will always change depending on the colour scheme they choose."

Three consequences, and they change the shape of §05:

  1. The menu is not invented. It is the Brand Manual's own palette. No new colours are commissioned. The permitted colourways are the colours Papa Pasta already owns — the manual's accents plus the documented logo variations — and the annexure then says which one this store is. This is stronger than v1.1: a franchisee choosing "a colour" is choosing from the brand's own wardrobe, so the brand cannot fragment by accident.
  2. The façade is part of the colourway, in full. Black does not stay as a fixed ground on the street, and lime does not stay as the fixed accent — both travel with the store's chosen scheme. The street sign is therefore the most visible expression of the colourway, and §08 must specify it per store in the annexure rather than fixing it in the pack.
  3. The crest anatomy is still untouchable; the colour relationship around it is not. The Living Crest's shield, anatomy and frozen EST 2025 never change — recolouring the anatomy or redrawing the shield is still an automatic rejection. What changes is the lockup: the substrate behind the crest and the accent that sits against it follow the store's colourway. That is the only reading that satisfies both this ruling and the brand's own "no recolour-only crest changes" law, and the pack states it explicitly so nobody on site has to interpret it.

The question this opens, and it is a real one — D10 in §10. If black can change on the façade, can it change inside? Store 1's interior is not black either — Julie's walls are white, her panels are blue, and only her floor is black. So the honest position is that the brand's "70% black" has already been interpreted per store on this project, and the pack must either generalise the rule or admit the rule is not being followed. §05b proposes the generalisation; D10 asks for the yes.

3. THE FOUR RULINGS THE PACK MAKES

Ruling 1 — Lighting is Ryan's, in full. 6000 K is built, and it is what makes colour possible.

  • Ryan governs levels, uniformity, fixture family, quantities, colour temperature, wall-washing and the calculated result. His report is the issued design, reproduced as evidence.
  • 6000 K throughout, as ruled. The manual's zone CCT law is superseded and recorded as a variance.
  • The technical argument, stated once and used everywhere: a neutral 6000 K base is what allows a chosen store colour to read as that colour. Warm 2700–3000 K light would tint every blue, lime and purple. The colourway system does not work on warm light — so Ryan's specification is not merely senior, it is correct.
  • Two items return to Ryan rather than being resolved by anyone else: the BOH minimum and the absent eat zone.
  • Where Ryan is silent the manual speaks — controls, maintenance access, threshold, emergency, exterior — and every such page says "Ryan silent — manual governs."

Ruling 2 — Colour is a store-level variable delivered by RGB; Store 1 is Blue and that is legitimate.

This is the ruling the whole pack now hangs from.

The system has three parts, and the pack must teach all three:

Layer What it is Who owns it Changeable?
Foundation The 70/18/12 proportion, typography, crest anatomy and EST 2025, the audit rules, the product law Brand. Fixed. No
Colourway The store's chosen triad (dark base / light ground / accent), carried on surfaces, light and the façade The store, within the manual's own palette Yes — that is the point
Signal Product truth, promos, price, daypart Operations Weekly

Two delivery mechanisms, and the pack must separate them because they cost and behave completely differently:

Mechanism How it works Change cost Change time
Lit colourway RGB fixtures wash white/neutral surfaces; the surface has no colour of its own Control re-programme only An afternoon
Painted colourway Panels and tiles are finished in the chosen colour Repaint / re-panel that scope Days, with a closure

Store 1 uses both. Julie's scheme has blue painted panels and blue lit strips. The pack states, surface by surface, which mechanism each one uses — because a franchisee who buys an all-painted store and then wants to change colour has bought a refit, not a switch. This distinction is worth more to a buyer than any render in the document.

What a store may choose: the accent colour from the permitted colourway set (§05), the scene programme within the daypart rules, and the product-facing graphic layer. What a store may not choose: black substrate, the 70/18/12 proportion, the type system, the crest wording or the frozen EST 2025, the base 6000 K, the brand's lime signature position, or any colourway that breaks contrast against the brand marks.

Ruling 3 — The pack is a prototype kit-of-parts, not one shop.

8040 × 5600 mm plus BOH is one shell. Present it as "the Papa Pasta store" and every future site is a redesign with no audit baseline. So every element is tri-partitioned:

Tier Meaning Examples

Adaptable Dimensioned modules with min/max, plus the colourway set. The franchise asset. 2420 / 1375 / 1450 counter modules · bay widths · bulkhead runs · zone adjacencies · the colourway menu
Site-specific This shell, this landlord, this colour. Re-drawn per store. Store 1's setting-out · shopfront line · extract route · landlord signage · Store 1 Blue

Ruling 4 — The pack states its own issue status, and names a deputy for the absent architect.

Julie's sheets are stamped INFORMATION / CONSTRUCTION TENDER — two contradictory purposes in one box — and are drawn and checked by the same hand (JC/JC). She is unreachable for three months, which is the entire procurement window.

So the pack: reproduces the status block verbatim; contains zero instances of "confirm with the architect" — every deferred item becomes a decision with a named owner and a default ruling; names a deputy design authority with power to approve samples, equivalents and deviations, on a 5-working-day turnaround with deemed approval on expiry; and carries a change-control rule, because nobody at King Protea can issue Rev B.

And by Ricardo's delegation ("it's fine for you to choose the std design detailing"): the standard details are not deferred. The pack's author specifies them — counter build-ups, RGB channel integration, driver access, tile setting-out, junctions — and they enter the pack as the network standard, subject to sample approval. This closes the largest gap in v0.9 rather than passing it to the fitter.

Ruling 5 — Two documents: a standard that never drifts, and an annexure that always varies.

The main pack is the standard. It holds every technical answer, the colourway menu, the RGB engine's rules and the audit translation, and it is revised centrally — never per store. The Colourway Annexure (PP-CWA-0XX, 8 pp) holds one store's colour and nothing else: the plate, the surface map, the RGB recipe, the procurement card, the signage variant and the sign-off.

Why split them. A single per-store document means every store carries a copy of the technical standard, and every one of those copies is a version that can fall out of date. Fifty stores, fifty copies of the wiring spec, and no way to know which copy the fitter built from. Splitting them means there is one standard with one revision number, and fifty short annexures that contain no rules to get wrong.

What the split costs, and how the pack prevents it. Two documents can disagree. So both are printed from the same colour record (§8.4), the annexure may not introduce a colour outside the menu, and a mismatch between the two is defined as a build defect rather than a design decision. The cross-reference is explicit in both directions: the pack says "as set in Annexure A"; the annexure cites the pack page for every rule it touches.

The commercial benefit, stated plainly: the annexure is what a franchisee actually receives on signing. Eight pages, their colour, their store, with the standard behind it. It is also the reason the pack can be sold to fifty territories without fifty redesigns.

05 · Colourway system & the RGB engine (5 pp) — the section that makes this a store pack

This is the new spine. Five pages, and every one of them is a decision the franchisee has to be able to make.

05a — The colourway idea, in one page (T1). A Papa Pasta store is black at its foundation and coloured in its character. Black, white and the proportion never change; the colour is the store's, and it can change without closing the doors. Store 1 is Blue. Store 2 may be Lime, Purple or Red. The page shows the same counter rendered or drawn in three colourways, side by side, with the identical structure — proving in one image that the colour is a layer, not the building. This single page is what sells the pack to a franchisee, because it answers the question every franchisee asks: how much of my money is the brand's taste versus my market's?

05b — The permitted colourway set: the Brand Manual's own wardrobe (T3). By ruling, the menu is not invented — it is the palette Papa Pasta already owns. Each colourway is specified to the same depth as the brand palette: brand name, Pantone, RAL, hex, RGB/HSV values for the LED channel, the white surface it is washed over, its measured contrast against white and against its own dark, and a three-letter code the fitter and the controller both use.

Each colourway is a triad, not a single swatch. This is the answer to the interior question the façade ruling opened (D10): a store's colour is not one value sprayed over everything — it is a dark base, a mid accent, and a light ground, and the brand's 70 / 18 / 12 proportion is then applied to the triad rather than to black-and-white specifically.

Role Share Brand default Store 1 Blue Function
Dominant / dark base 70% Black #000000 Midnight Blue (navy, #061B4C family) The ground everything sits on. Floors, carcass, substrate, shadow.
Light ground 18% White #FFFFFF RAL 9016 Cool white, never tinted The surfaces that carry the light and take the wash.
Accent 12% Lime #C9F034 / 389 C Blue #1B4FD8 family The one loud thing per surface. Neon, edge line, logo field.

Consequences of the triad model, and why it is worth the page: - The proportion survives even when the colours change. A store that swaps black for navy has not broken 70/18/12 — it has filled the same proportion with its own base. That is the only way a colour-choosing network can hold a proportion rule at all. - The dark base must always be dark. A colourway whose "dominant" is lime or red is refused: the base has a maximum luminance so the accents can still read. This is the guard that stops the system becoming a paint sale. - Black and lime are always permitted. A store may choose them, or default to them. The brand's own scheme is the first entry in the menu, not a separate rule. - The façade uses the same triad (§08): dark base on the surround, accent on the sign field, light ground where the crest sits — so the street and the interior are one decision, not two.

The menu as evidenced: the manual's accents — Lime #C9F034 / Pantone 389 C, Purple #A766E6, Red #FF3255, Silver #C0C0C0 / Cool Gray 4 C — plus Midnight Blue / navy from the documented logo variations (#061B4C linework family, Papa_Pasta_Logo_Variations_50.pdf p.4), plus Black as the brand default. Blue is therefore not an addition to the brand — it is already in the brand's own logo variation set, which is precisely why Store 1's scheme is legitimate rather than a departure. Every entry in the menu must be traceable to a page of the manual or the logo set. The pack may not introduce a colour the brand does not already own.

05c — The two delivery mechanisms, surface by surface (T5 + T3). The diagram that stops a franchisee buying a repaint by mistake: a single wall drawn twice — once as lit colourway (neutral surface + RGB source) and once as painted colourway (finished surface) — with the cost and time of change printed beneath each. Then a schedule listing every coloured surface in Store 1 and which mechanism it uses. Julie's blue is both, and the schedule says which is which. The rule stated plainly: if a store wants the ability to change colour cheaply and often, it buys the lit mechanism on that surface. If it wants the colour permanent, it paints it. Most stores will want both, in different places, and the pack shows them where.

05d — The RGB engine: hardware and control (T3 + T4). - Channels and loads. Every RGB run in Store 1 is scheduled: location, length, LED density, voltage (24 V), wattage per metre, driver, and the load per controller. Sources: Julie's drawings specify RGB on the ceiling strips, wall-wash, floor text, eating counter, display unit, logo boxes, bulkhead, dispensers and BOH. - The white-base rule. Ryan's 6000 K layer is the base illumination and must be able to run alone at the calculated lux. RGB is additive and never substitutes for it. Where both occupy one linear run, the 6000 K channel and the RGB channel are separate — co-extruded or parallel — because a single fitting cannot be both the design's light source and its colour engine without compromising the photometrics Ryan calculated. - Control — one network standard, ruled. Ricardo: "we need to fix the controller, or it needs to be a standard controller through the entire franchise network. The most capable controller so that we can control it remotely through all the different channels and all the different zones." The pack therefore specifies, and does not leave open: - Protocol: DALI-2 (IEC 62386) with DT8 colour control. Open international standard, so no franchise is locked to one supplier for twenty years, and DT8 carries full colour and tunable-white so the same protocol drives the 6000 K base and the colourway layer. One protocol, two jobs. - A networked application controller per store, addressable from head office: zone control mapped to Z01–Z08, scene library per daypart, and remote colour and scene updates pushed over the network without sending anyone to site. - A DMX512-A / Art-Net gateway wherever a surface needs pixel mapping — media façade, animated signage, the floor-text run. Specified as an interface off the same controller, never as a second system with its own brain. - Capacity with headroom, stated as a requirement rather than discovered later: maximum control gear per line, minimum universes/zones, scene count, and spare capacity for the next fit-out change. - Local override at the counter, and falls back to a safe scene if the network drops. A colour system staff cannot control is a system they will defeat with a plug; a system that goes dark when the internet blinks is a store with no lights at 19:00 on a Friday. - One address book, one commissioning record per store, held centrally — which is what makes the fifty-store promise real rather than aspirational. - Drivers. Every driver gets a location, an access panel and a service loop. An LED sealed inside a thermoformed counter with no access is a demolition item at year two, and a direct breach of the manual's own maintenance rule. - Commissioning. Colour calibrated on site against the colourway swatch, recorded, and signed — so "the blue looks wrong" becomes a measurement, not an argument.

05e — What a store may and may not choose (T6). The rights page: the colourway menu, the scene programme, what stays fixed, what happens when a landlord demands a change, and the colour-change procedure — who authorises, how long it takes, what it costs, whether the store must close (lit: no; painted: yes). Also the two hard limits: no colourway that breaks contrast against the brand mark, and no colour treatment that turns the store into the thing the manual calls a nightclub — colour is carried by light and accent surface, not by washing every plane in it.

05f — How this becomes your annexure (T6). The page that hands the reader from the standard to their own document. It shows the annexure's eight pages in thumbnail, states what is in them and what is deliberately not, and gives the one instruction that matters: this pack is the standard; your annexure is your store; read them together, and if they ever disagree, stop and call. It also states the rule that protects the network — an annexure may not invent a colour outside the menu — and the commercial reality behind it: the standard is what makes the eighth page cheap, because by the time a territory signs, everything except the colour has already been answered.

06 · Finishes & materials schedule (6 pp) — "the material listing with explanations"

Per element (T3 schedule + T4 cards): product, manufacturer, reference, colour code, finish, thickness, substrate, fixing method, sample approval status, approved-equivalent clause, lead time, maintenance, and why — the reason is what stops a fitter swapping it. And a colourway-critical column, new in v1.0: for every material, is this surface neutral or coloured, and does it take a wash? Because under the lit mechanism the material must be neutral, matte and non-yellowing — a gloss or an off-white surface will not render the chosen colour truly, and no amount of LED tuning fixes a warm substrate. That single column is what makes the colourway buildable.

A mismatch between a store's annexure and the main pack's menu is therefore impossible by construction, and if it ever appears it is a defect in the record, not a design decision to be argued.

D7 · RGB control standard. The pack will set one (DMX512 or DALI-2) as a network standard. - A (recommended): let the pack set it and treat it as fixed — a network with four controllers has no colour system. - B: leave it to each store's electrician (and lose the promise by store two).

D9 · The annexure's issue trigger and signatory. An annexure is the document a franchisee actually receives on signing, so who releases it matters. - A (recommended): the pack's design authority issues it, the CMO countersigns the colour, and the franchisee signs the approval record on page 7. Three signatures, one page, no ambiguity about who approved the blue. - B: issued automatically from the colour record once the franchise agreement is signed, with CMO approval of the colour only. - C: you sign every annexure personally.

D10 · Does the interior get the same freedom as the façade? — the one thing I need you to rule on. Your façade ruling means black no longer has to stay on the street. But Store 1's interior isn't black either — Julie's walls are white, her panels are blue, only her floor is black. So "70% black" is already being interpreted per store on this project.

I don't want to invent a rule you haven't agreed to, so §05b proposes generalising it:

Every colourway is a triad — dark base 70%, light ground 18%, accent 12% — and the proportion is fixed while the colours inside it are the store's.

  • A (recommended): yes, use the triad. A store can swap black for navy and still be 70/18/12, so the proportion rule survives colour choice instead of being quietly broken by it. Store 1 is then compliant rather than an exception.
  • B: black stays mandatory inside the store; only the façade and accents vary. Simplest to police, but then Store 1's own design is non-compliant and the pack has to say so on the page.
  • C: you name the interior rule.

On D8, in plain words, since I used a term you shouldn't have had to decode. "White box" just means a scheme drawn on white walls instead of black ones. Those three rejected colour concepts were drawn that way — so roughly 80% of each image is white, which is the opposite of the brand's 70% black. They'd need re-drawing on a dark base before they could be built. Confirmed: they go to the appendix as rejected in current form, with a note that they can be re-drawn on black later if you ever want them as real colourways.
