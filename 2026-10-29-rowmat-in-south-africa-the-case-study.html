Rowmat in South Africa: the case study

The short version. The board is a superb wet-area lining and a poor brick replacement. Wall for wall against 230 mm brick and plaster, it costs more, not less. In a bathroom it costs about the same and removes a trade and three to five days. Manufacturing it here does not work yet, because the local XPS core alone is dearer than the finished board landed from China. Low-cost subsidy housing will not take it; the backyard rental market might.

Every rand figure below is per square metre and carries its source in the table or in Evidence. Where a number is my own planning estimate it says so in the line.

Summary

  • Against brick, on a plain wall, it loses. A plastered, painted 230 mm brick wall costs R507–812/m² in 2026. A board partition on studs costs about R730/m² and needs a frame it cannot provide itself. Brick is cheap in South Africa because bricks are R2.20 each and plastering is R80–150/m².
  • In a wet area it wins on time, not price. Board lining lands at R370–415/m² and replaces plaster, membrane and screed, so tiling starts the next day instead of three to five days later. That is the whole commercial case.
  • The product is a substrate, not a structure. Rowmat calls it a structural board; it carries no building. In South Africa the structure is brick, light steel frame, concrete or a cast system. The board is a finish and a waterproofing layer.
  • Manufacturing here fails the arithmetic today. Local XPS core at 30 mm is R154.70/m² at published prices. A finished 30 mm board lands from China at about R138/m², freight included. The raw material costs more than the finished import.
  • An XPS line is $650,000 FOB (about R10.8m) before a coating line, a building, power, working capital or certification. Agrément's application fee alone is R6,581.80.
  • Low-cost housing: no. The subsidy house is a fixed-grant product built to a brick specification, enrolled with the NHBRC, and expected to hold resale value. A foam-core board does not fit that brief.
  • Backyard rental: yes, probably. Around 919,000 households lived in backyard rental units in 2016, about 26% of the rental market. That market buys speed and a room that can be rented out, and it is not waiting for a certificate.
  • Market to enter first: wet areas in renovation and commercial fit-out, sold as a system with the adhesive brand and the training attached.

1. What the product is, and what it is not

Rowmat is a 20–100 mm extruded polystyrene core with glass-fibre mesh and a polymer-modified cement face on both sides. It is rigid, waterproof when the joints are sealed, and takes tile, plaster, microcement and paint directly.

It is not a structural element. It has no racking strength, no fixings for a roof, and no capacity to carry a wall. Every credible use is as a lining or cladding on something else: masonry, a steel stud frame, or a timber frame.

That distinction decides the South African case, because in this market the wall and the finish are usually bought from the same bricklayer in one price. A board has to be compared with the finish layers, not with the whole wall, or the comparison is fixed before it starts.

2. The cost comparison that matters

Unit rates used, all 2026 and all sourced:

  • Bricks: R2.20 each for clay stocks (Build it Klerksoord, R2,199.99 per 1,000, valid to January 2026). Fifty bricks to the square metre for a single skin.
  • Cement: R90–130 per 50 kg bag (IndexBox, South African retail range).
  • Bricklaying labour: R165/m² for a single cement-brick wall; R275–330/m² is the common fixed rate; face brick up to R385/m² (LocalPros, 2026).
  • Plastering: R80–150/m² per face (BuildingContractorPretoria, 2026).
  • Imported board: R235/m² to the trade, landed cost R114–123/m² (see the sourcing note).
  • Local XPS core: R154.70/m² at 30 mm, R206.28 at 40 mm, R250.00 at 50 mm (Thermaboards list, ex VAT).

2.4 A wet area: the one place the maths moves

Approach What it costs What it takes
Brick, plaster, liquid membrane, screed plaster R80–150/m² plus membrane plus screed, all trades 3–5 days of drying before tiling
Board lining, joints sealed, tile direct R370–415/m² including fixings, tape and labour tiling the next day
Difference roughly cost-neutral to 15% more 3–5 days saved per bathroom

A bathroom of 20 m² of wall saves three to five days of a tiler's and a plasterer's time. At R1,500–R2,500 a day for a small crew, that is R5,000–R12,000 a bathroom — more than the board premium. And the leak risk moves from a painted membrane to a board that is waterproof through its core.

3. Why anyone would choose it, and when they should not

Job Choose Why
Shower or bathroom wall Board No membrane, no screed, tiling next day, waterproof core
Commercial wet area, gym, lodge, student block Board Programme matters more than unit rate; repeatable detail
Kitchen counter or sink unit former Board Curved and shaped, tile or stone directly onto it
Dry internal partition Brick R467–717 against R730, and brick is culturally expected
External house wall Neither alone The wall needs a structure; brick or a frame carries the load
Subsidy house Brick Fixed grant, brick specification, NHBRC enrolment
Backyard rental room Board on frame Fast, light, no wet trade, can be let within weeks

The honest marketing line for South Africa is not "cheaper than brick". It is "the wet area that does not leak, finished three days sooner, in half the thickness".

4. Is there a market for low-cost housing?

Three different markets get called low-cost housing, and only one of them fits a board.

Subsidy housing (BNG and RDP). One house, 40 m², funded from a fixed per-unit grant that the department sets annually — widely reported in the R250,000–R261,000 range for 2025/26, and worth confirming against the current circular before quoting. The state's house is brick, enrolled with the NHBRC (R745.61 registration, R526.32 a year), built by contractors on a schedule of rates, and it is an asset the owner expects to extend and resell. A foam-core board fails on three counts there: fire class E, the resale and security expectation, and the fact that no provincial schedule of rates carries it.

Backyard rental. About 919,000 households were living in backyard rental units in 2016, roughly 26% of the whole rental market, and backyard dwellings are described in the research as the dominant and fastest-growing type of informal rental housing. A landlord adding a 12 m² room wants it built in two weeks for cash, with no wet trades and no curing. This is the closest fit for the product in South Africa, and it is served today by timber frames, zinc, and cheap brick rooms.

Gap and affordable housing for the bonded market. A garden cottage or a second dwelling on an existing erf, built to plan and financed by a bond. Small, but it pays retail prices and needs a municipal approval, which is exactly where Agrément certification and a tested assembly help.

Harder than it looks, and the material economics are the reason, not the machinery.

5.2 The arithmetic that kills it

The local XPS core at 30 mm is R154.70/m² at published prices, and about R103/m² if you scale the rate down to 20 mm. A finished 30 mm board lands from China at about R138/m² including freight and clearing, and a finished 20 mm board at R114–123/m².

Read that again: the raw foam, before any cement, mesh, labour, packaging or margin, costs more than the finished imported product. A coating line buying local foam would have to negotiate at least 40% off list before it could compete with a container from Ningbo.

5.3 Where local manufacturing does make sense

  • If you extrude the foam yourself. That is the $650,000 question, and at that point you are an insulation manufacturer who also coats board.
  • Thick boards, 40–50 mm and up. Freight per square metre climbs steeply with thickness (about R35/m² at 30 mm against R23/m² at 20 mm), so the import penalty grows as the board gets thicker.
  • Long lengths. Local XPS comes in lengths up to 8 m (Thermaboards), which is a real edge over a 2.4 m imported board and worth money on tall walls.
  • Service, not price. Small orders, next-week delivery, cut-to-size, and a rep who answers.

Verdict: import first. Do not build a plant until you are selling more than roughly 50,000 m² a year or you own the foam supply.

7. The pilot I would run before any container

  1. One bathroom, one house. Strip to brick, line with 20 mm board, tile. Time it, cost it, and photograph every stage. That single job answers the whole case.
  2. One backyard room. 12 m², steel frame, board both sides, one wet corner. Time it against a brick room of the same size.
  3. Price both against the brick numbers in section 2, using the contractor's own quote, not list prices.
  4. Talk to four players: a bathroom renovator, a commercial fit-out contractor, a backyard landlord, and one quantity surveyor who prices BNG work.
  5. Only then order board, and only 500–1,000 m² to start.

8. Risks

  • Fire class E. The core burns. Commercial and multi-storey work will need a non-combustible alternative, so the product line must have one.
  • Waterproofing is only as good as the joints. The system sells on not leaking; one bad detail and the brand is finished.
  • The brick lobby is not the obstacle — habit is. South African owners, banks and insurers price a brick house higher, and a board wall reads as temporary.
  • Subsidy work is closed without a schedule-of-rates entry and NHBRC acceptance.
  • Currency and freight, as in the sourcing note, plus a 6–8 week lead time that small builders will not plan around.
