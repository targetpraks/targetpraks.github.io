01The product is a substrate, not a structure

A 20–100 mm extruded polystyrene core, glass-fibre mesh, and a polymer-modified cement face on both sides. It takes tile, plaster and paint directly, and it is waterproof when the joints are sealed.

It has no racking strength, carries no roof, and holds up no wall. Every credible use is as a lining on something else — masonry, a steel frame, or timber. That single fact decides the South African case, because here the wall and the plaster usually arrive on one bricklayer's quote. Compare the board with the wall and it loses before you start. Compare it with the finish layers and it has a business.

Board is 2–56% dearer than brick for the same partition, and the brick figure already includes the plaster finish. What the board buys back is 76 mm instead of 140 mm, a tenth of the weight, no curing, and a cavity for services.

Why brick is hard to beat here. A stock brick costs about R2.20 delivered, sand is cheap, and a bricklayer lays a square metre for R165. That combination is why South Africa builds in brick and will keep building in brick. No imported board beats it on a dry wall.

The marketing line for South Africa is not "cheaper than brick". It is "the wet area that does not leak, finished three days sooner, in half the thickness".

04Shopfitters: the best door into the building

A shop fit is where the board's strengths meet the contractor's actual problem — with one hard limit that has to be said out loud.

Retail fit-out is a volume business: a 100–300 m² store runs four to eight weeks from sign-off to handover, and Shoprite opened 268 stores in 11 months in FY2026 against a target of 223, on a R3.9 billion expansion. Every store has a wet area, a counter, an ablution, a kitchen, or all four.

The limit that must be stated first. Drywall partitioning costs R350–500/m² in 2026, and SANS 10400-T requires partition walls to give not less than 30 minutes of fire resistance. A class E foam board cannot carry that. Do not pitch partitions at a shopfitter — pitch the tiled surfaces inside the fit-out.

The size of the wedge, with the arithmetic shown. A full-format supermarket might carry 40–80 m² of tiled wet wall across butchery, bakery, deli, wash-up and staff facilities; the small formats carry almost none. If a third of Shoprite's 268 stores a year are full format, that is roughly 3,600–7,200 m² a year from one retailer, before Pick n Pay, SPAR, Woolworths, Clicks and Dis-Chem. Those are my planning assumptions, not measured figures — the pilot should test them on one store.

How to sell it: not to the retailer, to the fit-out firms and their shopfitting joinery partners. A handful of firms do most of the rollout work. Hand them a sample box, a tiled panel, a one-page method statement that will pass a mall's works approval, and a price that beats plaster plus membrane on time, because time is what the mall penalises.

05Is there a market for low-cost housing?

Three different markets get called low-cost housing. Only one fits a board.

Subsidy housing — no

A 40 m² house funded by a fixed per-unit grant, widely reported at R250,000–R261,000 for 2025/26. Brick, NHBRC-enrolled (R745.61 plus R526.32 a year), built by contractors on a schedule of rates, and expected to hold value and be extendable. A class E foam board fails on fire, on resale, and on the fact that no provincial rate schedule carries it.

Backyard rental — yes

Around 919,000 households lived in backyard rental units in 2016, about 26% of the rental market, and researchers call backyard dwellings the dominant and fastest-growing informal rental type. A landlord adding a 12 m² room wants it done in two weeks, for cash, with no wet trades. This is the closest fit in the country.

Gap housing — a garden cottage or second dwelling on an existing erf, bond-financed and built to plan — is small but pays retail prices and needs a municipal approval, which is exactly where Agrément certification and a tested assembly earn their keep.

The raw foam, before any coating, costs more than the finished imported product. A coating line buying local foam would need at least 40% off list before it could compete with a container from Ningbo — and it would still carry the certification cost and the lead time.

Where local manufacture does make sense: if you extrude the foam yourself; on thick boards, where freight per square metre climbs to about R35 at 30 mm against R23 at 20 mm; on long lengths, since local XPS comes in up to 8 m and an imported board is 2.4 m; and on service — small orders, next-week delivery, cut to size.

Verdict: import first. Do not build a plant until you are selling more than roughly 50,000 m² a year, or you own the foam supply.

09Risks

  • Fire class E. The core burns. Commercial and multi-storey work needs a non-combustible sister product.
  • Joints decide everything. The brand promise is "does not leak" — one bad detail ends it.
  • Habit, not the brick lobby. Owners, banks and insurers price a brick house higher; a board wall reads as temporary.
  • Subsidy work is closed without a schedule-of-rates entry and NHBRC acceptance.
  • Lead time and currency. Six to eight weeks from China, priced in dollars, sold in rand.
