Rowmat in South Africa: the case study

Rebuilt on the right material. Every number in this edition is priced off the imported board — R114–123/m² landed — not off Rowmat's Greek retail price of R541. Rowmat is the reference product and the proof that the material works; it is not the thing being sold. Against the import the wall comparison changes: the board is level with brick on a cheap external wall, cheaper on a wet wall, and within range on a partition.

On the "is it too niche" question. The material is not niche. Twenty-five applications below are real, and the demand arithmetic says the country could absorb millions of square metres a year. What is narrow is each gate: fire class, certification, and the habit of the trade. A business here is built by walking through one gate at a time, not by having a big total market.

The board wall lands at the bottom of the brick range, weighs about a tenth as much, goes up in days instead of weeks, and carries its own insulation. It also needs a structural design and a certificate before a municipality will approve it as an external wall — the gate in row 11 of the map.

Level. Board wins on thickness, weight and speed; brick wins at the bottom of its range and on what a South African buyer expects to see.

4.4 Wet area: the clearest win

Approach Rand per m² Time
Brick, plaster, liquid membrane, screed, then tile 260–380 plus trades 3–5 days of drying
Board, sealed joints, tile direct 315 Tiling the next day

A 20 m² bathroom saves three to five days of a crew at R1,500–R2,500 a day: R5,000–R12,000, against a board premium of nothing to about R1,100. This is the single best use of the material in the country.

4.5 Counters, vanities and joinery

A tiled or stone counter on board replaces shutterply or cement board, which swells, delaminates or cracks. Board costs R170/m² against R90–150 for shutterply, so the material is dearer — but it does not fail, it shapes, and it insulates. A premium joinery product, not a volume one.

One percent of that is 73,000 m² a year: R12.4m of revenue at R170, about R3.7m of gross margin at landed cost. Three percent supports a small factory. Demand is not the problem; distribution and certification are.

A sanity check on the two big assumptions: 260,000 bathroom renovations is 1.5% of roughly 17.5 million households, and 60,000 backyard rooms a year is modest against the 919,000 households already in backyard units. Both are estimates and should be replaced with a paid survey before capital is committed.

The core alone costs more than the finished import. A coating line buying local foam would need at least 40% off list before it could compete, and would still carry certification.

[!NOTE] Where local making does work Thick boards, since freight per square metre climbs from R23 at 20 mm to R35 at 30 mm; long lengths up to 8 m; cut-to-size and next-week service. Only past roughly 50,000 m² a year, or if you extrude your own foam.

8. Gates and risks

  • Fire class E closes partitions, multi-storey and most commercial compartments. Any line aimed there needs a non-combustible sister board.
  • No Agrément certificate, no external wall. European approvals do not transfer.
  • Waterproofing lives in the joints. One leaking shower at a reference site costs more than a year of marketing.
  • The trade is conservative and loyal to brick, plaster and Gyproc. Training wins the tiler, not price.
  • Currency and lead time: dollar cost, rand revenue, six to eight weeks on the water.
  • Thin files on demand. The segment volumes are estimates; a paid survey is the cheapest de-risking step available.
