The model is a typical pasta line — cycle times, breakdown rates and buffer sizes are placeholders chosen to be plausible, not measured from our plant. The engine is real: when you add a machine, the simulation runs a fresh server, the conveyor drains, and the red outline moves to the next constraint. Swap the numbers for measured ones and the same page becomes a real assessment.

Why 4,100 against a 2,400 commitment. The sizing rule asks the sealer for 0.7 of a machine. Nobody sells 0.7 of a sealer, so it gets one — and one sealer at fourteen seconds a case is 4,100 a day. The arithmetic wanted 2,800; the integer delivered 4,100 and it is still the constraint. That gap between the fraction and the whole machine is where most real plants actually live, and it is the reason a capacity number quoted from a spreadsheet and a capacity number measured on the floor rarely agree.

1What this software actually is

Three public repos, one man, and an unusually disciplined idea: a business process is a queueing system, so draw it, simulate it, and make it look like a photograph of a model.

Saifoulline ships three separate products, and they share almost no code:

air-sup lab

Machines you can take apart

Fusion reactor, Raptor 3, Cybertruck, humanoid — 90-odd machines. TypeScript, Vite, one page each, photoreal and cutaway.

machine-skill

One line in, a machine out

An open Claude Code skill: SKILL.md + a small runtime + a recorder. 378 stars, MIT.

factory-skill

The business floor

"A line that makes 2,000 e-bike motors a day" becomes a tilt-shift miniature with a real discrete event simulation under it. This is the one that maps a business.

The number that tells you everything

I cloned the lab and read its manifest. package.json has exactly one runtime dependency:

PackageVersionWhat it is
three^0.185.1The entire engine. That is the whole dependency list.
vite^8.2.2Dev server, dev-only
typescript^5.5.4Types, dev-only

No React. No @react-three/fiber. No drei. No postprocessing library. No GLTF models, no HDRI files, no texture downloads. Every machine, every material, every post effect is code.

Why that matters to us

This kind of page has no art pipeline to maintain and no asset drift. Change a cycle time and the geometry, the numbers and the HUD move together, because they all read the same spec object. That is why one person can ship a new "factory" almost every day — and why we could too.

The distribution trick

factory-skill builds the finished page into one HTML file of about 1 MB with three.js, the runtime and the spec inlined. It opens from disk with no server and no network. For a business audience that is the killer detail: a plant manager gets a single file, double-clicks it, and watches their own line run. Nothing to install, nothing to sign into, nothing that breaks when a CDN moves.

There is also a film mode: ?film=1 stops the clock, and a scripted cursor and camera step through numbered beats while a recorder screenshots each frame — with a magenta-frame scanner that refuses a bad capture. The LinkedIn clip and the page are the same artefact.

2The look — the exact recipe

It looks good because of seven choices, and six of them are numbers. Here they are, read straight out of their render code — the same chain is running in the canvas above.

The miniature is not a CAD view. It is a photograph of a model on a table, faked the way tilt-shift photographers fake it: high viewpoint, one narrow band of sharp focus, everything above and below melting into blur, saturated warm colour, direct sun. In order of impact:

#EffectTheir settingWhy it reads as a model
1Narrow focus band
two-pass CoC blur		 focusY 0.46 · band 0.07 · amount 20 px @1080p · power 1.25 The brain reads a thin depth of field as "small object, close lens". Gathers only from samples at least as blurred, so sharp edges don't bleed.
2One warm sun
long soft shadows		 #ffeed2 · intensity 4.0 · 4096² map · PCF radius 2.2 · bias −0.00025 Back-left, ~40°. Weak hemisphere fill so shadows stay readable rather than washing flat.
3Ambient occlusion GTAO radius 1.1 · 16 samples · blend 1.0 Contact shadow under every machine, cart and person. Without it, objects look pasted on the floor.
4Grade after tone map sat 1.32 · contrast 1.14 · warm (1.02,1.0,0.965) · vignette 0.38 · grain 0.018 Saturation is what separates a photo from a render. Grain and vignette are the camera, not the scene.
5Density free floor filled with pallets, carts, drums Empty floor reads as a render. Their world generator racks the back wall and litters every free square metre.
6Cut-away hall on a plinth back + left walls 6 m · front + right cut at 1.1 m · section face coloured You see inside without losing the room. A grass ring, a truck yard and a dark plinth under the diorama sell the table.
7Colour discipline cream machines · one accent per station · graphite conveyors · yellow zone tape The eye can track one accent colour per station, so the line reads as a sequence, not a pile.

Tone mapping is the quiet one

They light in HDR and tone map at the very end: a half-float render target with 4× MSAA, then ACES Filmic at exposure 0.98, then the grade. Bloom threshold sits at 2.6 — above the sunlit surfaces — so only genuinely emissive parts glow. That single number is why the page looks like daylight rather than a screensaver.

And the higher tier, for machines

The lab's own pipeline is hand-written and goes further: a 7-level bloom mip chain with a knee-based prefilter, GTAO sampled at half resolution from the resolved depth and blurred depth-aware, a 40-tap golden-angle depth of field that refuses to let a sharp foreground bleed onto the blurred background, chromatic aberration at the frame edge, an ACES fit in the final pass, and a live section cut — every part is a closed solid, and the shader draws the cut face from the back faces at the clipping plane, so the section can sweep instead of being pre-built.

Two tiers, one lesson: the expensive-looking parts are shader work, not model work.

"What to avoid: a top-down orthographic view (kills the miniature effect), bloom on sunlit surfaces, labels floating in 3D, and pure white materials (they clip under ACES)." — their own look.md

3The engine underneath

The pictures are the argument, but the simulation is the explanation. Every number on screen comes out of it. If the line misses its target, that is the finding — they never tune inputs to get a nicer answer.

The engine is about 530 lines of plain JavaScript and it is a classic discrete event simulation, not a game loop:

  • Event heap. A binary heap of timestamps. Nothing is integrated per frame; only real state changes are processed.
  • Servers and batches. Each station owns N identical machines. A machine takes a batch from every input buffer, works for a lognormal time with mean ct and coefficient of variation cv, then hands the units on.
  • Blocking after service. When the next buffer is full the machine holds its output and goes BLOCKED. Blocking chains upstream — this is the whole reason buffers matter.
  • Starving. An idle machine with nothing in its input is STARVED. Blocked and starved are different diagnoses and the HUD shows them in different colours.
  • Operation-dependent breakdowns. Time-to-failure counts busy time only; the interrupted cycle resumes where it stopped.
  • Scrap. Each finished unit is scrapped with probability 1 − yield at the station that caused it.
  • Determinism. A seeded mulberry32 generator drives every draw, so a spec and a seed give identical numbers in Node and in the browser. That is what makes a forecast quotable.

The sizing rule it prints on screen

Machines from demand, not from vibes

machines = CEIL( rate_in × cycle_time ÷ ( batch × availability × target_utilisation ) )

The page shows that formula with the actual numbers substituted, next to the machine count. A plant engineer can argue with the arithmetic instead of with the animation.

How it decides the bottleneck

Every machine's time in each state is accumulated exactly (not sampled), snapshotted every 5 production minutes, and the HUD reports a rolling 4-hour window. The bottleneck is the station with the highest busy + down share — the active period share. In capacity mode the machines before it read BLOCKED and the machines after it read STARVED, which is how you can tell a capacity constraint from a downtime constraint without touching a spreadsheet.

Where I departed from them, and why

Their bottleneck is the highest busy + down share, which is correct — with one artefact. The demo above releases raw material at capacity, so the first machine is fed for ever and reads ~99% busy even when the real constraint is three stations down the line. I rank stations by load on their own capacity instead: the rate the line actually achieves, divided by what that station could do on its own. It picks the same constraint when the head station is genuinely the constraint, and the right one when it isn't. Throughput is a rolling 15-minute measure rather than a 4-hour average, so a what-if answers in seconds rather than in an afternoon.

Where the sim stops — say it out loud

Their own reference is honest about the limits: one sink, a tree rather than a network, no rework loops, no changeovers between variants, no shared labour pool, no material-handling capacity (forklifts and AMRs are scenery), and a breakdown on a continuous machine stops one slot rather than the whole tunnel. For a first, legible model of a line that is the right set of cuts. For a plant-level capacity study you would graduate to AnyLogic or Simio — and you would still steal this presentation.

4Mapping a business: the part that isn't three.js

The reason this lands with a business audience is that the mapping is one-to-one. Every word a manager uses already has a parameter in the simulation.

A business process is a queueing system with money attached. You do not need a 3D model of the company to start — you need a station table. The translation is mechanical:

What the business saysWhat the model calls itWhere the number comes from
"the process steps"stations[]The value stream, in flow order. Six to ten steps, branches where sub-work is made in parallel.
"how long a step takes"ctTimestamps: ticket in → stage → stage → closed. Median, not average, and a spread.
"how many people / machines"serversDerived from the sizing rule, then checked against the real headcount.
"how much work sits in the queue"bufferActual WIP in the tracker on a normal day.
"it breaks / people are off"mtbf · mttrDowntime logs, leave, rework. Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR).
"we redo a lot of it"yieldRework and reject rates at that step. This is usually the biggest surprise.
"we need X a day"demand.perDayThe commitment, not the wish. Takt follows from it.
"where are we stuck?"bottleneckOutput of the simulation — never an input. If you write it down in advance, it isn't a study.

The data spine we already have

This is the part that makes it ours rather than a demo. A process-mining event log is three columns — case id, activity, timestamp. That is exactly what a Zoho Projects ticket already produces as it moves through a list: created, stage moved, commented, closed. Add Cliq activity and WorkDrive document events and each of the five companies already emits a case-level event log strong enough to measure station cycle times and real buffers.

So the build order is inverted from the usual: map first from data we have, then render. The 3D is the last step and the cheapest one — it is the part an LLM writes in an afternoon.

What a good first map looks like

One line, six stations, measured

  • The boundary named out loud ("cases out of the plant, from mixer to pallet")
  • Every cycle time carrying a note: where it came from, or "typical"
  • The sizing formula printed next to each machine count
  • One honest finding, e.g. "the sealer is fine; the packer's 15-minute repairs cost 190 cases a day"
What kills it

Three ways this goes wrong

  • Tuning inputs until the picture matches the hope — the model then proves nothing
  • Modelling the whole company at once: twenty stations and nobody looks
  • Pretty first, numbers later — then it is a video, and the first engineer who asks a question ends it

The craft rules worth stealing

  • Procedural over imported. No asset files means no drift, instant parameter changes, and a page that still works in three years.
  • One accent colour per station. The eye follows colour along the line; without it, machines merge into grey.
  • One label per station, on a post. No floating 3D text — it is the fastest way to make a scene look like a dashboard in space.
  • Density sells realism. Pallets, carts, drums, people. Half the perceived quality is clutter, not shaders.
  • Check it headless before you look at it. Their pipeline runs the sim in Node, prints per-station machines, capacity, busy, blocked, starved and failures, and only then builds the page. The numbers are reviewed like an engineer's, not a designer's.

6How we build ours

Not a proposal for a platform — one page, one line, measured, then repeated. Staged by dependency, so the first thing he can look at arrives before the hard part.

Stage 1 — visible fast

One floor, one company

Pick the line with the clearest start and end. Pull six to ten stations and their cycle times out of ticket timestamps. Ship one page in the shape of the demo above, with every number annotated by where it came from. This is the thing that decides whether the rest is worth building.

Stage 2 — the spine

Measured, not estimated

A nightly job turns Zoho Projects (plus Cliq and WorkDrive) into a case-level event log: case id, activity, timestamp. Stations then read their cycle times from data, and the map updates itself when the process changes.

Stage 3 — the fleet

One page per company, one kit

Shared machine kit, shared look, a switch at the top between the five floors. Each company gets its own bottleneck instead of a monthly argument about whose team is behind.

Stage 4 — what-if

Change it and see the forecast

Add a person, change a cycle time, watch the constraint move and the forecast recalculate from a fresh seeded run. A link per scenario, so a decision gets a URL instead of a slide.

The short version

A business is a queueing system. Draw it as stations, simulate it honestly, and photograph the result as a model on a table — and a manager finally sees the whole floor at once.

  1. The look is seven numbers, not a library: focus band, warm sun, AO, post-tone-map grade, density, cut-away plinth, one accent per station.
  2. The engine is ~530 lines of discrete event simulation with a printed sizing rule; the visuals read the simulation, never the other way round.
  3. The mapping is one-to-one: process step → station, cycle time → ct, WIP → buffer, downtime → MTBF/MTTR, rework → yield, and the bottleneck is an output.
  4. Our event log already exists in Zoho Projects. That is the difference between a demo and an assessment.
