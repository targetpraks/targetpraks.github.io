How this was scored

Every format is put through one formula, so the table compares businesses rather than brochures.

Take the ticket, multiply by a fair day's volume at a site that would actually take the machine, apply the gross margin, then subtract three real costs: the site's commission on turnover, card and cash handling at about 4%, and a maintenance provision. What is left is the net a month. Divide the machine's cost by it and you have the payback.

The second number matters more than the first. Break-even is how many units a day the machine needs just to stand still. A format that pays back in 20 months but needs 90% of its expected volume to survive is a worse bet than one that pays back in 24 and needs a third. That ratio is the fragility column, and it is the honest test of a vending site.

The caveat, stated plainly

These are modelled estimates, not supplier quotes. Machine prices move with the exchange rate, and site commissions are negotiated per landlord. Any one of these needs a written quote and a signed site before money moves. What the screen is good for is ruling things out cheaply.

What the screen changed

1. The business I missed is not a consumer machine at all

PPE and MRO vending — gloves, earplugs, grinding discs, safety glasses — dispensed on a factory floor or a mine site. The ticket is R180, not R18, the site pays by invoice, there is no cash to steal, no food to spoil, and the buyer is a safety manager with a budget and an audit. It repays in about eight months. Every consumer format on this list is fighting for a corridor; this one is sold, once, to one client.

2. The default vending business is the worst use of capital here

A snack and cold-drink combo costs about R120 000 and sells R18 items at roughly 30%. At 55 sales a day it takes 125 months to return the machine. It only works at a workplace doing 150+ a day, which is a different business from the one people start.

3. Cheap coffee beats good coffee

Instant coffee pays back in 13 months and bean-to-cup in 21, which is backwards until you notice that Bunacoffee and Dallmayr install bean-to-cup machines free on a pay-per-cup contract. You cannot sell a R15 flat white from a machine you had to buy when the office next door is paying nothing for its machine.

4. The protein machine holds up

Seventeen months here against 16.8 in the build model — the difference is only how maintenance is spread. It stays the cheapest credible consumer test on the list: R95 000, one gym, and a break-even of three shakes a day.
