01 — The boy, plainly

He is nine. He turns ten on 8 February 2027 — early in his Grade 4 year — and he starts at Reddam House Durbanville in the same class as children who are reading three, four and five years ahead of him. He is the older brother: Gianna is two years behind him, and there is a baby sister, Sofia, who arrived in September.

His thinking is not the problem. His verbal comprehension sits at an index of 112 — above average. Maths and Science are his subjects; he builds things, he asks why until he gets a real answer, and he can hold a complicated explanation if someone gives it to him in words rather than in text. What sits at the bottom of his profile is decoding: turning the marks on the page into words, quickly, without effort.

He knows. A nine-year-old who reads slowly in a class of fast readers can tell you exactly where he ranks, and he minds it. Anxiety is part of the picture and weekly play therapy is being arranged. When he is overwhelmed he goes quiet and withdraws rather than acting out — so a facilitator who reads silence as defiance will misread him every time.

Two things his parents and his reports say in the same words: he needs the reason for what he is asked to do, and he needs correcting in private. Both of those are instructions for how to work with him, not a personality review.

03 — Why Grade 4 is the year it matters

Up to the end of Grade 3, school teaches children how to read. From Grade 4 it assumes they can, and starts asking them to read in order to learn: the instruction on a maths worksheet, the comprehension passage in English, the question in Natural Science, the Afrikaans sentence he has to answer in Afrikaans.

So he is not behind in one subject. He is behind in the tool every other subject is taught with — which is exactly why the plan is not “extra lessons after school”. It is somebody in the room, every day, doing two jobs: getting the words into him properly, and making sure the words on the page never become the reason he cannot show what he understands.

05 — What helps him, and what backfires

Helps
  • Short blocks with more repetition than the average child needs. Twenty to thirty minutes, mastery before moving on — not a new sound every day.
  • Text he can actually decode. Decodable readers at his level for practice; the class text read aloud to him for content. Both, not one or the other.
  • Glasses on, near the board, less copying. His tracking is poor; the spectacles are not optional and the copying is.
  • Choice instead of command. “Do you want to start with the words or with the reading?” works where “sit down and do this now” does not. This is the demand-avoidance diagnosis doing its work, and it is the single most useful thing on this page.
  • Give him the reason. He will do hard things for a reason he accepts, and refuse easy things for a reason he does not.
  • Correct him privately, praise him specifically. “You heard that vowel the second time” is fuel. “Good boy” is noise.
  • Let him struggle — briefly, and bounded. Count to ten before you rescue him. A facilitator who quietly does the work produces a child who cannot.
  • A scribe, a keyboard or a reading pen for volume. His ideas are better than his handwriting; don't let the handwriting decide the mark.
Backfires
  • Reading aloud in front of the class. It buys nothing and costs him the rest of the day.
  • Silent independent reading of grade-level text as the main task. It looks like reading and is not.
  • Timed drills in front of peers, or anything that turns his speed into a public score.
  • Correcting his spelling in front of other children, or finishing his sentence for him.
  • Bare commands, ultimatums, and “because I said so”. With his diagnosis that is the reliable way to lose the lesson.
  • Reading his quietness as attitude. He withdraws; he does not act out. Ask, don't press.

08 — What good looks like at the end of term one

A proposal, for you and the school to agree or change before January — not a verdict handed down.

  1. A baseline you took yourself, in week one. Words per minute on a passage at his level, and the same passage again at the end of term. Two numbers from the same test, three months apart, is worth more than four numbers from four different tests.
  2. Forty sessions in the log, and the same passage faster. Four a week for ten weeks is what the arrangement actually promises; the words-per-minute number from (1) is what it delivered. Slower is a finding to report, not a failure to hide.
  3. He finishes the class task in most lessons, with you reading and scribing where it was needed. The measure is how often he produced the work, not how often you helped.
  4. He stops apologising for needing you. A child who says “I can't read” before he has tried is the thing this year is meant to change.
  5. One page a week, twelve pages by the review. If the term ends and nobody can say what changed, the plan failed regardless of how hard everyone worked.
