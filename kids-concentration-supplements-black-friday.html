First, the words

“Mushrooms, lime, mane” is almost certainly lion's mane — Hericium erinaceus, the white pom-pom mushroom sold as a capsule for mood and memory. If you did mean the citrus, there is nothing there: no trial of lime or citrus has ever tested attention in children.

And “prescribed” is worth separating out. None of these is a prescription medicine. They are supplements a pharmacist or a health shop can hand over without a script, which is exactly why the evidence bar matters more rather than less — nobody has signed a clinical judgement when you buy one.

Lion's mane is not junk science, and it deserves that said plainly. It has a real mechanism story: hericenones and erinacines raise nerve growth factor in cells and in animals. What it does not have is a single randomised trial in children.

Everything on one axis

The one thing with a real trial, and its catch

The one product family with paediatric trials behind the product, not just the ingredient, is Equazen — a fish-oil and evening-primrose blend in a 9:3:1 ratio. Two trials matter:

  • The Oxford-Durham study, 117 children aged 5–12, three months at six capsules a day: reading, spelling and behaviour improved against placebo; motor skills did not. That population had movement-coordination difficulties, not an attention diagnosis — which matters.
  • A 90-child, 12-month trial against methylphenidate: the inattention score came out similar across all three arms, and the drug-plus-oil arm did best on the total score.

So the honest sentence is: this is a modest adjunct with a plausible dose, not a substitute for an assessment. And the meta-analysis above says the benefit concentrates in children whose blood omega-3 is genuinely low — which nobody knows about Donatello or Jana until someone measures it.

The sale changes nothing about the answer. A discount on a product with no trials is still a product with no trials. R241.19 for thirty days of lion's mane is R241.19 for thirty days of nothing measurable, and a three-month run costs R723.57.

What to do instead, in order

  1. Book the blood test first. A ferritin and full blood count through your GP. Low iron is the one deficiency on this list that genuinely looks like poor concentration, and it is the only one you can rule in or out for a few hundred rand instead of guessing with a bottle.
  2. Fix the movement dose. The network meta-analysis puts the threshold for impulse control at about 340 MET-minutes a week — roughly fifteen minutes of brisk activity a day, or two proper sessions. Nothing on the shelf comes close to that effect size.
  3. Protect the sleep window. Same bedtime every night, screens face-down an hour before. Boring, free, and the largest lever on a school morning.
  4. Then, only if you still want a bottle: Equazen chewable, six capsules a day, twelve weeks, with a before-and-after rating of five school behaviours. Stop at twelve weeks if the ratings have not moved — do not drift into a second box because the first one was expensive.

If you want the trial anyway, the order line is: Equazen 180 chewable capsules at Dis-Chem, R589.99 — or the capsule version at R471.99 on the member price, if the children will swallow capsules. Do not buy the Fishies as a substitute: one 300 mg gummy a day is about 40% of the dose that was actually tested.

What I could not verify

  • Whether either child has been assessed for attention difficulty. That single fact outranks everything on this page: if not, a proper assessment is worth more than any supplement, and it is the question I would want answered before spending on a trial.
  • Any lab price for a ferritin test — I did not call a laboratory, so I am not quoting one.
  • Whether the 20% member price is the same campaign price as the Black Friday set or a standing member discount; both appear on the same product card.
