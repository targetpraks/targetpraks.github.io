01 Shark Bay: the answer

Both researchers went at it independently and reached the same place, with different evidence. Shark Bay is a lifestyle and land-banking purchase, not an income asset — and the estate is stricter than the brochures suggest.

The number that decides it Finished space in the same lagoon node sells at R12 641/m² at Blue Lagoon, R13 333/m² in Middedorp and R13 531–19 097/m² in Myburgh Park. Inside Shark Bay Estate, completed houses ask R28 500–38 300/m². You are paying roughly double per square metre for the address — and the estate forbids pools, requires design review, and fines you for not building within five years.
The scorecard splits Shark Bay in two Every plot on the estate came out between 2.38 and 2.93; the completed houses between 3.71 and 4.61. But the gate's highest score in the entire list of 107 — 6.89 — is also a Shark Bay product: The Shark Bay Villas, a two-bedroom hospitality unit at R2.2m on a Touch Down Group leaseback, advertised at about R230 000 a year in rental and a 10.5% return, with 28 days' own use. A R725 000 hotel-share in The Shark Bay Hotel (two shares, about R76 000 a year, 10.5% advertised) scored 3.48.

That split is the useful answer to your question. The land and the houses are a lifestyle purchase. The hospitality product is the only thing on that lagoon that behaves like an investment — and it is the same category as everything else in this document that promises a return: the operator publishes a return, not a guarantee, and the price may already contain it. Touch Down Group is described as the leaseback operator, not a verified guarantor; lease duration, minimum rent and vacancy protection are not published. The score reflects the advertised economics because those are the only figures that exist; diligence means proving them.

Plot and build does not rescue it — and now that can be priced. A Western Cape builder's August 2026 guide puts upmarket-estate construction at R18 000–26 000/m² excluding VAT, land and professional fees; a 2026 building-cost schedule gives exclusive private houses at R26 450/m². Put the R2.6m entry plot under the 249 m² house that is asking R7.7m, add 15% VAT, and the all-in lands between R7.75m and R10.05m before fees, contingency and holding costs — and that is on a 249 m² build. Build to the 270 m² of the larger completed house, or to the estate's 390 m² bulk ceiling, and the number climbs from there. Building reaches the finished asking price at the very bottom of the range and passes it above that. Buying finished is the cheaper way to own this house.

The holiday evidence points the same way. The Shark Bay Villas retail at R1 751 a night, sleeping four — a modest number that makes an advertised R230 000 a year look like a best case rather than a base case. And the levy has moved: a local neighbourhood guide quoted R1 500 a month in 2024 against R2 220 on today's adverts, a rise no HOA statement confirms but every owner will recognise.

The hospitality angle was the one genuinely new finding: a hotel-share in The Shark Bay Hotel at R725 000, and two-bedroom hospitality villas at R2.2m. Those are the only ways into this lagoon under R5m — and a hotel share is a different asset class from a house, with the same questions the aparthotel chapter raised: who operates it, what the split is, and what happens when the operator changes.

04 How to read a score

The rubric asked one question per property: is this fit for a Cape Town investor deploying the proceeds of a Johannesburg sale, weighing cash yield after all costs and tax, capital growth, protection if letting income disappoints, resale liquidity, and the levy and fee burden — against a 5.31% after-tax hurdle and 5.2% inflation.

Three limits are worth stating plainly. A score is not a valuation and it is not a forecast; it is the gate's judgement of fitness on the facts supplied. The facts are unequal — where a levy or rates figure was not published, the candidate was scored on incomplete information, and those candidates should be re-scored once the numbers are in hand. And the rubric weights yield first, which is the brief's bias: a plot that earns nothing will always score badly, even if it is the right long-term bet for a different investor.

One artefact to be aware of: the same physical property, advertised by three agencies with slightly different levy and rate figures, scored differently — the two Sotheby's listings of the same R7.7m Shark Bay house came out at 4.61 and 4.00, and a third mandate of it at 3.71. Where duplicates disagree, the arithmetic is being driven by the listing's own numbers, not by the building. Treat scores within half a point of each other as a tie.

What to do with the table Take the top of the list as your shortlist for diligence, not for purchase. Then apply the only test that has survived every report this week: does it clear 5.31% after tax on published numbers, and if not, is there a written reason to believe the capital will grow enough to pay for the shortfall? For Shark Bay the answer is no on both counts — it is a house at the lagoon, priced like a house at the lagoon.
