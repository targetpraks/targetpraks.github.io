Summary

Status — read this first

This is a specification. No ClickUp account, trial, Workspace or purchase exists. Nothing here is approved for implementation. Every price is a published list price read on 2 November 2026, and every platform limit is quoted from ClickUp's own documentation on the same date. Where the document says "we will", the correct reading is "we propose, and Ricardo decides".

What changed in v4.1

The build is now planned in eight stages, ordered by dependency and parallel inside each stage. The thirty-day pilot became a proof stage with exit conditions, and the twenty-one-day chat trial became three behaviour gates. Nothing in the design or the money moved.

What changed from v3.1

v3.1 compressed the plan into a decision memo. This edition does the opposite: it expands every Space into its full inventory, defines every field and every status transition, writes out all four dashboards card by card, maps every chat channel to the Cliq group it replaces, states the SLA clocks, sequences the migration, and gives the pilot a script. It also carries the configuration pack — fourteen CSV registers — so the build can be executed from data rather than from prose.

0. How to use this document

Three audiences read this differently.

Ricardo reads §1 (the decisions), §18 (the money) and §23 (what is still open). That is four pages. Everything else is delegation.

Whoever builds the Workspace reads §3 (the contract), §4 (Space by Space), §5 (fields), §6 (statuses), §7 (views) and works from the CSV pack in Appendix C. The pack and this document must not disagree; where they do, the pack wins because it is what gets imported.

Whoever runs it afterwards reads §8 (goals), §9 (meetings), §10 (dashboards), §17 (permissions) and §21 (governance). The build is a handful of stages; the operation is years.

Precedence. Where this document and any older edition disagree, this one governs. Where this document and a ClickUp platform limit disagree, the platform governs — limits are quoted with their source in Appendix D.

What "done" means for the build is defined in §21.5, not in this introduction. A Workspace that looks right and has nobody in it is not done.

0.1 How the work is staged

This plan is not written in days or weeks. It is written in stages, ordered by dependency, with everything inside a stage running in parallel.

Three rules follow from that, and they are load-bearing rather than decorative.

A stage ends on a condition, not a date. S1 is finished when the fields, statuses and Spaces exist and their tests pass — not when the calendar says so. A date that arrives with the work half-done is worse than no date, because it teaches people the plan is fiction.

Everything inside a stage runs at once. Fields, statuses, Spaces and invitations in S1 do not queue behind each other. The only ordering that exists is between stages, and it exists for one reason: something downstream cannot start until something upstream is true.

The fastest visible change goes first. S2 builds the goals dashboard before the bulk of the structure, because the people paying for this need to see the company on plan early. Visibility is a dependency in its own right.

Where an older edition of this document said "week one" or "day thirty", the stage map in §15.1 replaces it.

3. The Workspace contract

3.1 The hierarchy, stated once

Every Space below carries Folders for its sub-teams and Lists for its objects of work. Nothing deeper than List. A task's home is one List, permanently; it may appear in twenty views and that does not move it.

3.2 One Workspace, and why

ClickUp bills per Workspace, not per Seat spread across Workspaces. A person who needs to work in five brand Workspaces needs five paid seats. The arithmetic at forty people is in §18.3: one Workspace is roughly $8,160 a year, five brand Workspaces run $11,220 to $12,240, and if everyone needs every brand it is $40,800.

The decisive argument is not the money though. It is Ricardo's own requirement list. He asks for a company OKR dashboard and per-team dashboards. ClickUp's Goals and Dashboards live inside a Workspace. A dashboard cannot read goals from a different Workspace. So a five-Workspace design makes the single most senior requirement in the list — one place where the company's goals are visible — structurally impossible, and pays 37% more for the privilege.

Brands therefore become: a Brand field on every task, a Folder per brand inside Brand Operations, and a dashboard card that splits by brand. Anyone who wants "just Papa Pasta" filters. Nobody pays twice.

Inconsistent names are the tax you pay for eighteen months. These rules are short on purpose.

The number prefix on a Space is not decoration. It fixes the sidebar order, it stops two people creating Finance and Finance 2, and it makes the eleven Spaces addressable in conversation: "it's in 09" means one thing.

Four rules about fields, learned expensively elsewhere.

A dropdown is a decision, a text field is a hope. Anything that will be grouped, filtered or counted is a dropdown. Free text in a reportable column guarantees "Papa pasta", "Papa Pasta" and "PP" in the same chart.

Fields are inherited, not repeated. Set Brand and Function at the List level where a whole List belongs to one brand, and only override on the exceptions. Otherwise every task needs six clicks and nobody does six clicks.

Every field has an owner. A dropdown whose values nobody maintains becomes a museum within a year. The owner is named in the pack, and the review cadence is quarterly.

Read-only fields are the automation's contract. Response Due and Resolution Due are written by automation and read by people. If a person can edit them, the SLA is a fiction.

6. The status model

Statuses are where installs fail quietly. Two Spaces inventing their own vocabulary means no dashboard can ever count across them.

6.1 The universal set

Seven statuses cover roughly nine tenths of all work. Every Space that has no stated domain set uses exactly these.

Status Group Means Who moves it
Open Not started Created, not picked up Anyone assigned
In Progress Active Being worked now Assignee
Waiting Active Blocked on someone outside the task Assignee
Review Active Delivered, awaiting an approver Assignee
Blocked Active A named blocker exists; cannot proceed Assignee
Done Closed Accepted, with evidence attached Approver
Closed Closed No further action, archived by age Automation

6.2 Domain sets

Where a workflow has real stages, the Space gets its own vocabulary, and it is declared here so nobody invents a fourth.

Space Domain statuses Why it cannot use the universal set
02 Marketing Drafting, Internal Review, Client Review, Approved Two approval gates exist and they belong to different people
03 Finance Control Run, Exception, Approved A control run is a state, not an activity
08 Client Delivery Scoped, Delivering, Client Acceptance, Accepted Client Acceptance is a waiting state owned by a guest
09 Store Ops Requested, Scheduled, Completed A request is scheduled before it is worked
10 Goals On Track, At Risk, Off Track, Achieved A goal is never "in progress", it is on pace or not

6.3 The transition matrix

The rule is not "these transitions are legal"; it is "these transitions are the only ones that mean anything". Anything else is a person using a status to communicate instead of a comment, and it corrupts every count downstream.

From Legal next Illegal and why
Open In Progress, Blocked, Closed Waiting before starting is a lie about effort
In Progress Waiting, Review, Blocked, Done Back to Open means the work restarted; create a new task
Waiting In Progress, Blocked, Closed Straight to Done skips the verification
Review In Progress, Done Review never goes to Waiting; the reviewer is the assignee now
Blocked In Progress, Closed Blocked to Done skips the fix
Done Closed, In Progress Reopening is allowed, silently closing is not

6.4 Work in progress

Two limits, both enforced by a view rather than by permission, because ClickUp will not stop a person and a dashboard will embarrass them.

Limit Value Where it is counted
Tasks in Progress per person Five MANCO dashboard, "WIP over limit" card
Tasks in Review per approver Ten Team dashboards, oldest-first

The number five is a starting position, not a law. It is set low on purpose: the proof stage will show whether the real ceiling is four or eight, and the limit moves once, on evidence.

7. Views and the standard view set

8. Goals and OKRs

Ricardo's requirement: company goals and OKRs, broken into quarters, per team and per person. That is a four-level cascade, and it fails in a predictable way — each level invents its own goals, nobody connects them, and by March the dashboard shows forty unrelated numbers.

8.1 The cascade

Level Who sets it Count Rolls up to
Company Ricardo with Exco 4 to 5 annual, 3 to 4 quarterly Nothing; this is the top
Function Function lead, agreed with Ricardo 2 to 4 per quarter One or more company goals
Team Team lead 1 to 3 per quarter Exactly one function goal
Person The person, agreed with their lead 1 to 3 per quarter Exactly one team goal

Three rules make the cascade real rather than decorative.

A goal above must have a number below it. If a company goal has no function goal carrying a number toward it, the company goal is a slogan.

A person carries three, never nine. Three personal targets is a focus; nine is a wish list with better formatting.

Every goal has one accountable name. Not a team. A person. Goals assigned to a department are goals nobody checks.

8.2 What the goals are

The specific targets are Ricardo's to set, and this document deliberately does not invent numbers that will be mistaken for decisions. What it specifies is the shape, the measurement and the cadence — which is the part that is hard.

Function The function's contribution to a company goal Number or percentage "Finance closes within five working days, four quarters running"
Team A delivery commitment Count or percentage "Design ships 90% of requests within five days"
Person A capability or a number they control Either, but stated precisely "Cut my exception count to under ten a month"

8.3 Target arithmetic

ClickUp Goals carry targets and can compute progress from tasks, from money, or manually. Three measurement modes, and each has a right and a wrong use.

Mode Use when Do not use when
Tasks The goal is "close these things" and the tasks exist The goal is a financial number; a task count lies about revenue
Currency The goal is money and the values are in task fields Values live in a spreadsheet nobody maintains
Manual The number comes from outside the system Someone is expected to remember to update it weekly

Manual goals always decay. If a goal is manual, the weekly check-in task must carry a required field for the current value, or the number is stale within a month.

9. Meetings and minutes

9.1 The structure Ricardo asked for

The Monday morning meeting, per team, recorded across years, with notes and documents attached. The question he asked was whether this needs its own folder or Space.

9.2 Why it is a Space and not a Folder

Four reasons, in order of weight.

Privacy. Minutes contain personnel matters, commercial terms and decisions taken about named people. If they live in a Folder inside a function Space, everyone in that function reads them by inheritance. A separate Space is the only way to hold a record that is sensitive and durable.

Ownership. A Folder inside Finance belongs to Finance, and when the finance lead changes, the archive changes hands. A Meetings Space belongs to the company, with one custodian, and survives every personnel change.

Cross-team reading. Exco minutes reference every function. Filing them under Finance means a marketing lead has to be given Finance Space access to read a decision about marketing.

Scale. One Doc per team per year, times eleven teams, times five years, is fifty-five documents. That is a Space, not a Folder somebody remembers to maintain.

9.3 The Monday meeting, timed

Ninety minutes, per team, same shape every week. The shape matters more than the content: a meeting with a fixed shape can be prepared for in ten minutes, and one without cannot be prepared for at all.

5–15 Last week's actions Every action from last week, by owner: done, not done, dead Status per action
15–40 This week's commitments Each person says what they will finish, in one sentence each Commitments, as actions
40–60 Blockers Only blockers needing someone else in the room Blocker, holder, and the ask
60–75 Decisions Anything needing a decision today Decision, alternatives rejected, owner
75–85 Risks and escalations What is going to bite in the next month Risk, rating, review date

Rule Detail

11. Chat — replacing Cliq

Ricardo wants chat spaces that are functionally better than Cliq and can replace it. Two facts decide the design, and both are uncomfortable.

There is no Cliq importer. ClickUp's importer list covers Slack among others; Cliq is not on it. History does not travel. Every migration of this kind therefore starts with a decision about what to do with the old history — and the answer is to keep Cliq read-only for a year rather than pretend the archives moved.

History is not the problem; control is. Message history is included from Core upwards, so the archive survives on Business. What Business does not have is an audit trail and the ability to restrict who may invite a guest — both are Enterprise and Max rows on the comparison table. So chat on Business is usable and ungoverned, and the governance has to be a written rule with a monthly review (§17.3) rather than a setting.

11.1 Channels, mapped

Nine channels replace the Cliq groups that exist today. The mapping is deliberately one-to-one at the start, because a new tool should not simultaneously change who talks to whom.

11.2 The three rules that make chat work

A decision in chat is not a decision. If a channel produces a decision, someone writes it in the minute or the Decision Register the same day. The channel is where it is discussed; it is never where it lives.

Chat does not carry work. A message that asks for work becomes a task at the moment it is asked. The test is simple: if nobody created a task, nobody has been asked to do anything.

One channel per audience, not per topic. Topic channels multiply and die; audience channels survive because the audience does not change.

11.3 The three gates

Chat is the part of ClickUp most likely to be abandoned, because it is the part people did not ask for. So it is proved in three gates, and each gate is a condition that is either true or false. None of them is a duration.

Gate What must be true Evidence If it fails
G1 — Adoption The cohort's two channels carry its internal conversation for one complete working cycle, weekend included Message counts per channel for that cycle, compared with the same cycle in Cliq Chat stays a nice-to-have and Cliq remains the conversation tool
G2 — Traceability Every decision taken in chat during that cycle reached a minute or the Decision Register Count of decisions in chat against decisions written down The gate fails, and minute discipline is fixed before G3 is attempted
G3 — Client One engagement runs in its guest channel, and the client asks for something there without sending an email Observed once, and written down Client chat is dropped; guests stay on lists and reports

All three gates hold and Cliq is retired at the end of the year that follows. Any gate fails and that part of chat is simply not adopted: Cliq stays for conversation, ClickUp carries work, and the two are joined by links. That is a legitimate outcome, and it is cheaper than a tool nobody opens.

18.3 The four gaps on Business, and what each one costs us

These are not reasons to buy Enterprise. They are reasons to design around four things, and to put each one in a person's hands rather than a setting's.

Gap What it means The manual control that replaces it
No Team-level sharing A List cannot be shared with a Team; it is shared with people or a Space Share at Space level, and accept that a List has a wider audience than its owners

Guest invites cannot be restricted Any member can invite a guest A written rule plus the §17.3 checks; guests are reviewed monthly because the tool will not do it

18.5 The conditions that would force Enterprise

Enterprise is not a bigger version of the same thing; it is bought for control features. It becomes the answer only if one of these is true, and each one is observable.

Condition How you would know What to try first
Team-level sharing becomes necessary People cannot share a List with a group and start sharing task by task Share the Space instead; it costs a wider audience, not a contract
An audit trail becomes a requirement A client, an auditor or a funder asks who changed a record Export and version the pack; the register is the record
SSO is mandated Security policy requires SAML or Okta Google SSO is on Business and may satisfy the policy
Guest control becomes a real exposure A guest was invited by someone who should not have had the right Written rule, monthly review, and one owner for guest access

Recommendation: Business, and stay there until one of those four fires. At this company size the manual controls above cost a few hours a month; Enterprise costs a negotiation and a contract.
