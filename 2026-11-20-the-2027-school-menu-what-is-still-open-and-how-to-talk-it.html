Grade 4 · 12 min · R91 800 a year, plus R5 700 once-off

The thing not to say to either child. All three live options are schools Donatello would be happy at. The difference between them is not one a nine-year-old can weigh — it is a support model, a year of his life and R35 000 a year. 05 How to have the conversation

Three conversations, not one. Ask what he noticed, not what he prefers — a child answers the question you ask, and "which school do you want" invites him to solve an adult problem.

With Donatello — the schools

1. Open with the visits, not the decision

"You've been to two schools now. What did you notice about the classrooms?"

Listen for: whether he mentions the number of children, the noise, or a teacher. Those are the things that will actually decide his year.

2. Ask about the work, in his words

"When the reading is hard, what helps? Someone sitting with you, or a smaller group?"

Listen for: whether he can name a mechanism. If he says "nothing helps", that is information about the support, not about him.

3. Name the Grade 3 question out loud, once

"One of the schools would have you do Grade 3 again, in a class of nine. I don't want to hide that from you. What do you think about it?"

Listen for: shame, relief, or a shrug. A boy who is relieved is telling you the pace is the problem; a boy who is ashamed is telling you the label will cost more than the year buys.

4. Give him the real choice, which is sport

"Cricket in the summer and hockey in the winter, or swimming instead — which sounds better?"

Listen for: the team he wants to belong to. That is a decision he is competent to make, and it is the one that keeps him going to school.

5. Close with what does not change

"Whatever we choose, the reading help stays. That's not going away."

Listen for: nothing. This one is not a question. He needs to hear that the support is a fixture, not a punishment attached to a school.

With Gianna — her week, not her school

She has a confirmed place and a squad place. The honest conversation is about the four afternoons, and she can genuinely choose inside them.

1.

"You've got swimming on a Wednesday next year. Do you want dance on Tuesday and Thursday as well, or just one of them?"

Listen for: whether she wants the stage or the water. Both is fine; three is too many for a seven-year-old.

2.

"Piano is in school time, so it doesn't take an afternoon. Do you want to keep it?"

Listen for: a real no. If she is done with piano, that is R2 325 a term and two hours of her week back.

3.

"If you want to play matches in winter — hockey or netball — that's a different sign-up. Do you want to?"

Listen for: whether swimming is enough competition for her. If it is not, the winter matches are the cheap way to add a team without adding a practice.

What not to do. Do not tell either child the decision is theirs. Donatello will read a "you choose" as "you are responsible for what happens next year", which is the one thing a nine-year-old should not carry through a support year. Take their answers, say thank you, and decide.
