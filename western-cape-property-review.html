01 What we set out to do

We are selling the Johannesburg properties. The question is what to do with that money: leave it in a bond, or put it into property in the Western Cape that we could use as a family and that would also hold its value.

For that question to have an honest answer, every property has to be measured the same way. So each one was priced on four things: what it costs, what the monthly building levy and municipal rates are, what it would actually rent for, and — the part agents never mention — what is left after tax.

Everything is compared against one number. Money in a five-year bond earns about 9% before tax. At our tax rate that is 5.31% after tax, with no tenant, no levy and no phone calls. That is the line a property has to beat to be worth the trouble.

Why 5.31% and not the rental yield on the brochure A brochure quotes gross rent as a percentage of price. It ignores the levy, the rates, the managing agent, the months the flat sits empty, and the tax on the profit. By the time all five are taken out, a 9% "yield" is usually 5% or less. Every figure in this document is the after-everything number.

06 Hotel apartments: days and facilities

This is the part that changes the maths. A hotel-managed apartment — an aparthotel, a condo-hotel or a hotel residence — is not bought for the rent. It is bought because the contract gives us the right to stay in our own unit for a set number of nights a year, usually with the hotel's pool, gym, spa and housekeeping.

The Colosseum, worked through R1 040 000 for a suite in Century City, bought for cash because no bank will bond a unit inside a hotel rental pool. Rent after costs and tax: 3.78%, which is below the bank. But owners get 28 nights a year at no charge, splittable any way we like, and exchangeable at RCI resorts worldwide. At the hotel's own published rate of R1 280 a night, those nights are worth R35 840 a year — 3.40% of the price, in money we would otherwise spend. Total: 7.2%.

Two cautions. The nights only count if we would genuinely use them; if we would not, the Colosseum is simply a rental that returns less than the bank. And because it must be bought for cash, our eventual buyer must also pay cash, which makes it slower to sell than an ordinary flat.

07 What it costs to own

The levy is the number that surprises people. It is a monthly charge to the building for security, cleaning, maintenance, insurance and management — and it runs whether the flat is let or empty.

Taj Cape Town — R11 612 a month on R6m 2.3% of price a year
Pepperclub — R10 579 a month on R4.75m 2.7% a year
Harbour Bridge — R7 055 a month on R6.495m 1.3% a year
Hermanus Beach Club — R3 226 a month on R1.35m 2.9% a year
Colosseum — R1 188 a month on R1.04m 1.4% a year

Buying costs

On top of the price: transfer duty on anything above R1.1m, conveyancing at roughly 1.5%, and if we bond it, another 2% or so for bond registration. New-build units bought from a VAT vendor carry no transfer duty, which is why the off-plan options look cheaper on the day than a resale at the same price.

Tax

Rental profit is taxed at our marginal rate — 41% at the top. Losses on rental cannot simply be set against salary; they are ring-fenced and carried forward. When we sell, the gain is taxed at an effective 16.4% for a person, or 21.6% inside a company before any tax on getting the money out. The tax-free primary residence exclusion does not apply to an investment property.

Cash-only, and what it really means

A unit inside a mandatory hotel rental pool generally cannot be bonded. For us that is fine — we are paying cash — and it removes the bond cost and the finance condition that loses a negotiation. The cost arrives at the other end: our buyer must also be a cash buyer, so the pool of people who can buy it is smaller and a sale takes longer.

08 What can go wrong

RiskWhat it means in practice
The levy keeps runningAt 2.7% of price a year, a vacant year on a R4.75m flat costs R127 000 before anything else. Levies also rise above inflation more often than not.
Guarantees expireMost rental guarantees run twelve months from transfer. After that the income is a projection, not a promise.
Cash-only exitThe Colosseum and similar pool units need a cash buyer at resale, which narrows the market and can cost us on price if we need to sell quickly.
Body corporate healthIf a building has no reserve fund or owners in arrears, the next buyer's bank refuses the bond — which blocks our exit, not just theirs.
Short-let rulesCape Town treats dedicated short-lets as commercial accommodation, and a registration by-law is in the pipeline. A building can also ban short-lets on its own.
Estate rulesShark Bay carries a 60-month build deadline, approved-builder and design review, penalty levies and a 1% resale levy. It is a managed estate, not a free hand.
Bad or stale listingsOne flat advertises municipal rates of R90 a month. Another plot's price had not moved since April 2025. Both are flagged rather than smoothed over.

09 What we would do

The recommendation Buy the Colosseum at R1 040 000 for cash. It is the only thing above our price floor that returns more than the bank once the free nights are counted, and it gives us a Cape Town base with 28 nights a year. Keep the rest in the bank until something better appears.

Why not the alternatives:

  • Delaporte at R2 395 000 returns 5.31% after tax — exactly the bank — but on a twelve-month guarantee, before a single year of operating history, with the furniture pack still unpriced. Matching the bank with extra risk is not a good trade.
  • Hermanus Beach Club at R1 350 000 returns 4.37% and would give us a beach flat the family uses. That is a fair trade, not a clever one. Worth doing only if the family use is the point.
  • Parow and Langebaan flats under R1m genuinely beat the bank — 6.95% and 5.68% after tax on cash. Our R1m floor is what excludes them, and it costs us roughly 1.5% a year to keep.

10 What Julie needs to decide

  1. Do we want the Colosseum? R1 040 000 in cash, 28 nights a year in Century City, exchangeable at RCI resorts. If we would not use the nights, the answer is no — it is then a below-bank rental.
  2. Do we want a beach flat as a family thing? Hermanus at R1 350 000 is the best of them. It returns about what the bank does and we get the use of it. If the answer is yes, we buy it for the use, not the return.
  3. Is the R1 million floor still right? The two properties that beat the bank were R799 000 and R899 000. Dropping the floor buys us about 1.5% a year and two small flats in Parow and Langebaan instead of one hotel suite.
And one thing to be clear about None of this makes money quickly. The best case here matches the bank or beats it modestly, with the use of a property thrown in. Property in this market is not the bargain it is usually described as, and anyone telling us otherwise is quoting gross rent.

11 How this was built

Seven areas were searched across live listings and developer pages, and each property was captured with its price, size, levy, rates and, where published, the rent it achieves. Forty-six could be priced; the rest could not, and are shown as unpriced rather than estimated. Levies and rents were read from the listing or the operator's own page on 23 November 2026, and every figure in the tables links to its source.

What we could not verify: the actual short-let income achieved by any specific coastal unit (only area averages are published); the body corporate financials of any building, which only arrive at due diligence; and any hotel unit's launch price against its current resale price, because no such comparison is published.

Full data, listings and workings are held in the research folder alongside this document.
