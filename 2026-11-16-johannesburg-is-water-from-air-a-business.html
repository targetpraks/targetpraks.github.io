Johannesburg: the demand is real, the machine is the wrong answer

You are right that households are paying for water they cannot get from the tap. But the arithmetic of a water-from-air unit in Joburg winter kills the utility model before it starts: the electricity to make a family’s daily water costs 8.7× what the same family pays Checkers for it.

Electricity at City Power Prepaid High Block 3, R4.37/kWh incl. VAT. Climate from Open-Meteo ERA5 hourly data for Johannesburg, 2025. Prices read 16 Nov 2026.

Verdict. As a water utility selling litres to households — not viable. The marginal cost is R4.68/L annual average and R8.74/L in August, against refill water at R1/L and tanker delivery at R0.53/L.

As a hardware business selling water security to the top decile of Joburg households — viable, thin, and support-heavy. One configuration genuinely works: a unit bolted onto a house that already has 5–6 kWp of solar, where the marginal electricity is free and the cost collapses to about R1.20/L.

1 What Joburg households are actually facing

This is not a perception problem. The system is losing the water before it reaches anyone.

  • Roughly 47% of water is lost nationally, about 48% in Johannesburg, and the city lost about R10.3 billion of drinking water over four years. Johannesburg Water’s infrastructure backlog is put at R27 billion.
  • Quality failures are killing people. In Tshwane more than 800 people fell ill after contaminated water, and a 2023 government report found 46% of supply systems nationally had poor or bad microbiological quality, up from 5% in 2014.
  • Outages now run days, not hours. In mid-November 2026 an unplanned outage hit 19 Johannesburg areas when Eikenhof’s bulk reserves had to be closed urgently; protests followed in October.
  • Wealthy households are already going off-grid — Reuters reported in August 2026 that the crisis is driving the rich off the municipal system. That is your market, and it is the top slice only.

2 Joburg is the worst climate in South Africa for this machine

The Highveld is dry in exactly the months when you need stored water least and drink hot drinks most. August air carries 4.5 g of water per cubic metre — 19% of the 30 °C/80% rating condition (24.2 g). You get a fifth of the advertised output for the same electricity bill.

3 The unit economics of one household

Take a family of four needing 15 L/day for drinking and cooking — 450 L a month — and an 80 L-class unit at R24,950 plus installation. It draws 1,250 W, which is 30 kWh a day when working, whatever the weather delivers.

That is the whole argument in one line: in August, the electricity alone to make one family’s 450 litres costs R3,933. The same 450 litres at Checkers costs R450, and a delivered 5,000 L tanker load costs R2,650 — eight times the water for two-thirds of the money.

The machine cannot pay for itself against R1/L water. Ever. There is no volume, no scale and no tariff at which it does, because the compressor’s energy per litre rises exactly when output falls.

4 Four business models, scored

A. Sell the water by subscription — dead

You own the machine, the household pays a monthly fee, you pay the electricity. At R1,200/month for 450 L the customer is paying R2.67/L and you are spending R3,933 on power. You lose R2,733 a month per household before a single service call. Raise the fee to R1,800 and you still lose R2,133 while pricing yourself at 4× Checkers. There is no fee that both covers the electricity and undercuts the refill shop.

B. Sell the hardware and service it — viable, thin

A 100 L/day Chinese unit is $1,799–1,999 FOB (FND is one of several). Landed with freight, duty and 15% VAT at about R43,500; sold at R58,000 that is R14,500 gross, 25%. You are then the warranty, the filters and the call-outs for a customer who has just spent R58k and expects water. Two units a month is a living; it is not a scalable water business.

C. The solar-coupled unit — the only version that wins on price

If the house already has 5–6 kWp of PV and a battery — increasingly common in Joburg after load-shedding — the marginal cost of the 30 kWh is the foregone feed-in, roughly R0.60/kWh. That makes water at about R1.20/L: competitive with Checkers, and free of both the tap and the queue. This is a real product for estates and premium suburbs, and it is the only configuration where the household’s total cost of ownership beats what they pay today.

D. Don’t sell water from air — sell water security — the actual market

The thing Joburg households are buying is not a litre price, it is guaranteed litres during an outage. A 2,500 L tank, pump, filtration and UV install runs R20,000–R40,000 complete, holds 2,500 litres against the 26.5 litres a T-12 holds, and can be refilled by tanker at R0.53/L. Gross margin on the install is 30–40%, the support burden is a plumber rather than a refrigeration engineer, and it works in August.

5 What the market is worth, honestly

City of Johannesburg has 1.84 million households. The slice that can spend R25,000–R90,000 on water security is the top 10–15% — roughly 200,000–275,000 households. At 1% penetration that is 2,000–2,700 units. At R14,500 gross a unit that is R29–39 million of cumulative gross margin, spread over years, against a support base that grows with every unit sold. It is a good small business. It is not a utility.

And you would be selling into a market where the alternative is R15,999 for a 20 L unit or a tank install that a plumber can do on Monday. The moat is service, not the machine.

6 If you want into this market, this is the shape I would build

1 Lead with water security, not water from air. Tank + pump + filtration + UV, installed, R20–40k cost, R32–55k sell. It solves the outage, which is the actual pain, and it works in a dry August.

2 Offer the AWG as the premium upgrade for solar homes only. Qualify on PV size first — under 5 kWp, refuse the sale. R25k–R58k a unit at 25% gross, with a service contract at R250/month that is where the recurring revenue actually lives.

3 Never sell a litre price you cannot hold. The moment a customer compares you to Checkers at R1/L you lose — so sell availability, storage and independence, and be honest that in June the machine makes a fifth of what the brochure says.

4 The refill lane is the real volume business, and it is already taken at R1/L. Competing with Shoprite on price is a losing game; supplying refill stations, estates and offices with equipment and service is not.

7 Kill criteria — when to walk away

  • If City Power’s tariff keeps rising above inflation, the AWG case dies faster than it is already dying: every 50c/kWh adds R1.30 to the cost of a litre in August.
  • If the customer does not already have solar, the household economics do not clear — walk away from the sale.
  • If a Checkers or Spar refill station is within 2 km of the customer, the convenience premium is gone.
  • If you cannot carry a 25% hardware margin and a R250/month service book, this is a hobby, not a business.
