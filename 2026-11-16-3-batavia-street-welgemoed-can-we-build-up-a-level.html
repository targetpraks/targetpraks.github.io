Can we build up a level at 3 Batavia Street, Welgemoed?

Erf 465, Bellville (Welgemoed), 7530 · owner R M Nova Prop (Pty) Ltd · checked 16 November 2026 against the City's live zoning records and the Municipal Planning By-law, 2015

The answer

Yes — the zoning allows a second storey. As of right.

Erf 465 is Single Residential 1 (Conventional Housing). On a 2,050 m² erf the City's rules allow a wallplate up to 9 m and a roof up to 11 m. A second storey lands the house at roughly 6–6.5 m, so about 4.5 m of height sits unused. Floor space headroom is just as open: the house uses about 580 m² of the 1,500 m² permitted.

So this is not a land-use question. No planning application or departure is needed if the design stays inside that envelope — only an approved building plan.

One document can still stop it: the title deed. If it carries a "single dwelling" or "single storey" condition, that condition has to be relaxed first. That is the only open item, and it takes one email to close.

The one open item — the title deed

Old Cape townships often carry conditions written into the deed at township establishment: one dwelling only, no trade, sometimes a height or "single storey" limit. Where one exists, the City can relax it, but that is a separate application with its own clock — so it is worth knowing now, not at plan-submission stage.

Three ways to get it, cheapest first:

  1. Your transferring attorney. Bill Tolken Hendrikse Inc held the March 2024 transfer (Belinda, belinda@billtolken.co.za). They have the deed and the surveyor-general's diagram on file — a same-day email.
  2. The bond holder. If the property is bonded, the bank holds a copy of the deed.
  3. The Deeds Office. A deeds search at Cape Town (or any attorney/conveyancer with a deeds account) pulls T15018/2024 for a small fee.

The building plan application needs the deed anyway — it is item one on the City's own document list — so this is not extra work, it is work moved forward.

The build path, in order

  1. Read the title deed. No restrictive condition → go straight to design. A condition → add a relaxation application before anything else.
  2. Pull the previously approved plans from the City. They settle three things at once: the real storey count today, the build date (heritage question below), and where the walls sit relative to the boundaries. The City advises applicants to do this before preparing a submission (submit a building plan).
  3. Structural engineer. The existing footings and walls were designed for one level. A second storey over a 1960s–80s house means new load paths, possibly new columns down to foundation. This is the real cost driver, not the brickwork.
  4. Architect and submission. Plans go in online through the City's e-Services / DAMS portal — the City accepts no paper. A SACAP-registered architect is required for anything beyond minor works. Because the owner is a company, add the company resolution.
  5. Approval, then build, then occupation certificate. The City inspects at trench, at sewer, and at completion; the new space may not be occupied until the occupancy certificate is issued.

Trap one · the 4 m boundary cap

Any part of a building within 3 m of a common (side) boundary may not exceed 4 m to the top of the roof, whatever the erf size. If the existing house already sits inside 3 m of a neighbour's line, going up over that strip needs a departure — and that is the one place a planning application could appear.

The common boundary building line here is 6 m, so a compliant house is nowhere near it. The approved plans will confirm.

Trap two · the 60-year heritage rule

The house was standing by 1984 (it sold that year for R29,000). If it was built before 1966 it is 60 years old or more, and alterations then need a heritage comment from Heritage Western Cape before the City can approve.

The City's heritage inventory has no record for the erf, which is a good sign, but the build date on the approved plans is the evidence that counts.

Timing, and the tenant

The house is let until 30 June 2027. A second storey is a vacant-possession job — scaffolding, a stripped roof, water off, dust. That means either waiting for the lease to end, or giving the tenant proper notice and losing the rent for the build. It does not change whether we can build; it decides when.

What it costs, and what it returns

The money side is already modelled — the November 2026 research note Renovation vs Rhodes vs currency puts the R3.0m double storey at about 125–156 m² at R16–20k/m² plus 20% for fees and contingency, recovering roughly 60% of its cost on resale. At a R38,000/month Welgemoed rental, a R3m bond at prime does not clear its own debt.

So the building permission is not the constraint — the return is. This is a live-in-and-keep decision, not a build-to-sell one. The zoning work above removes the "can we" question; the R3m question stays open on its own merits.

Current market view from the December 2025 Brickfield comparative analysis: R6.85m – R8.89m, against R5.5m paid in January 2024.
